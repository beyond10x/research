## Question

When a coding agent's patch passes the functional tests of a repair task, how often does it still
violate an acceptance constraint that a maintainer raised in code review, and does stating the
constraint to the agent change that (Abstract, Introduction)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · functional-pass-hides-review-constraint-violations · E2
- practice: observed: patches that pass the instance's functional test and fail its review-constraint test ("hidden failures"), counted per model with the constraint description given to the agent (+C) (RQ1, Tables 2 and 3).
- claim: "functional success does not entail review constraint compliance"; functional-only evaluation "leaves a substantial fraction of constraint-violating repairs undetected" (RQ1).
- evidence: 644 functional passes over four models, 221 fail the constraint, 34.3% (Table 3). Per model: GPT-5.5 67 of 227 (29.5%), GPT-5.4-mini 67 of 187 (35.8%), DeepSeek-V4-Flash 72 of 202 (35.6%), GPT-4o-mini 15 of 28 (53.6%) (Table 3). Functional success rate 74.9, 61.7, 66.7 and 9.2%; joint success rate 52.8, 39.6, 42.9 and 4.3% (Table 2).
- effect: hurts
- reader: counts of a measured gap, nothing to compare against, so E2. Whether a maintainer would reject the hidden failures is not tested: the constraint tests were written by an LLM agent and checked by an LLM and by the authors (§Quality Assurance), so "fails the constraint" means fails the benchmark's own test. The direction is the paper's (functional-only scoring overstates success), not a result of a treatment.

### F2 · practice · review-constraint-stated-in-task · E4
- practice: give the agent the natural-language review constraint next to the issue (+C), against the issue alone (−C); same 303 instances, framework, 100-step budget and hidden tests; the measurements are constraint following rate (CFR, share of functional passes that also pass the constraint test) and joint success rate (JSR) (§Experimental Setup, RQ2).
- claim: "Explicit constraint descriptions improve the observed joint success rate for all evaluated models and substantially increase the fraction of functional repairs that also satisfy constraint validation" (RQ2).
- evidence: Table 4, −C to +C. JSR: GPT-5.5 41.3 to 52.8 (+11.5), GPT-5.4-mini 36.3 to 39.6 (+3.3), DeepSeek-V4-Flash 38.0 to 42.9 (+4.9), GPT-4o-mini 3.3 to 4.3 (+1.0). CFR: 54.6 to 70.5, 50.7 to 64.2, 54.2 to 64.4, 20.8 to 46.4 (+10.2 to +25.6). Joint successes over the four models 360 to 423 (RQ2).
- effect: helps
- reader: an input ablation on equal instances, so E4. One generation per model, instance and condition and no significance test, so a +1.0 point change (GPT-4o-mini, about 3 of 303 instances, reader's arithmetic) is within what a rerun could move. Even with the constraint stated, 35% to 54% of functional passes still fail it (F1). Authors evaluating their own benchmark: `independent: false`.

### F3 · practice · review-constraint-stated-in-task · E4
- practice: as F2; the measurement is the functional success rate (FSR) on the issue's functional test (RQ2).
- claim: FSR "does not improve under the Constraint-Provided condition"; the authors read it as "an observed trade-off ... rather than ... a general causal claim" (RQ2).
- evidence: Table 4, FSR −C to +C: GPT-5.5 75.6 to 74.9 (−0.7), GPT-5.4-mini 71.6 to 61.7 (−9.9), DeepSeek-V4-Flash 70.0 to 66.7 (−3.3), GPT-4o-mini 15.8 to 9.2 (−6.6).
- effect: hurts
- reader: same ablation as F2, E4 on the direction. All four changes are negative, the sizes range from 0.7 to 9.9 points, and there is no test. The explanation offered ("satisfying an additional requirement increases the complexity of the repair") is a guess; the paper does not look at the patches or trajectories to check it.

### F4 · observation · review-constraint-category-difficulty · E2
- practice: observed: CFR and JSR per review-constraint category under +C; categories are multi-label (Table 5, RQ3).
- claim: "Constraint-following difficulty is not uniform across engineering requirements" (RQ3).
- evidence: Table 5, CFR for the three stronger models. Scope generalization (62 instances) 63.0, 46.3, 53.5%; lifecycle cleanup and resource (19) 62.5, 53.8, 57.1%; encoding, escaping, quoting (74) 68.3, 51.1, 55.9%; missing-versus-empty or sentinel distinction (51) 81.6, 74.2, 75.9%; ordering and argument preservation (86) 76.9, 75.0, 79.6%. Largest categories: error semantics 152 instances (50.2%), schema, metadata, typing 143 (47.2%) (§Dataset Characteristics).
- effect: mixed
- reader: descriptive counts with no comparison group, E2; the authors say "all comparisons are descriptive". Categories overlap and several hold under 30 instances (idempotence 30, lifecycle 19), so single-model differences of a few points are not stable. The categories were assigned by the benchmark's LLM pipeline.

### F5 · practice · separate-constraint-tests-beside-functional-tests · E0
- practice: score a repair with two executable tests, a functional test and a constraint test derived from a maintainer's review comment, and check each instance against a four-state matrix: original repository passes the functional test, the bug-injected repository fails it, a non-compliant patch passes functional and fails constraint, a gold patch passes both (Table 1, §Quality Assurance).
- claim: the two dimensions "are separable, executable, and jointly satisfiable"; constraint compliance "is not implied by functional correctness" (Introduction, §Benchmark Instance).
- evidence: the 303 retained instances satisfy the matrix by construction (Table 1). Counts of candidates rejected at each stage are "in the supplementary material" and not in the text.
- effect: helps
- reader: the claim is that the design works, backed only by the final instance count, so E0. F1 is the nearest evidence, and it uses the same tests. Whether the constraint tests measure what a maintainer would require is not checked against maintainers; the authors name this as future work.

### F6 · practice · two-phase-generate-execute-refine-synthesis · E0
- practice: have an agent build the instance in two phases with execution feedback: first the bug-injecting patch and a functional test, run until the test passes on the original and fails on the mutant; then the constraint test, the non-compliant patch and the gold patch, run until the matrix holds; go back to phase one if the functional task cannot support a separable violation (§Instance Synthesis).
- claim: this "progressive construction strategy" reduces "the difficulty of producing all interdependent patches and tests ... in a single generation step" (§Instance Synthesis).
- evidence: none given. No comparison with single-step generation, no success rate per phase, no counts of refinement rounds.
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted, E0. The practice is the authors' way of building the benchmark; it is of interest as a pattern for agent-made tests with a red and a green reference, not as a measured gain. The paper also says that the requirement that a reproduction program run against the buggy state "is prompt-guided rather than independently rule-enforced".

## Limits stated by the authors

- Python only; extension to other languages is future work (Conclusion).
- Review requirements that cannot yet be expressed as executable tests are outside the benchmark (Conclusion).
- Maintainer feedback is not part of instance construction; LLM-assisted synthesis and screening may introduce bias (Conclusion).
- One generation per model, instance and condition, so the −C and +C results are "an observed trade-off ... rather than ... a general causal claim" (RQ2).
- The construction mitigates memorisation of solutions "without claiming to eliminate all training-data contamination" (§SWE-Gate Benchmark).
- Categories overlap and several are small, so category comparisons are descriptive; GPT-4o-mini has too few functional successes per category for stable conclusions (RQ3).

## Reader's notes

- The benchmark's instances, issues, constraint texts, tests and gold patches were produced by an LLM agent and screened by an LLM and by the authors. The same pipeline defines what counts as a violation, so a systematic bias in the generator (for example, constraint tests that favour one implementation style) would enter every score. The paper reports no inter-rater check, no count of instances rejected by the manual review and no run of a human maintainer on the constraint tests.
- The 303 instances are bugs injected by an agent into existing repositories, not real issues. The paper says its transfer "preserves the authenticity of the original review requirement", and gives no evidence that agent performance on injected bugs matches performance on real issues.
- The three OpenAI models and DeepSeek-V4-Flash ran under one scaffold with a 100-step limit. Step use, cost and trajectories are not reported, so the FSR drop in F3 cannot be tied to behaviour.
- The headline 221 of 644 pools four models of very different strength; GPT-4o-mini contributes 28 functional passes (4.3% of the 644, reader's arithmetic) and the highest HFR, 53.6%.
- Practice for the repository's purpose: the −C setting is close to an agent working without review feedback, and the +C setting to an agent given the reviewer's comment in advance. The paper measures only the effect on test outcomes, not on any human reviewer's decision.
- The authors evaluate their own benchmark and their own pipeline; `independent: false`.

## Discrepancies

- The Introduction says the instances span "six software domains"; §Dataset Characteristics lists seven ("data analysis, web frameworks, testing frameworks, command-line tools, configuration management, symbolic computation, and data validation"), introduced with "including", so the list may be partial.
- RQ3's answer names Encoding/Escaping/Quoting among the lowest CFR values "for the three stronger models", but GPT-5.5's value is 68.3% (Table 5), above its Scope generalization (63.0%) and lifecycle (62.5%) values and equal to its schema value (68.3%).
