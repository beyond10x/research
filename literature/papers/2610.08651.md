## Question

Where did human control reside when implementation and review were delegated to coding agents
(RQ1), and what did the resulting checks and reviewing agents contribute and where did they fail
(RQ2) (§1)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · operator-approval-at-durable-and-production-points · E1
- practice: the coding agent and the reviewing agents may propose code, findings and lessons; the operator approves the approach before implementation, decides what becomes durable project context, and approves every change that goes to production after staging (§2, §3).
- claim: "The operator delegated implementation and much of its technical review, but not the authority to make consequential changes"; control was concentrated where agent outputs become persistent or operationally consequential, not exercised through line-level inspection (§3).
- evidence: one case described in prose. No counts, no comparison with another way of controlling the agents (§2, §3).
- effect: helps
- reader: E1 because it is one operator's workflow described by the researchers; the paper says it "does not establish that the workflow caused fewer defects" (§5). The claim is about where control sat, and the helps direction rests on the authors' reading of the operator's account.

### F2 · practice · fresh-context-reviewer-agent · E0
- practice: after the project tests pass, a separate agent instance with no access to the development conversation reviews each change before it goes to staging; it shares the builder's model family (§2, Appendix A).
- claim: "additional reviewers and monitors were useful, but their outputs could not be treated as ground truth"; adding a reviewer "moved rather than removed part of the oversight problem", because the operator had to judge the reviewer's evidential scope and authority (§5, §3).
- evidence: no data is given on what the reviewer found, missed or cost. The only stated limit is that the reviewers shared the builder's model family, "so shared blind spots are possible" (Appendix A).
- effect: mixed
- reader: asserted, with no review outcomes reported. Mixed because the paper credits the reviewer as useful and also names a cost in operator oversight and a possible shared blind spot.

### F3 · practice · fresh-context-meta-audit · E0
- practice: the operator commissions audits by newly instantiated agents that do not see the development conversation, over wider parts of the system and its records, not tied to one code change; their findings enter the normal remediation, regression-test and lesson process (§2).
- claim: the audits inspect "for problems missed by the normal development loop" (§2).
- evidence: no data is given on how many audits ran or what they found. The only audit outcome in the text is a failure of an audit (F10).
- effect: helps
- reader: described as designed to catch what the normal loop missed; the paper does not show that it did. The helps direction is the design intent, not a measured result. Same model family as the builder (Appendix A).

### F4 · practice · user-approved-project-memory · E0
- practice: a versioned project rulebook, the task and issue lists and reusable skill files are re-read by the primary agent at the start of each session; agents propose lessons at the end of a session and the operator approves which are stored (§2, §3).
- claim: carries "lessons and unfinished work" across fresh sessions; operator quote: "That's how the lessons and unfinished work stick even though each session starts fresh." (§2)
- evidence: the operator's quote only. No count of lessons, no measure of repeated failures before or after (§2).
- effect: helps
- reader: the operator's statement is the only support. The approval step is the same control as in F1.

### F5 · practice · fault-seeding-to-verify-tests · E0
- practice: check a test by deliberately recreating the targeted failure and confirming that the corresponding check fails; motivated by the fault-seeding idea behind mutation testing (§4).
- claim: the operator "became particularly concerned with whether these tests could genuinely detect the failures they claimed to cover"; "I don't trust a test just because it passes." (§4)
- evidence: no data is given: no count of tests checked this way, none found unable to fail.
- effect: helps
- reader: described as the operator's practice; no outcome is reported. The paper's own incidents (F9 to F12) are of monitors and audits that this practice is not said to have covered.

### F6 · practice · failure-to-gate-feedback · E2
- practice: when a problem is confirmed, the agent is asked to fix it, search for similar problems elsewhere, record a lesson that should carry forward, and add a regression test where appropriate; the accumulated check inventory was then mapped against four external references that had not been consulted when it was built: OWASP Top 10, OWASP Top 10 API Security Risks, CWE Top 25 and relevant HIPAA Security Rule provisions (§2, §4).
- claim: the checks accumulated through incidents and adversarial reviews address "many concerns represented in established security guidance"; this does not establish full platform security (§4).
- evidence: "Of 84 mapped items, 69 (82.1%) were covered by the pre-existing checks" (§4). No comparison with another way of building the checks.
- effect: helps
- reader: E2, a coverage count with nothing to compare against. "Covered" means a check exists; §4 itself shows that existing checks can fail to observe the outcome (F9 to F12). Who did the mapping is not stated. The standards are public, so training-data independence cannot be assumed (§4).

### F7 · practice · production-data-outside-agent-reach · E0
- practice: coding agents can reach repositories, test data and staging systems; production data is kept outside their reach by system architecture and file and folder permissions (§2, §3).
- claim: part of the asymmetric delegation: "production data was also kept outside agent reach" (§3).
- evidence: no data is given on attempts, breaches or incidents involving this boundary.
- effect: helps
- reader: stated as part of the design, with no incident or test of the boundary. The paper cites tool fencing and write staging as matching tactics (§3).

### F8 · practice · human-staging-inspection · E1
- practice: deploy each reviewed change to a private staging system for the operator to try and judge (§2).
- claim: "Staging worked well for observable behavior, while the clearest assurance failures occurred in less visible properties such as backups, audit execution, monitoring completeness and automated recovery." (§5)
- evidence: the four incidents of F9 to F12 as cases of properties she could not inspect; no count of defects caught in staging (§2, §4, §5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E1, a few cases. The split between visible and hidden properties is the authors' reading of the incidents. Staging results are not quantified, so "worked well" is asserted.

### F9 · observation · monitor-checks-proxy-not-outcome · E1
- practice: observed: several backup monitors checked whether intermediate events occurred without establishing that a usable backup existed (§4).
- claim: "a reassuring signal supported a stronger claim than the evidence justified"; the four concerns the authors draw are whether a check observed the intended outcome or a convenient proxy, whether it ran, whether missing results stay visible, and whether its authority is limited (§4).
- evidence: several backup monitors, no count and no dates; one operator's platform (§4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: one case class with no numbers. The operator "could not directly see whether a backup was usable" (§2), so the proxy was not caught by inspection.

### F10 · observation · audit-stops-without-failure · E1
- practice: observed: an audit stopped running without producing a failure (§4).
- claim: the absence of a failure signal was not evidence that the audit ran; "whether there was evidence that it had actually run" is one of the four concerns (§4).
- evidence: one incident, no duration or count of missed runs stated (§4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: a single incident reported from project records and the interviews. The paper does not say how long the audit was silent or how it was noticed.

### F11 · observation · empty-test-run-reported-as-success · E1
- practice: observed: a report calculated its pass rate only from checks that emitted results, so missing checks disappeared from the total (§4).
- claim: "whether missing or empty results remained visible" is one of the four concerns (§4).
- evidence: one incident, no pass-rate values stated (§4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the same condition as a test run that finds no tests and is read as success. One incident; how many checks were missing is not stated.

### F12 · observation · unbounded-automated-repair-from-monitor · E1
- practice: observed: a monitor classified legitimate long-running work as a failure and repeatedly attempted an automated repair (§4).
- claim: "the most consequential incident", causing operational disruption; the authors conclude a check's authority should be limited enough that a wrong result cannot immediately trigger harmful action (§4).
- evidence: one incident; the length and extent of the disruption are not stated (§4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the only incident the paper marks as having operational consequence. The paper does not say whether a retry limit was added afterwards.

## Limits stated by the authors

- A single case: one operator, one platform, an evolving workflow (§5).
- The operator took part in creating both the system and its governance process, "which may have led to an overly favourable interpretation"; the authors compared interviews with project records and focused on concrete failures, but the evidence is observational (§5).
- The study does not establish that the workflow caused fewer defects, that the platform was secure or compliant, or that the approach suffices for other users or systems (§5).
- Compliance with US health-privacy law was not assessed (§2).
- The 82.1% coverage does not show full platform security or model generalisation, because the standards are public (§4).
- The reviewing agents shared the builder's model family, so shared blind spots are possible (Appendix A).

## Reader's notes

- The authors' relationship to the operator is not stated. The data are the operator's account plus records the authors reconstructed from; no record is quoted or counted except the 84-item mapping. `independent: false` because the workflow's creator is the main source and the paper names this as a risk.
- The multi-model council (several providers answer one brief independently, for hard decisions, §2) has no outcome in the paper, so it is not a finding.
- No model versions, no dates for the incidents and no counts of agent sessions, reviews or audits are given. Nothing allows a rate of reviewer or monitor failures to be computed.
- The four incident classes are a listing of failures, not a sample; how many monitors and audits worked is not stated.
- The paper is a NeurIPS 2026 workshop paper of four pages of text (comment field in the arXiv metadata).
