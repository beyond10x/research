## Question

How much autonomy do developers give coding agents and when do they intervene, does careful code
review extend to approving agent actions, and what does supervising agents demand (§3.1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · autonomy-by-activity · E3
- practice: respondents rated, for 11 development activities, how much autonomy they gave agents on a scale from 1 (close supervision) to 5 (independent work); a crossed random-intercept model split the variance between developers and between activities (§4.2).
- claim: "Autonomy varies more across developers than across activities" (Finding 1).
- evidence: developers explain 27.6% of the variance (95% CI 22.7 to 32.7), activities 2.1% (CI 0.4 to 4.8), the remaining 70.3% is unexplained; an ordinal model gives 29% against 2% (§4.2). Rated 4 or 5 (high autonomy): testing 53% (n = 191), documentation 51%, planning 34% (n = 197), security and compliance 39% (Figure 2). Even for testing 12% kept close supervision and even for planning 13% let the agent work independently (§4.2).
- effect: none
- reader: E3 as a comparison of two variance components with no control. The effect is "activity type changes autonomy little"; the activity mean differences are small but the 70.3% residual may include developer-by-activity variation, so "developer" is not shown to be the cause. Self-reported ratings on a study-specific scale (§3.2).

### F2 · observation · intervention-for-intent-deviation · E2
- practice: why developers step in while an agent works, and where they want it to pause (§4.2).
- claim: "Developers intervene mainly when agents deviate from task intent, scope, or direction, and their desired check-ins reflect similar boundaries" (Finding 2).
- evidence: 57% watch the output and intervene when needed. Reasons: misunderstood the task or went the wrong way 65%, wrong or overcomplicated approach 54%, changed more than requested 51%, about to modify files it should not touch 35%, about to run a risky or destructive command 34% (§4.2). Check-in wanted before changes to critical, production or security-sensitive code 65%, when instructions are ambiguous 57% (§4.2). Of 70 reason and check-in pairs among 196 respondents, 14 are significant after correction, none involving risky commands; for example, those who intervened for misunderstanding wanted a check-in on ambiguity more often, 66% against 40% (ϕ = .244, q = .009) (§4.2).
- effect: hurts
- reader: counts of self-reported reasons, so E2; the pair associations are exploratory and unselected options are not "never" (§4.2). `effect` records that agent misreading of intent is the most frequent trigger of human correction, which is a cost; the paper measures no outcome of intervening.

### F3 · observation · action-approval-without-close-review · E2
- practice: how often developers approve seven types of agent action without close review, and whether they log agent actions (§4.3).
- claim: developers' care "does not necessarily extend to action approval" (Finding 3).
- evidence: 64% (130/203) often approved at least one of the seven action types without close review. Often or very often, by type: internet, APIs or connected tools 36% (n = 199), installing dependencies 29%, deployment or production configuration 27%, destructive or privileged commands 26%, security-sensitive code 22%, sensitive or customer data 21%, secrets or credentials 21% (Figure 3). Only 20% (41/203) logged or audited agent actions (§4.3). 63% required approval or warnings before risky actions and 53% restricted agent access (§4.3).
- effect: hurts
- reader: descriptive numbers from self-report with nothing to compare against, so E2. The authors state that approving without close review can be reasonable for familiar or low-risk actions and that they did not ask about allowlists, so pre-approved actions may be counted as approved without review (§6). For this repository: the paper measures how developers say they treat approval turns, not whether the approvals mattered.

### F4 · observation · careful-code-review · E3
- practice: respondents who carefully review most or all agent-generated code, compared with the others, on approving agent actions without close review (§4.3).
- claim: "Careful code review does not necessarily extend to careful action approval" (Finding 3); "code review and action approval are separate checks" (§4.3).
- evidence: 52% (105/202) carefully reviewed most or all code. Of them 61% (64/105) often approved at least one action type without close review, against 67% (65/97) of the others (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.41 to 1.42, p = .383). General code-review habits were not associated with any of the seven action types after correction (§4.3).
- effect: none
- reader: E3, two groups with no control; a difference of 6 points with a wide interval is a null at this sample size, not proof of independence. The grouping is self-described carefulness. Same respondent supplies both answers, which can share a response style (§6).

### F5 · observation · autonomy-and-action-approval · E3
- practice: developers who give agents more autonomy in an activity, compared with those who give less, on approving agent actions without close review (§4.3).
- claim: "approval requests may provide the least scrutiny where developers delegate the most" (§4.3).
- evidence: higher autonomy went with more frequent approval without close review in 48 of 77 activity and action pairs (ρ = .16 to .43, q < .05); none of 110 activity and safeguard pairs was significant. Autonomy in planning, architecture, and security and compliance was associated with it for all seven action types; autonomy in documentation and research for none (§4.3).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3 correlational, cross-sectional, and the authors say the two measures may overlap in meaning (§6); they point to the documentation and research null as the evidence against this. Direction of cause is open (H2 in §5.2).

### F6 · observation · incident-awareness · E3
- practice: developers aware of at least one security or privacy incident or concerning practice involving agents, compared with those not aware, on approving actions without close review (§4.3).
- claim: "Risk awareness and concern do not consistently correspond to more careful approval" (Finding 4).
- evidence: 73% (147/201) were aware of at least one. Of the aware, 70% (103/147) often approved at least one action type without close review, against 43% (20/46) of the others (OR 3.02, 95% CI 1.46 to 6.38, Fisher p = .001). With controls for frequency and duration of agent use and recruitment channel, adjusted OR 2.76 (CI 1.32 to 5.78, n = 183). 26 of 56 pairs of awareness answers and action types were significant. The 65 developers in safety-critical work (32%) approved without close review more often for six of seven action types. Most respondents were very or extremely concerned about leaking personal data (68%) or secrets (68%) (§4.3).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3 with a logistic adjustment, not a control. The comparison group is 46 people. Heavy users may both hear of more incidents and use more auto-approval; the adjustment uses only two usage measures. Answers from the same person on awareness and approval habit.

### F7 · practice · diff-inspection · E3
- practice: inspecting diffs when reviewing agent code, compared with developers who do not report it, on approving actions without close review (§4.3).
- claim: "One exception was inspecting diffs, which gives developers something concrete to check before they approve" (§4.3).
- evidence: six of 76 practice and action pairs were significant. Diff inspectors approved four action types without close review less often: reading secrets, accessing personal or regulated data, modifying CI/CD, infrastructure or production configuration, and running destructive or privileged commands (rrb = −.329 to −.252, q = .004 to .021). Checking edge cases went with less unreviewed approval of destructive or privileged commands (rrb = −.291, q = .007). Consulting documentation went with more of it for reading secrets (rrb = .285, q = .018) (§4.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: E3 correlation. The explanation (a diff is concrete) is the authors' guess. Six of 76 pairs is a small share, and one of the six has the opposite sign.

### F8 · observation · supervision-effort · E2
- practice: how demanding developers find 10 supervision activities and whether agents reduce their workload (§4.4).
- claim: "workload savings coexist with demanding supervision" (§1, Finding 5 and §4.4).
- evidence: 83% agreed agents reduced workload and 72% the mental effort. 63% (128/203) rated at least five of 10 supervision activities very or extremely demanding; 101/203 agreed on workload and rated at least five demanding. Highest: understanding code not written by me 59%, tracking changes across files, sessions or agents 58%, uncertainty about correctness 57%, deciding when to intervene 53%, approval interruptions 51% (Figure 4). 69% (140/202) agreed that line-by-line review could not keep up with agent-generated code; 55% found large or multi-file changes difficult to review (§4.3).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E2, shares of self-reports with no comparison against supervision of human work or before agents. "Workload reduced" and "supervision demanding" are both perceptions; the related work in the paper (§2.3) notes that perceived and measured benefit can differ.

### F9 · observation · recurring-agent-problems · E2
- practice: how often respondents meet agent problems and workflow disruptions (§4.4).
- claim: agent "output cannot be taken at face value, which adds to the supervision demands" (§4.4).
- evidence: often encountered: agent claiming completion while parts were missing or broken 41%, over-engineering 41%, plausible but incorrect output 39%. Over time 65% noticed code becoming more complex, bloated or hard to maintain. 37% often restarted sessions that became too long or confused, 39% hit usage limits before completing a task, 25% lost progress after unexpected context resets (§4.4). More frequent agent problems went with less agreement that agents were worth their cost for 11 of 12 problems (for example plausible but incorrect output, ρ = −.261, q = .007) (§4.4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E2 for the frequencies, with the worth-the-cost association E3 within the same finding. No baseline against human-written code or other tools.

### F10 · observation · supervision-demand-and-unreviewed-code · E3
- practice: developers who find monitoring while multitasking and approval interruptions more demanding, compared with those who find them less demanding, on approving generated code they did not fully understand (§4.4).
- claim: "Supervision demands may also affect what developers accept" (§4.4).
- evidence: 50% agreed they sometimes approved agent-generated code they did not fully understand because rewriting would take too long; 46% worried about being held responsible for such code. Two of 10 supervision activities were associated: monitoring while doing other tasks (ρ = .236, q = .040) and approval requests and interruptions (ρ = .224, q = .043) (§4.4). Hypothesis H4 states the reverse direction is still open (§5.2).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3 correlation, 2 of 10 pairs, with q just under .05. Causal direction is not tested.

### F11 · practice · bounded-observable-reversible-autonomy · E2
- practice: agents that work within permissions and limits (bounded), show what they do and why (observable), and can be rolled back (reversible), as conditions respondents name for granting more autonomy (§4.4, Table 1).
- claim: "Beyond reliability, developers want autonomy that is bounded, observable, and reversible" (Finding 6).
- evidence: in 175 coded answers to what would make them grant more autonomy: reliable 115 (66%), observable 61 (35%), bounded 60 (34%), reversible 34 (19%); 104 answers (59%) raised at least one of the last three. In 97 answers to "one thing to fix or add": reliable 58 (60%), observable 13 (13%), bounded 11 (11%), reversible 8 (8%). Among the 90 who answered both, bounded 32% against 12% and observable 28% against 14% were higher in the autonomy question (§4.4, Table 1).
- effect: helps
- reader: E2, counts of coded open-ended answers; the stated willingness has not been tested against what developers do when such features exist. Coding was by the first author with team discussion (§3.5). Category names were taken from one respondent's words (P017).

### F12 · practice · action-preview-and-rollback-by-risk · E0
- practice: agent designs proposed by the authors: confirm goal and scope before acting, apply permissions by action risk, preview what an action will change with a reviewable record, and offer checkpoints and rollback (D1 to D4, §5.2); teams agree on permissions, review agent actions as well as code, and count supervision as work (P1 to P3).
- claim: these "could" improve approval decisions and reduce supervision effort; the authors state the effect as a hypothesis for later studies (§5.2, H1).
- evidence: none tested. The suggestions rest on F1, F3 and F11 above.
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted by the authors from survey data; E0 for the effect on approval quality. Listed because the proposed designs are specific and testable.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Self-reports: recall error, social desirability, and differing understanding of questions (§6).
- The approval question does not cover allowlists, and approval without close review can be reasonable for familiar or low-risk actions (§6).
- One time point: associations are not causal and the direction between risk awareness and approval habit is unknown (§6).
- Possible response bias, careless or AI-generated responses; screening may have removed valid responses and kept fraudulent ones (§6).
- Non-probability sample of current agent users, most in the United States and using Claude Code; not claimed to generalise (§6).
- Many tests, controlled with Benjamini-Hochberg correction; non-significant results do not show no relationship (§6).

## Reader's notes

- No finding has a control. Every comparison is between self-selected groups of respondents who answered both sides of the comparison, so a shared response style can produce correlations. The authors list this and give three arguments against it (§6).
- Screening was heavy: 583 submissions to 203. The time cut at 13 minutes removed 251 of 379 community-channel responses (about 66%, reader's arithmetic) and none from Prolific (§3.4). The cut was set from a mixture model fitted on the same data. Estimates for F2 and F6 and part of F10 were "too uncertain" in the community-channel half alone (§4.5).
- LLM judgement (GPT-5.6) was used only to flag responses for human review, with 5 of its 9 independent flags new (§3.4).
- "Without close review" is the respondent's own label for a habit. The paper does not measure what the unreviewed approvals led to, so it cannot say whether the approvals were wrong.
- 70% of respondents' autonomy-rating variance is unexplained by developer or activity (F1).
- Authors have no stake in an agent product, so `independent: true`.
- For this repository's question: F2, F3 and F5 describe where human approval and correction turns occur and where they are given least attention, from the developer's side and without transcripts.

## Discrepancies

- Abstract says "more than half of their responses called for agents that are bounded, observable, or reversible"; the 59% (104 of 175) applies to the question about granting more autonomy only. In the "one thing" question the three categories are 13%, 11% and 8% of 97 answers (Table 1). The text does note the difference in wording (§4.4).
- The abstract and §1 give 61% as the share of careful code reviewers who often approved an action type without close review; §4.3 gives 67% for the other developers, so careful reviewers were 6 points lower, not significantly (F4). The abstract does not give the comparison group.
