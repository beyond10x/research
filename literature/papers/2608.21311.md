## Question

How prevalent is review of agent-authored pull requests by other AI agents on GitHub (RQ1), and does
reviewer behaviour vary with the author-reviewer pairing (RQ2) (§1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · authoring-agent-changes-reviewer-comment-mix · E3
- practice: the same reviewer, CodeRabbit, comments on PRs written by different authoring agents; its self-declared comment category (potential issue, refactor, nitpick, verification) is extracted by rule-based matching and compared across authors with at least 200 classified comments (§3.5, §5.1).
- claim: "CodeRabbit produces different comment-category distributions for Claude Code- and Copilot-authored PRs"; the authors "make no claim about code quality" (§5.1).
- evidence: 35,248 classified comments. Refactor share: Claude Code 35.0% (N = 8,757), Cursor 33.5%, OpenAI Codex 29.2%, Copilot 10.5% (N = 4,260), Devin 9.7%. Potential-issue share: Copilot 49.0%, Devin 43.9%, Claude Code 42.0%, Cursor 34.9%, Codex 29.9% (Table 3). Claude Code against Copilot refactor: 24.5 points, 95% CI [23.1, 25.9], Wald test. Cramér's V = 0.150, chi-square 3177.5, df 24 (§5.1).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3, a comparison between author groups with no control and no matching on PR size, language or repository, which the authors name (§5.1, §6). The categories are CodeRabbit's own labels, not validated defects (§7). A larger refactor share is not a better or worse review. Effect is mixed because refactor rises where potential-issue falls.

### F2 · observation · same-product-review-comment-volume · E3
- practice: a PR is reviewed by the same product that authored it, against a PR reviewed by another product; mean review comments per PR by reviewer, for the 4 reviewers with at least 200 PRs in both groups (§5.2).
- claim: "three of four bots show same-product PRs receiving 58-65% more comments on average"; the gap is a long right tail, not a population shift, and "should not be interpreted causally" (§5.2).
- evidence: mean comments per PR, cross against same-product (Table 4): Copilot 1.49 against 2.35 (21,022 and 166,442 PRs, |delta| 0.15), Devin 1.09 against 1.80 (392 and 3,811, 0.14), Amazon Q 4.94 against 8.08 (522 and 535, 0.27), OpenAI Codex 0.91 against 0.89 (5,485 and 37,247, 0.02). Mann-Whitney p below 10^-292 for Copilot, 2.0 × 10^-3 for Codex.
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3, between groups with no control. The authors list change size, language, repository and reviewer composition as differing between groups (§5.2) and say same-product review may be an integrated product workflow that fires automatically (§6). Cliff's delta is small or negligible for all 4, so the p-values reflect sample size. Effect is mixed: 3 bots higher, Codex flat.

### F3 · observation · same-vs-cross-product-first-review-latency · E3
- practice: time from PR creation to the first AI review event, same-product pairs against cross-product pairs, among pairs with complete nonnegative timestamps (§5.3).
- claim: cross-product pairs had the lower median latency, "the opposite of a 'product rubber-stamping its own' reading", but the difference "is compositional" (§5.3).
- evidence: cross-product median 1.2 minutes (p25 0.4, p75 4.4, N = 37,420); same-product median 4.7 minutes (p25 2.4, p75 38.2, N = 66,500) (Table 6, labelled Table 5 in the text). 103,920 pairs retained; 79.2% of cross-product and 31.9% of same-product pairs had usable timestamps (§7). Copilot, the reviewer that dominates the same-product group, has median 17.7 minutes (p75 73.9) (Table 6).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3 with no control, and the authors decline to attribute the gap to product pairing. The retained share differs by 47 points between groups (reader's arithmetic from §7), so the comparison may be selection. The paper has no human-reviewer baseline for latency (§5.3).

### F4 · observation · first-review-latency-by-reviewer · E3
- practice: median time to the first AI review differs by reviewer bot, pooled over same- and cross-product PRs, for bots with at least 100 qualifying pairs (§5.3).
- claim: "AI review on these PRs commonly begins within minutes of PR creation"; the aggregate difference between groups reflects which reviewers are in each (§5.3).
- evidence: median minutes (p25, p75, N), Table 6: Gemini Code Assist 0.5 (0.3, 1.8; 9,873), Amazon Q 1.2 (0.9, 1.6; 959), OpenAI Codex 2.6 (1.9, 3.7; 34,765), CodeRabbit 7.0 (3.7, 31.6; 6,709), Copilot 17.7 (1.0, 73.9; 51,437).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3, a comparison between reviewer products with no control on PR size, repository or trigger configuration. Latency is first recorded review event, and says nothing about usefulness (§8). Effect is mixed: the span runs from 0.5 to 17.7 minutes across reviewers.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Attribution is by signature; absence of a signature is not evidence a PR was not AI-authored or AI-reviewed, so counts are lower bounds and rates describe the attributable population (§7).
- Author-side attribution is far weaker than reviewer-side; 38.0% of candidate PRs were quarantined, 96.9% of them as branch-name-only (§3.3, §7).
- The content measure uses CodeRabbit's self-declared categories, not validated against human annotation or an LLM judge (§7).
- Observational associations only; PR size, language, repository and product integration are confounders (§7).
- "Closed-loop" does not exclude human review; human review is not observed (§1, §7). There is no matched human-authored control dataset (§3.4).
- Public GitHub only; results may not generalise to non-adopting projects. Cross-product is product level, not model level (§7).
- The latency analysis is complete-case and the authors read it descriptively (§7).
- No data on whether AI reviews were correct, accepted or useful; proposed as future work (§8).

## Reader's notes

- No finding here measures whether an AI review improved the code. All four findings describe reviewer output (category mix, comment count, delay). Counts of how common AI review is (8.8% of identified agent-authored PRs, 1.6% cross-product, 57 to 25,492 cross-product PRs between 2025-Q1 and Q3, Table 2 shares per author) are prevalence figures with no direction, so they are not findings under the schema; they are in the header, §4.1 and Table 2.
- Product-level pairing does not tell whether author and reviewer ran the same model (§6, §8). The same-product against cross-product split is therefore a weak proxy for the same-model question that matters for correlated blind spots.
- Same-product groups are dominated by Copilot (166,442 of 208,145 PRs, Table 4 and §3.4), and cross-product by OpenAI Codex as author (31,601 of 45,269, §4.2). Aggregate same-against-cross comparisons mostly compare these two products' setups.
- Table 4's "Cross PRs" column is reviewer-perspective pairs, and for Copilot (21,022) equals the pair count of §4.2, not the author-perspective PR count of Table 2 (§5.2, Table 4 caption).
- The authors built the attribution and the classifier, and say the relabelling check between their two passes is internal consistency, not external validation (§3.3).
- Reviewer-side and author-side attribution coverage differ, so the percentages 8.8 and 1.6 inherit both (§7).

## Discrepancies

- The latency table is headed "Table 5 Table 6" and the text refers to Table 5 (§5.3); it is the table labelled 6.
- §3.2 gives 4,141,107 review entries and §3.3 gives 4,141,598 review events with 590 quarantined; 4,141,598 minus 590 plus the 98 duplicate matches is 4,141,106, one fewer than 4,141,107 (reader's arithmetic).
- The same-product group is called "same-product" (Tables 2 and 4 text), "self" (Table 4 columns) and "A×B-self" (Figure 1 legend). Naming only.
