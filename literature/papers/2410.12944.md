## Question

How much does access to three AI coding features shorten the time a professional developer needs for
an enterprise-grade task, and do developer or task characteristics change that (RQ1 to RQ3, §I)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: randomly assign developers, after a questionnaire and 30 minutes of training on the AI features, to enable the three AI features (AI Code Completion, Smart Paste, Natural Language to Code) or to disable them, for the same task (§IV).
- claim: developers with the AI features "were significantly faster at completing the task" (§VI.B).
- evidence: mean time on task 96 min (N = 47, SE = 9.3) with AI against 114 min (N = 46, SE = 8.1) without; Student's t-test on log time, t(83.6) = 2.11, p = .038 (§VI.B). The task was a 10-file, 474-line change list in C++, finished when all tests passed (§IV-A).
- effect: helps
- reader: randomised on identical tasks, so E4. The unadjusted gap is 15.8% of the control mean (reader's arithmetic). One task of at most a few hours, one company, the company's own tools, and the authors evaluate their own team's tools (§V). The measure is time in the IDE until the last interaction, not correctness or review outcome; code quality was not measured (§VIII).

### F2 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: as F1; the effect is estimated again in regressions on log time with developer-level and task-level covariates (§IV-C).
- claim: "developers who used AI were about 21% faster ... controlling for other factors"; the adjusted estimate "lost its significance" (§I, §VI.C).
- evidence: ExpCon coefficient in Table III, −0.25 (Model 1), −0.24 (Model 2, best fit), −0.30 (Model 3); 21% from Model 2 and 26% from Model 3. Model 2: 95% CI [−0.51, 0.03], p = 0.086 (§VI.C, footnote 2). Model 2 adjusted R2 0.095, model p = 0.011.
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 by randomisation. The direction is the same in every model and the interval includes zero in the best-fit model, so the 21% is a point estimate and the abstract's "significantly" refers to the unadjusted test in F1. The authors call the interval "large". Model 3 (task covariates only) shows p < .05 for ExpCon in Table III but the model itself has p = 0.209.

### F3 · observation · daily-coding-hours-and-ai-speedup · E3
- practice: none applied; the interaction of the AI condition with self-reported hours of coding per day (0 to 4 hours against 5 or more) is estimated (§IV-B, §VI.D).
- claim: developers who code more hours a day "may be faster with AI tools than those who code less" (§VII).
- evidence: interaction coefficient β = −0.29 (SE 0.29), not significant; Model 4 as a whole p = 0.018, adjusted R2 0.075 (Table III, §VI.D). Outside the interaction, 5 or more hours a day went with 32% less time (β = −0.38, p < .05) across both conditions (§VI.C).
- effect: helps
- reader: a subgroup split, not an assigned condition, and the interaction term is not significant; the authors say "tentatively". The hours variable is self-reported and dichotomised, and the study states it was powered for the main effect and not for interactions (§V). Hours coded per day correlates with seniority level (Table I), so the two are not separated.

### F4 · observation · seniority-and-ai-speedup · E3
- practice: none applied; the interaction of the AI condition with seniority level (3 to 7) is estimated (§IV-B, §VI.D).
- claim: seniority was negative against time but "not statistically significant", so the hypothesis that senior developers gain more is rejected (§VI.D); the authors still read the sign as hinting that senior developers work faster with AI (§VII, §VIII).
- evidence: interaction p = 0.706 (model not shown). Main effect of one level up: 15% less time (β = −0.16) in Model 2, across both conditions (§VI.C).
- effect: none
- reader: no difference was found. The prose in §VII reads a non-significant sign as a result, which the p value does not support. Participants are all Google engineers, so the level range is narrow (§V).

### F5 · observation · tool-experience · E3
- practice: none applied; the interaction of the AI condition with prior AI tool use, counted as the number of 13 lifecycle areas where AI tools are used more than once a week, is estimated (§IV-B).
- claim: the hypothesis that more frequent users are faster with the study's tools is rejected (§VI.D).
- evidence: interaction p = 0.235, negative sign (model not shown). NbrHighFreqAreas coefficient −0.01 and 0.00 in Models 1 and 2 (Table III).
- effect: none
- reader: no difference was found, with a sample not powered for interactions (§V). The authors list four possible reasons, including a ceiling effect and a low usability floor (§VII), none tested. All participants had a 30-minute training on the tools before the task, which may reduce the gap between experienced and new users (§IV).

## Limits stated by the authors

- All participants are Google employees using Google's own tools in the summer of 2024; the estimate may not carry to other populations, tools or later dates (abstract, §V).
- The authors cannot remove the risk of evaluating their own team's tools, and no outside party could run the study (§V).
- The task was designed to take under 3 hours and cannot match the complexity of real work (§V).
- The study was powered for the main effect, not for interactions; larger samples or field experiments are needed (§V).
- Code quality and equity across seniority levels were not studied (§VIII).

## Reader's notes

- The AI features are inline completion, paste adjustment and a prompt-to-code popup in an IDE. No autonomous agent, no multi-step agent loop and no instruction file is involved; the paper bears on agentic coding only as a randomised baseline for developer-with-AI against developer-without-AI.
- Randomisation came after the 30-minute training, which went to everyone. The no-AI group had been shown the tools and was then asked not to use them (§IV); the paper does not report checks of compliance in either group.
- 96 were assigned and 93 completed, but the paper does not say whether the 3 who did not complete are excluded from the speed comparison, nor why the groups are 47 and 46 (§VI).
- Time on task ends at the last observed IDE interaction, so time spent verifying outside the IDE is not counted (§IV-B).
- The outcome is time to passing tests. Whether the AI-assisted solutions differ in quality was not measured.
- Time ranged from 4.4 to 271.1 minutes (§VI); the regression uses log time, and the group means in minutes (F1) are not log-scale values.

## Discrepancies

- §VI.B gives the mean log time as 4.6 for the AI group and 4.3 for the no-AI group and calls the AI group faster. A higher log time is slower, and the group means in minutes (96 and 114) imply log means near 4.56 and 4.74 (reader's arithmetic), so the two labels are swapped.
- §VI gives overall time as "a mean of 104.4 minutes (Standard Error of the mean = 60.3)"; with the group SEs of 9.3 and 8.1 the 60.3 looks like a standard deviation.
- §IV-A says the task was expected to take 30 minutes to 2.5 hours and §V says it was designed to need no more than 3 hours; §VI reports times up to 271.1 minutes.
- The abstract says AI "significantly shortened" time and gives about 21% as the best estimate; the 21% is the Model 2 coefficient with p = 0.086, "NS" by the authors' own wording (§VI.C).
- §VI.C says the adjusted effect "lost its significance", while Table III marks ExpCon at p < .05 in Model 3 (−0.30).
- Table I lists 95 for TopLangCpp and 96 for every other variable; the text does not say which participant lacks it.
- §VII writes "we estimate a roughly 21% increase in development speed"; a 21% shorter time is not a 21% higher speed.
