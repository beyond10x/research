## Question

Do human reviewers of AI-agent pull requests reduce review rigor with repeated exposure, and can this
be detected from the language of their review comments (abstract, §1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · approval-rate-rises-with-reviewer-exposure · E3
- practice: observed, not assigned: each reviewer's approval rate on agent PRs early against late in that reviewer's own sequence of reviews, and across ten experience deciles (§3.2, §4.1).
- claim: "behavioural drift is real"; approval rate rises with reviewer exposure, gradually and not in a phase transition (§4.1, §6).
- evidence: midpoint split over 400 reviewers, 30.5% to 36.6% (+6.1 pp), Wilcoxon p = 8.6e-8, Cohen's d = 0.25; 52% of reviewers more approving, 28% less, 20% unchanged (§4.1). Table 1, 173 reviewers: deciles 27.9, 27.1, 30.0, 27.8, 33.9, 35.5, 32.5, 34.2, 38.3, 42.8, a rise of 14.9 pp, R2 = 0.47. A linear fit is preferred over PELT segmentation, ΔBIC = +12.0 at N = 50 bins (§4.1).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3, a before and after within reviewers with no control group. The paper reads a rising approval rate as less rigorous review, but measures no review depth and no defects; "AI-agent code genuinely improves over time" is not ruled out (§5.4), and the paper does not say what counts as an approval or whether the PR mix changes with experience. d = 0.25 is small. `hurts` follows the paper's habituation reading, the schema has no value for a result with no outcome measure.

### F2 · observation · approval-rise-specific-to-agent-prs · E3
- practice: observed, not assigned: the experience effect of F1 re-estimated with calendar date and PR size as covariates, per agent, and set against the monthly approval rate of human-authored PRs in the same repositories (§4.6).
- claim: the rise is not general reviewer leniency and not a calendar trend (§4.6).
- evidence: experience coefficient +0.11 with 65% of reviewers positive, calendar coefficient −0.007; PR size "does not eliminate the experience effect"; Copilot (n = 209) and Devin (n = 88) similar in direction (§4.6). Human-PR approval "does not exhibit a directional change" over the same months, shown in Figure 6 only.
- effect: hurts
- reader: the human-PR comparison rules out one confound (global leniency) and the authors say so (§5.4). Agent quality, PR type and repository mix over time are not controlled. Only two agents have enough data for the per-agent check. The coefficient's unit is not stated in the parts read.

### F3 · practice · lexical-metric-monitor-of-reviewer-habituation · E3
- practice: monitor reviewers' inline comments with four hand-crafted indicators (MTLD lexical diversity, Shannon entropy, technical specificity, constructive actionability) across experience deciles and in a classifier of early against late comments (§3.3, §3.6).
- claim: these indicators do not detect habituation: "dashboards that report MTLD or technical-specificity trends should not be expected to flag habituation" (§5.2).
- evidence: 10,104 comments, Table 2: Spearman rho against decile MTLD +0.13 (p = 0.73), entropy −0.53 (p = 0.12), technical specificity +0.12 (p = 0.75), constructive −0.04 (p = 0.91); Bonferroni-corrected Mann-Whitney deciles 1 against 10, all p ≥ 0.36. Within reviewer, 339 reviewers: paired Wilcoxon p ≥ 0.29, about 50/50 up and down (§4.2). Flat on the 15-word subset (n = 4,094) and the 6 to 22 word band (n = 5,239) (§4.2). Window classifier, Table 3: logistic regression F1 = 0.485 and MLP F1 = 0.590 against majority-class F1 = 0.71.
- effect: none
- reader: the comparison is a null result against a majority baseline and against the paper's own embedding method, not a controlled test. The target label is sequence position (late half), not a validated habituation event, so a flat indicator and a missing effect cannot be told apart. Comments are short (median 11 words, 24% at most 5 words, §5.1). The authors propose the alternative, `independent: false`.

### F4 · practice · embedding-centroid-monitor-of-reviewer-habituation · E3
- practice: embed each inline comment with the off-the-shelf all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence encoder (384 dimensions) and measure the distance between a reviewer's late-half and early-half comment centroids against a random-permutation null on the reviewer's own comments (§3.4, §4.3).
- claim: reviewers drift idiosyncratically in embedding space; the per-reviewer centroid distance "is a usable signal", cheap and unsupervised (§4.3, §5.2).
- evidence: 342 reviewers, mean late-to-early cosine distance 0.444 against 0.330 under the null, paired Wilcoxon p < 1e-3 (§4.3). Population-level probes are flat: cosine to the decile 1 centroid 0.677 to 0.686 (rho = +0.39, p = 0.26), dispersion rho = −0.22 (p = 0.53), PCA drift rho = +0.16 (p = 0.65); only the k-means cluster mix (k = 8) shifts, chi-square 135.3, 63 dof, p = 3.3e-7 (§4.3). "Grows with reviewer experience in about 60% of individual cases" (§5.2, no table).
- effect: helps
- reader: E3, a comparison against a permutation null, with no check that the drift is a loss of rigor rather than topic change, a change of repository or of PR type. A shift in comment content is not a shift in review depth. The authors evaluate their own method.

### F5 · practice · embedding-statistics-classifier-of-reviewer-phase · E3
- practice: train a logistic regression or a small MLP (32 and 16 units) on 3 embedding statistics of a 5-comment window to predict whether the window lies in the late half of a reviewer's sequence, with 5-fold reviewer-stratified cross-validation (§3.6, §4.5).
- claim: the embedding classifier reaches F1 = 0.74 and "outperforms hand-crafted" features; "a usable habituation classifier" (§4.5, §1).
- evidence: Table 3, 8,039 windows from 237 reviewers, 55% positive. Majority class F1 0.71 (accuracy 0.55); stratified random F1 0.55; embedding LR F1 0.674; embedding MLP accuracy 0.660, precision 0.638, recall 0.884, F1 0.741; hand-crafted MLP F1 0.590; combined features F1 0.672 (LR) and 0.667 (MLP). The authors call the gap over majority "+0.03", specificity about 0.38, and the model "biased toward predicting late" (§4.5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3 between feature sets on the same windows; there is no external control. The F1 gain over a majority classifier is 0.03 and it comes from recall; balanced accuracy (stated as 0.61 and 0.63) is the fairer number and it is modest. The label is position in the sequence, so a window classifier can learn any drift over time, habituation or not. The authors evaluate their own method.

### F6 · observation · approval-shift-precedes-comment-style-shift · E3
- practice: observed, not assigned: Granger-causality tests in both directions between bin-level approval rate and six language signals, 50 experience-percentile bins, first differences, lags 1 to 4 (§3.5, §4.4).
- claim: the popular claim that linguistic decline precedes behavioural drift is not supported; approval-rate change predicts later technical-specificity change, not the reverse (§4.4).
- evidence: approval rate to technical specificity p = 9.6e-4, 5.0e-4, 8.1e-4, 1.5e-3 at lags 1 to 4; technical specificity to approval rate p = 0.036 at lag 2 and p ≥ 0.11 at the other lags; MTLD, entropy, constructive, the linguistic composite and the embedding distance do not predict approval rate at any lag (p ≥ 0.10). Of 48 tests only approval rate to technical specificity survives Bonferroni correction (§4.4).
- effect: none
- reader: 50 bins of first differences is a short series, and bins pool different reviewers, so the result is a population-level statement about ordering in experience, not about any reviewer. The direction found for one metric out of the four that showed no trend elsewhere (F3) is hard to interpret. `none` records that no language signal led approval rate; the schema has no value for an ordering result.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Seven months of public-repository activity; enterprise settings and longer reviewer trajectories may differ (§5.4).
- The shift in approval rate is small (d = 0.25) and its practical importance depends on defect rates that are not measured (§5.4).
- An off-the-shelf encoder without code-aware fine-tuning; F1 = 0.74 is "a lower bound" (§5.4).
- The late-phase label is sequence position, not an externally validated habituation event (§5.4).
- AI-agent code may have improved over time, raising approval rate with no reviewer-side change; the human-PR control addresses only global leniency; post-merge defect rates lie outside the AIDev release (§5.4).

## Reader's notes

- No outcome that matters downstream is measured: no defect escaped review, no review depth, no merged-then-reverted rate. "Habituation" is inferred from approval rate and comment style.
- Experience is the index of a reviewer's review in their own sequence, so decile and calendar time are confounded for each reviewer; §4.6's covariate check is the only separation, and its model is not described in the parts read.
- Reviewers are repeat reviewers with at least 10 agent PRs, a selected group; the share of review activity they cover is not stated.
- The five agents are pooled in the main results; agent attribution is 94% precise per the dataset authors (§3.1).
- Of the 400 reviewers, 391 contribute comments (Table 2) and the embedding steps use 342 or 339 with enough comments; the sample changes between analyses.
- The reviewed code is the output of the agents named, not a treatment applied by the authors; nothing here says what a team should do about a rising approval rate. Its use for this loop is as a measure of when a human gate on agent PRs may stop checking.

## Discrepancies

- The abstract says Granger approval-rate to technical specificity is "p < 10^-3 at all lags"; §4.4 gives 1.5e-3 at lag 4, and §4.4 later says "p < 0.002 at all lags". A Bonferroni threshold over 48 tests is 0.05 / 48 = 0.00104 (reader's arithmetic), which 1.5e-3 does not pass.
- The abstract gives Spearman "p >= 0.11"; the Spearman p values in §4.2 and Table 2 are 0.73, 0.12, 0.75 and 0.91, so the smallest is 0.12. The 0.11 occurs in §4.4 as a Granger lag p.
- §4.5 gives balanced accuracy 0.61 in parentheses and 0.63 in the next sentence for the same classifier; Table 3 gives no balanced accuracy.
- The slope of the decile fit is +1.6 pp per decile in §4.1 and +1.51 in the Figure 1 legend.
- The abstract and §4.5 call F1 = 0.74 evidence of signal; §4.5 says the gain over the majority baseline (0.71) is +0.03.
- §3.1 gives the data window as January to July 2025; Figure 6's axis runs to 2025-08. §1 and the abstract say 207 days.
