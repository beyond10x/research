## Question

What can GitHub account records and agent-session transcripts establish about agents' part in
approvals and merges, which merges lacked approval and required-check evidence on the exact revision
merged, and what must come from the reviewers themselves (§I, RQ1 to RQ3)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · enforce-conventions-in-tooling · E3
- practice: branch protection on main and production requiring one approval from a non-author account and a passing required check, applied to administrators too and left on, compared by period (§II.D, §III.A).
- claim: from 3 October, "all 263 non-bot merges into main had a valid non-author approval and a passing check"; protection "held where it applied" (§I, §V.A).
- evidence: core gate property (approval and passing check on the merged head, both before the merge), Table III. Setup period, all branches: 103 of 391 merges (26.3%). Transition period: 41 of 59. Enforced period: 267 of 273 over all destinations and 263 of 263 into main. Of the 24 gated-phase failures, 18 are in the transition period and 6 in the enforced period (the 6 are stack merges, see F4).
- effect: helps
- reader: a before and after comparison by period with no control. The periods "describe our history; they are not experimental treatments" (§III.A). The required check changed on 7 October, and the enforced period is about two weeks. The authors run the team they evaluate.

### F2 · practice · gate-recomputation-from-platform-records · E2
- practice: recompute the gate property for every merge from the platform's review and check-run records (Algorithm 1), independently of what branch protection enforced, fetching every attempt of the required check, not the latest only (§III.B).
- claim: the recomputation "confirmed the gate wherever branch protection applied and found where it did not": enforcement switched off, a merge script that recorded failed checks as passing, and a stack collapsed through unprotected branches (Abstract, §III.B).
- evidence: the check needs "on the order of a thousand read-only API calls" for the 786 pull requests (§III.B). It found 24 failures in the gated phase; none rests on absent data alone, except 4 merges never approved at all (§III.B). The authors ran it once for the study (Table VII, item 6).
- effect: helps
- reader: the numbers describe what the check found in this one repository; nothing is compared with a check that was not run. The authors name the script and the data limits: missing history can look like a missing check (§IX).

### F3 · practice · agent-credentials-reach-protection-settings · E2
- practice: agent sessions ran under the technical lead's GitHub account, which could open pull requests, approve, merge and administer branch protection; no agent had an identity of its own (§II.C).
- claim: "The credentials that let a session approve also let it suspend the rule it was approving under" (§VII.A).
- evidence: every protection change found in the transcripts "was issued by an agent session" (§VII.A; web-interface changes would not appear). 25 September: a session removed administrator enforcement at 14:03 and it and its workers merged nine pull requests as administrators; none of the 10 pull requests merged that day was approved (§V.A). 29 September: a script set the required approval count to zero, merged one pull request and restored it ten seconds later. From 30 September scripts removed enforcement before each merge and restored it after. All 18 transition-period exceptions match recorded protection changes (§V.A).
- effect: hurts
- reader: cases and counts from the authors' own change log and transcripts. Part of the toggling was under a recorded emergency change that the lead directed, so the agent acted on a person's instruction in some cases (§V.A). Counted by what could be seen, not by what sessions were told.

### F4 · practice · gate-limited-to-main-and-production · E2
- practice: protection covered main and production only; stacked pull requests based on each other's branches merged into unprotected working branches (§II.D).
- claim: stacked merges carried approvals on superseded heads and checks that had not run, and "nothing blocked" them (§V.B).
- evidence: 14 October, a stack of 8 pull requests: the orchestrator merged the 7 upper ones in 58 seconds with one loop command; the six lower heads had checks that started about 8 to 11 minutes after the merge, and their approvals named earlier commits (Table V). In the enforced period 6 of the 7 stack merges lacked the property (§V.A). The combined change (1,325 changed lines in 56 files) then passed the gate into main with a fresh approval and check (§V.B).
- effect: hurts
- reader: one stack case and 7 merges; the authors say integrating a stack and gating only the combined change "is a reasonable design" and violated nothing under their control (§V.A, §V.B). The harm is to the evidence an auditor could read, not to a shown defect.

### F5 · practice · agent-submitted-approval-through-human-account · E2
- practice: a PM session pre-reviewed an engineer's pull request and submitted the approval through the lead's account, through a wrapper that approved only if every check on the exact head had passed, or directly with `gh` (§II.C, §IV.B).
- claim: for 71 merges the record shows "a two-person control between two people" while the only valid approval was submitted by a session, and "the decider cannot be established" (§V.A, §VI.B).
- evidence: gated phase, the lead account approved 86 non-bot pull requests and a session submitted all 86 (Table I; 88 approval commands, 31 direct and 57 by wrapper). Of the 263 enforced-period merges into main, 192 rested on an engineer's approval and 71 only on the lead account's (Table IV); for 57 of the 71 the change also carried agent markers from the engineer's account. 71 of 263 is 27% (Abstract). 43 of the 71 had reached production by the cutoff (§VII.B). The lead kept no record of which approvals carried his own decision (§IV.B).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the outcome is the audit evidence of a person's decision, not code quality. The count of agent submissions is matched from transcripts to platform events; a hand check of 15 approval matches and 15 merge matches confirmed 15 and 14 (§III.C). Whether the lead read the change is unknown, so the 71 do not show absent decisions.

### F6 · practice · merge-script-reads-combined-status · E1
- practice: a merge script read the pull request's combined status instead of the required check on the head commit, after a failed job was re-run (§V.D).
- claim: the script recorded four merges as passing CI when they had not, at a time when enforcement was off and the script "was the only remaining check" (§V.D).
- evidence: 1 October. Three of the four had a completed failed `test` run before the merge and the fourth had no run that started before it; the failing test was date-dependent and failing on main as well. The fix reads the required check on the head and requires success; a standing rule forbids recording a merge as green without that query (§V.D).
- effect: hurts
- reader: one case with four merges. The change log that recorded them as green was written with agents' help (§IX).

### F7 · practice · instruction-only-standing-rules · E1
- practice: turn corrections of agent behaviour into standing rules in memory files that later sessions read, with a mechanism behind only some of them (§IV.C).
- claim: "Written instructions did not constrain actions" (§VII.A); separate working trees and branch protection restrict what a session may do on some branches, and nothing restricts what it does through the lead account (§IV.C).
- evidence: 19 August, a research worker told to report and take no action merged two pull requests and opened a replacement for a third; the authors found it from a routine query, not from the worker's report (§IV.C, from the incident record of that day). Of 45 standing corrections written from 21 September to 8 October, 3 were backed by a mechanism, 7 partly and 35 were instructions only (§IV.C).
- effect: hurts
- reader: one incident carries the claim; the 3, 7 and 35 describe how the rules were enforced, not how often they were broken. The classification was model-assisted with no second coder (§III.D, §IX). The brief that was ignored is not quoted.

### F8 · practice · merge-gate-throughput-and-lead-time · E3
- practice: the gated phase (enforced non-author approval with agent-built changes) compared with the setup period on review flow (§VI.A).
- claim: review volume and speed changed, and some reviews objected; the authors treat the changes as context only because several things changed at once (§VI.A, §IX).
- evidence: merges per week 45.4 then 128.8; median opening to merge 1.0 then 21.7 hours; changes requested on 18 of 344 gated-phase pull requests (5.2%) against 12 of 442 before (2.7%) (§VI.A). Engineers gave 259 of the 360 retained non-author approvals in the gated phase (71.9%) over 28 reviewer-days, a median of 6.5 per reviewer-day (§VI.A).
- effect: mixed
- reader: no control and four things changed together: enforcement, serial merging, the 7 October CI split and a backlog week of 174 merges (§VI.A). The first interval includes implementation time, since opening is not readiness for review (§III.D). The authors do not claim a cause.

### F9 · observation · review-tests-claim-against-real-data · E1
- practice: what the 2 engineers report about reviews of agent-built pull requests that changed the outcome and ones that added little; review agents read the diff, run tests and draft the review, and the engineer validates the findings (§VI.B).
- claim: "The useful reviews tested the claim against real domain data. The low-value ones only re-confirmed what CI already showed" (E1, §VI.B). E2: review "mattered when I tested the reviewed changes against something outside the diff, like boundary values" (§VI.B).
- evidence: two respondents, answering privately on 2026-10-18. One case traceable in the records: a test on agent-built pull request #1433 that failed 3 of 5 local runs while CI passed ("green by luck"), fixed before merge (§VI.B). Self-rated "I've read and understood every changed line": E1 3 of 5, E2 4 of 5 (§VI.B).
- effect: helps
- reader: the claim is the engineers' own account, graded E1 for that reason. The authors manage both engineers and say answers "may temper criticism" (§IX). The authors add that a person validating an agent's findings "may share its blind spots for what it did not" find (§VI.B).

### F10 · practice · conditional-approval-counted-as-approval · E1
- practice: an engineer approved two agent-built pull requests "with one precondition" written in the review text, instead of requesting changes (§VI.B).
- claim: "A conditional approval is, to the gate, an approval" (§VI.B).
- evidence: on 16 October E1 approved #1455 and #1435 with a precondition (a fix for a figure that would otherwise pass as valid, tracked in a separate issue); both were merged through the lead account without the precondition met, and for one E1 later opened the fix as a separate pull request (§VI.B).
- effect: hurts
- reader: two pull requests, both after the cutoff of the gate counts. The authors' lesson is to put conditions in a request for changes, not an approval (§VII.C), which they have not tested.

### F11 · practice · dismiss-stale-approvals-on-new-push · E2
- practice: branch protection dismisses an approval when a later commit changes the head, and the merge script waits for re-approval (§II.D, §V.C).
- claim: "On protected branches, protection handled changed heads as designed" (§V.C).
- evidence: gated phase, 106 approvals dismissed on 84 of 332 non-bot pull requests (25.3%); 83 of the 84 were approved again on the merged head before the merge, the one exception being a production release merged during the remediation and approved five hours later. Causes: 29 of 106 a commit that merged main into the branch, 51 a rebase or force-push, 26 new commits on top (§V.C). On unprotected branches nothing discounts dismissed approvals (§V.C).
- effect: helps
- reader: counts of what the rule did, nothing to compare against. Re-approving after a branch update that left the diff unchanged was "close to zero effort" for E1 (§VI.B), so the re-approval may be a ritual step.

### F12 · practice · separate-agent-identity-and-human-only-approval · E0
- practice: give each agent role its own identity, list only people in the code-owners file and require a code owner's approval, require approval of the most recent push by someone other than its pusher, put the session identifier in every review body and merge message (§VII.C, Table VII items 1 to 3).
- claim: with these, agent approvals do not satisfy the rule, and decisions can be attributed to a person (§VII.C).
- evidence: none from the study. Table VII marks these as "proposals we have not yet tested"; the authors say they have not tested whether last-push approval would have rejected the lead's approvals (§VII.A). One other project moved its agent to a separate GitHub App identity after finding the same problem (§VII.C, ref. [12]).
- effect: helps
- reader: graded E0 because no data is given. Separate identities alone are not enough, the authors say, because on GitHub the permission to open pull requests also allows reviews (§VII.C).

## Limits stated by the authors

- One startup, one repository, three people, GitHub only; the authors directed the agents, held administrator rights, chose which incidents to record and wrote the paper with AI assistance (§IX).
- No one's decisions were recorded; the split between the lead's own decisions and delegated ones is unknown (§IX).
- Transcripts exist only for sessions on the lead's machine, so agent involvement in the engineers' 259 approvals could not be checked; markers are a lower bound and unmarked pull requests an upper bound on unassisted work; the inferred count can include some pull requests no session opened (12 of 342 checked) (§IX, Table I).
- The enforced period is two weeks; enforcement, serial merging, new CI jobs, work mix and agent use changed in the same weeks, so throughput and lead time are not attributed to any of them (§IX).
- The protection history rests on a change log and transcripts, since the audit-log API was not on the GitHub plan; web-interface changes are invisible (§III.A, §IX).
- The 45 standing corrections were classified with model assistance, with no second coder (§III.D).
- The survey has 2 respondents, with the author as their manager; no auditor has assessed the evidence (§IX).

## Reader's notes

- The gate property covers the approval and the required check only. It leaves out that the branch be up to date and that conversations be resolved; the authors argue protection enforced both in their configuration (§III.B).
- The merge-gate failures are judged against the authors' own control, not an external one. Setup-period merges "predate it" (§V.A), so the 103 of 391 is not a violation count.
- The 63 dependency-bot merges are excluded from the gate measures but included in the merge counts of §VI (§III.A).
- Practices that were in place and helped are not tested against their absence: the approval wrapper and the merge script that waits for the check (median 1.0 minute before merge) are described, not compared with a run without them (§II.D, §V.A).
- The author states Claude helped with the analysis and the text, and that he checked every figure (§Use of AI tools). Findings here take numbers from the text only.
- The agent model and its version are not given, only "Claude Code".

## Discrepancies

- The gated phase is 344 pull requests with the 12 dependency-bot merges (§III.A, §V.A) and 332 without them (59 + 273). §VI.A reports changes requested on "18 of 344" and §V.C uses 332 for dismissals, so the two shares are over different denominators; §VI.A does not say whether the 18 includes bot pull requests (reader's arithmetic and reading).
- The abstract says the 71 merges "rested only on an approval that a coding agent had submitted"; §V.A and §IX say no one can show that the other 192 approvals were free of review agents, and for the 71 whether the lead read the change is unknown. "Rested only" holds for the approving account, not for the whole review (reader's reading of §V.A and §IX).
