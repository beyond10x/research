## Question

What happens when Spec-Driven Development with an AI agent (four phases: investigation, planning,
implementation, review) is introduced into a student team-project course: how much students use AI
in each phase, whether the lines of code they add change, and whether their understanding of their
own code relates to their AI use (§1).

## Findings

### F1 · practice · stage-gated-spec-workflow · E3
- practice: in the 2025 course, teach a four-phase workflow (investigation, planning, implementation, review) in which Copilot agent mode writes investigation reports, `tasks.md` and `specs.md` and the code, students are told to review and understand each artifact, a failed review returns the work to investigation, and AI agents are recommended; compared with the 2022 to 2024 cohorts of the same course, in which "active use of generative AI was not recommended" until 2024 and not prohibited (§3.2, §4.2).
- claim: added lines of code per student per lecture rose, "suggesting an increase in students' implementation throughput ... that corresponds temporally with the proliferation of LLM technologies" (§4.2).
- evidence: students (teams) 38 (10) in 2022, 28 (7) in 2023, 25 (7) in 2024, 14 (4) in 2025 (§4.2). Steel-Dwass test over 8 lectures (lec06 to lec13): significant differences between years in 7, not in lec11 (p = 0.1608). Minimum pairwise p per lecture, Table 1: lec06 0.0077 (22 < 24), lec07 0.0052, lec08 0.0292, lec09 0.0237 (22 < 23), lec10 0.0021, lec12 0.0260, lec13 0.0063 (23 < 24). No case of 2024 or 2025 below 2022 or 2023 (§4.2). Box plots in Figure 5, values in the figure only.
- effect: helps
- reader: E3, a comparison with earlier cohorts and no concurrent control; the authors say so (§5.2). The treatment bundles the agent, the spec workflow, the template and the recommendation to use AI, so the effect of the spec workflow alone is not separable. Table 1 pairs show 2022 below 2023 in lec07, lec09 and lec10, and below 2024 in 5 lectures, years in which AI use was not recommended, so part of the rise predates the 2025 treatment. The metric is added lines of code, with AI-generated Markdown removed by hand; it is not quality or correctness. Only 4 teams in 2025.

### F2 · practice · weekly-one-on-one-comprehension-check · E1
- practice: from lec07 on, every week each student explains in a one-on-one interview the code they committed, and the instructor scores comprehension 0 to 3; students are told they must be able to explain any AI-generated code (§3.2, §3.3, §4.3).
- claim: "regular verification of code comprehension by instructors and appropriate feedback are essential for maintaining educational effectiveness" (abstract); a drop in comprehension in lec09 was followed by "a measurable recovery in lec10" after instructor support (§5.1).
- evidence: 3 of the 4 teams (25A, 25B, 25D) dropped in comprehension in lec09, when their implementation-phase AI use became very high, and most teams improved in lec08 and lec10; in lec11 and lec12 most teams scored high "regardless of AI usage" (§4.3). No score values in the text; Figure 6.
- effect: helps
- reader: E1, one course and one pattern across 2 lectures read off a figure; no course ran without the interviews, so the claim that they cause the recovery is not tested. The same instructors score and intervene, and the scale is a subjective judgement (§5.2). The authors give a second reading of the same data: the interviews may be why no AI-versus-comprehension correlation appeared (§5.1).

### F3 · observation · ai-use-and-code-comprehension · E3
- practice: nothing assigned; students' self-reported AI use level in the implementation phase (0 to 4) is compared with the instructor-scored comprehension level (0 to 3) of the same students, by period (Early lec07 and lec08, Mid lec09 and lec10, Late lec11 and lec12) and by team (§3.3, §4.3).
- claim: students who implemented without AI "maintained consistently high code comprehension", and students at AI level 3 "implemented code with very little comprehension", especially in lec09 and lec10; across all students "no clear statistical trend was confirmed" (§4.3, §5.1).
- evidence: level 1 had no respondents in the implementation phase; Team 25C, the lowest AI user, had consistently high comprehension in all periods (§4.3). The standard error of the mean was "relatively large across all levels" (§5.1). The conclusion says the trend of lower-comprehension students relying more on AI "was not statistically significant" (§6). Means only in Figure 6; no test statistic given.
- effect: mixed
- reader: the level-0 and level-3 contrast is visible in the figure, and the whole-cohort trend is reported as not significant. Levels are self-reported perceived helpfulness, not amount of use (§3.3), so "level 0" means no use but levels 2 to 4 mix use and perceived value. Cells are small (14 students, 4 teams) and not tested.

### F4 · observation · ai-use-by-development-phase · E2
- practice: nothing assigned; students rate weekly, per phase, how much AI they used and how helpful it was (0 to 4); results are aggregated by team (§3.3, §4.1, Figure 4).
- claim: all teams used AI to some extent in implementation and debugging, "in the Review phase, all teams did not use AI much", and in investigation and planning use "differ greatly by team and by student" (§4.1).
- evidence: Teams 25A and 25B used AI throughout; 25C used it least; 25D hardly used it in investigation and planning and used it effectively in implementation and debugging (§4.1). Values in Figure 4 only.
- effect: mixed
- reader: described, not compared; no values in the text. Student self-report of perceived effectiveness. The review phase is the one the workflow added so that agents and students both check the result against the specification (§3.2), and it is the phase students used AI in least; the paper does not say why. The `effect` value is a placeholder for a result with no direction.

### F5 · practice · project-template-with-instruction-file · E0
- practice: give every team a template project that holds `.github/copilot-instructions.md` (workflow and project rules), a `docs/` tree for `specs.md`, `tasks.md` and the investigation, done and review reports, and a specification template, after a 30-minute lecture and a one-hour demonstration of one full cycle by two instructors (§3.2, §5.1).
- claim: the templates "appeared to help students smoothly initiate their development tasks using AI agents", may "reduce cognitive load", and serve as examples of how to write instructions for agents (§5.1).
- evidence: none given. The statement rests on the instructors' observation; no student data on template use is reported.
- effect: helps
- reader: E0, an impression by the course's authors without a measurement and without a course run without the template.

### F6 · practice · foundation-first-before-agent-use · E0
- practice: run the first 5 of 14 lectures as Spring Boot tutorials with reference sample applications, before teams start the project and the agent workflow in lecture 6 (§3.1, §5.1).
- claim: "This foundation appears to be essential for building basic skills before students begin to utilize AI tools more extensively" (§5.1).
- evidence: none given. The course had no variant without the tutorial period.
- effect: helps
- reader: E0. The survey covers AI use in lectures 1 to 5 as a baseline (§3.3), so students did use AI during the tutorials; the practice is a sequence of teaching, not a ban on agents. The paper offers no comparison.

## Limits stated by the authors

- No concurrent control group abstaining from AI agents; year-to-year differences in student skills, cohort size or the environment may confound the LOC change (§5.2).
- Added LOC does not capture design quality, refactoring or removal of redundant code (§5.2).
- Comprehension scores rest on instructors' subjective judgement (§5.2).
- One course, Java and Spring Boot, only four teams in 2025; not generalisable to all contexts (§5.2).
- Small sample size and large SEM limited the power to detect a trend between AI use and comprehension; timely instructor interventions may have influenced the result (§5.1).

## Reader's notes

- No outcome of the spec-driven part is measured on its own. The three measures (self-reported AI use, added LOC, comprehension) are about AI agent use in general; the survey asks about AI "regardless of the platform" (§3.3), so use of chat services is included.
- No spec-quality, test, defect or review-outcome data is reported. Nothing in the text shows whether `specs.md`, `tasks.md` or the review reports were correct or were used by the agents in the way the workflow intends.
- The 2025 cohort is 14 students; the baseline cohorts total 91 students (38 + 28 + 25, reader's arithmetic). The cohorts also differ in the 2025 SDD lecture, template and recommendation, and the authors count AI-generated Markdown out of LOC by hand, so the counting rule is applied by people who know the year.
- Lines above 1000 are treated as extreme and not drawn in Figure 5 (note to Figure 5); whether they were kept in the test is not stated.
- The model behind Copilot agent mode is not stated.

## Discrepancies

- The abstract says AI agent use "tended to encourage students to proceed with development without fully understanding the code", and §5.1 and §6 say no significant or clear trend between AI use and comprehension was found.
- The conclusion says students in 2024 to 2025 added more lines than students in 2022 to 2023. Table 1 shows 2022 below 2023 in lec07, lec09 and lec10, so 2022 and 2023 differ from each other, and 2025 is in a significant pair in 3 of 8 lectures only (lec07, lec08, lec10); 2024 against 2025 is not reported.
- The conclusion says students "actively utilized AI agents across all development phases"; §4.1 says all teams used AI little in review, and team 25C used it less and 25D hardly in investigation and planning.
- The abstract says the study "demonstrates" that regular verification and feedback are essential; §5.1 and §6 say it "may have" played a role and "appear to be important", and no course ran without them.
