## Question

When the error text an MCP tool returns tells the caller to do something the agent cannot do (run a
command, change a setting, open a page, wait), how much does that lower the agent's recovery, and do
naming a server tool in the step or removing the step before the model reads it restore it
(Abstract, §1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · mcp-error-text-written-for-developers · E2
- practice: the error messages in the source of the 150 most-starred public MCP servers were labelled by OpenAI Codex agents for whether they state a next step and whether the step depends on something the server cannot see about the caller (§3).
- claim: much MCP error text is written for a developer who can act outside the call; "half" of the next steps depend on the caller, and on credential errors "nearly every step" asks for a terminal command, a configuration change or a web page (§1, §3).
- evidence: 3,001 messages, 949 with a next step, 477 of those depend on the caller (Table 1). Credential, permission and rate-limit messages: 209, of which 128 have a step and 99 depend on the caller; 93 of the 99 ask for a configuration change (55), a web page (24), a terminal command (13) or waiting (1) (§3). Credential errors: 62 of 67 steps ask for a configuration change, a web page or a terminal command; in 12 of them, from 5 servers, the server offers a tool that could make the repair (§3). Rate limits: 20 of 30 steps say to wait and retry, none names the call to repeat (§3).
- effect: none
- reader: E2, counts with nothing to compare against. The finding is a prevalence and no direction was measured; `none` is the closest value in the closed set (a case for the proposed `describes`). The labels are an LLM's, from source code, with no agreement check against a human stated in the parts read. The 150 servers are those with the most stars that match a filter; no sampling claim beyond that.

### F2 · practice · caller-unavailable-step-in-error-text · E4
- practice: append a next step the agent cannot carry out (a terminal command, "Please run: reddit-mcp-buddy --auth") to the cause statement of an expired-credentials error, against the cause statement alone, on the same 24 scenarios and 3 runs per scenario (§4.2, §5.1).
- claim: the command lowers recovery, and "55% of trials ended without a repair" although the agent's own login tool could make the repair (§5.1).
- evidence: recovery averaged over five models, cause with command against cause alone: 45% [35, 54] against 82% [70, 92]; tool calls 2.22 against 3.25; trials ended without a repair 55% against 18% (Table 4). Per model, command against cause alone: GPT-5.5 58 against 76, GPT-5.6 Sol 57 against 92, GPT-6 Sol 46 against 85, GPT-6 Astra 6 against 75, GPT-6 Luna 57 against 83 (Table 4). The same command in Table 6 (expired credentials, unavailable incorrect step) gives 45 against 61 for the generic notice. In 48 of GPT-6 Astra's 68 trials without a repair, its final message left the repair to the user although the login tool was in its tool list (§5.1).
- effect: hurts
- reader: a within-scenario comparison of assigned texts on equal tasks, so E4. The tasks are synthetic replays of BFCL calls with a one-turn state check, and all five models are from one vendor. The inserted step is the authors' wording, taken from one real server.

### F3 · observation · stop-on-unavailable-step-grows-with-model-generation · E3
- practice: none applied; the loss in recovery caused by the F2 command is compared across the five models (§5.1).
- claim: "the more capable the model, the more often it stopped"; the newest, largest model stopped in almost every expired-credential trial, which the authors call literal compliance and link to what OpenAI and Anthropic say of their newest models (§1, §6).
- evidence: loss from the command, in points of recovery: GPT-5.5 18, GPT-5.6 Sol 35, GPT-6 Sol 39, GPT-6 Astra 69, GPT-6 Luna (smallest) 26 (§5.1). GPT-6 Astra against GPT-5.5: 51 points more loss, interval 29 to 72 (§5.1). GPT-6 Astra under the command: recovery 6% [0, 13], 0.60 tool calls, 94% ended without a repair (Table 4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: a comparison across models, not a control: models differ in more than capability, and "capable" is the authors' ordering by release and size, not a measured one. The losses are not monotone in that ordering (Luna 26 sits between GPT-5.5 and GPT-5.6 Sol). Each model is one point with 24 scenarios; only the Astra against GPT-5.5 difference has an interval. The explanation rests on vendor blog posts (§6) and the authors do not test it.

### F4 · practice · error-step-names-server-tool · E4
- practice: on expired credentials, replace the terminal command with a step that names the service's login tool ("Call ticket_login first.") (§4.2).
- claim: naming the login tool "raised recovery on expired credentials to 84%" (Abstract).
- evidence: recovery averaged over five models, login tool against terminal command: 84% [72, 93] against 45% [35, 54] (Table 4). Against the cause alone, 82% [70, 92], it is "about the same" (§5.1). Tool calls 2.89 against 3.25 for the cause alone, and tokens 13,643 against 14,860 (Table 4). The other correct phrasing, "Please re-authorize to continue." with no tool named, also gives 84% (Table 6).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 against the command, and the gain is the removal of the harmful step, not the tool name: the cause alone and the vague correct step reach the same level (Table 4, Table 6). The authors write the rewritten step and measure it themselves.

### F5 · practice · error-step-names-server-tool · E4
- practice: on a rate limit, replace "Wait before retrying." with a step that names the failed call to repeat ("Wait a few seconds and call place_order again") (§4.2).
- claim: naming the call raised recovery "from 6% to 88%, at about one extra tool call" (§1).
- evidence: recovery averaged over five models, 88% [83, 93] against 6% [1, 13], a difference of 82 points (interval 75 to 88); per model 78 to 99% against 1 to 11% (Table 5, §5.2). Tool calls 1.42 against 0.37, tokens 5,409 against 3,098; ended without repair 12% against 94% (Table 5). Under "Wait before retrying." between 89 and 99% of trials ended without a repair (§5.2). An executable but wrong step (a different server tool) gave 66% (Table 6).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4. The comparison is between two correct steps on equal scenarios. The environment accepts the same call again at once and enforces no wait (Table 2), so the experiment cannot show what happens when a real server still refuses the retry. The 66% under the wrong-tool step is the authors' evidence that "the essential part is the instruction to call again" (§6).

### F6 · practice · error-step-names-server-tool · E4
- practice: on a missing permission (the session is logged in but one tool is rejected until a login to the same service succeeds), name the login tool in the step, against a vague step and against the cause alone (§4.2, §5.4).
- claim: "naming the login tool raised recovery on a missing permission to 53%, against 28% for 'Please re-authorize to continue.'" (§5.4).
- evidence: recovery averaged over five models: generic notice 0, cause alone 0, vague correct step 28, login-tool step 53, executable incorrect step 0, unavailable incorrect step 0 (Table 6). Agents given only the cause ended the turn without a repair in at least 89% of trials, on a missing permission and a rate limit (§5.4). No interval is given for Table 6.
- effect: helps
- reader: E4, with no intervals or per-model values in the text for this row. Even the best text leaves 47% unrecovered. This is the one failure type where the tool name beats a vague step.

### F7 · practice · strip-next-step-from-tool-error-before-model · E4
- practice: before the model reads a tool error, run a one-sentence prompt on a small model (GPT-6 Luna) that removes every sentence telling the caller what to do next and keeps the sentences that say what went wrong; applied to the four texts with a step of each expired-credential scenario (§4.3).
- claim: deleting the step "raised recovery on expired credentials to 82%" (Abstract) at no measurable cost where the removed step was correct (§5.3, §6).
- evidence: GPT-6 Luna reduced all 96 texts to exactly the cause statement (§5.3). On the text with the terminal command, recovery rose 37.5 points (interval 26.9 to 47.8) to 82% [70, 93], equal to the cause alone (Table 4, §5.3). Where the removed step had been correct, recovery changed by -1.7 to -0.8 points, every interval including zero (§5.3). On the 764 survey messages where the step can be separated from the cause, the prompt removed the step from 752 and left the rest word for word in 548; running it on all 949 messages with a step cost 0.09 US dollars (§5.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 for expired credentials, 24 scenarios, 1,440 trials. Not shown to help on other failure types: the authors state that on a missing permission or a rate limit the cause alone leaves most tasks unrecovered, so the filter would remove the repair (§6, Table 6). The 96 texts are built so that the cause alone already points to the repair, which favours the filter. No check of the filter on real server messages beyond whether the step was removed.

### F8 · practice · cause-only-error-text · E4
- practice: return only the cause of the failure (what the tool can see when the call fails), against a generic notice ("Operation failed.") (§4.2).
- claim: the paper does not claim this directly; the contrast appears in Table 6, and the text says that on a missing permission and a rate limit agents given only the cause stopped (§5.4, §6).
- evidence: recovery averaged over five models, generic against cause alone (Table 6): expired credentials 61 against 82, rate limit 59 against 5, missing permission 0 against 0.
- effect: mixed
- reader: E4 by design (same scenarios, assigned texts), no intervals in the table. The rate-limit result is the reverse of the credential one: with only "Operation failed." agents retried in 59% of trials, and a cause naming the rate limit made them stop. The authors read the stop as correct HTTP behaviour for a 429 (§6) and do not explain the generic-notice retries.

### F9 · practice · error-text-for-failure-visible-in-own-call · E4
- practice: vary the error text (generic, cause, two correct steps, two incorrect steps) when the failure lies in the agent's own call: a wrong unit or format, a missing required field, a wrong tool, a missing resource (§5.4).
- claim: "recovery under every text stayed within four points of that under the generic notice" (§5.4).
- evidence: Table 6, recovery by text from generic to unavailable incorrect step: wrong unit or format 83, 79, 82, 82, 81, 81; missing required field 86, 88, 88, 88, 87, 86; wrong tool 80, 81, 82, 81, 81, 81; missing resource 98, 98, 97, 97, 99, 98.
- effect: none
- reader: E4 by design. The authors' reading is that a malformed call shows the agent what to change whatever the message says (§6); the scenarios are replays of tasks the models are scored on, so ceiling effects (98% on missing resource) limit what the table can show.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Recovery is judged by the BFCL state check for the turn in which the failure occurs (§7).
- The scenarios cover seven failure types across the BFCL multi-turn domains (§7).
- The survey covers widely used open-source MCP servers on GitHub in September 2026 (§7).
- Model names are API aliases and the paper reports access dates (§7).

## Reader's notes

- All five models are OpenAI models; no other vendor is run. The claim in the title about "the most capable agents" rests on one model (GPT-6 Astra) and on vendor descriptions of newer models quoted in §6.
- The survey labels were assigned by LLM agents (Codex) from source, and the codebook is released; no human audit or agreement figure appears in the parts read.
- The six texts and the BFCL environment were built by the authors, and the recovery check is state-based. The environment accepts an immediate retry on a rate limit and accepts a login with the earlier credentials (Table 2), so the scenarios reward the repair the authors' rewritten step names.
- The paper tests agents whose only actions are the task's tools. A coding agent with a shell, which §1 says can run commands and wait, is not run, so the result does not say how a terminal-capable agent responds to the same steps.
- The intervals come from bootstrap over 24 scenarios within each failure type (§4.4); the three samples per scenario are not independent of the scenario, and Table 6 reports no intervals.
- The paper reports no test on the explanation of literal compliance and no run on other prompts, temperatures or reasoning efforts (all runs use high reasoning effort and default sampling, §4.3).
- The survey is a count over text and not a measure of how often agents hit these errors in deployment.
- The scenarios and data are public (Data availability), so the numbers can be recomputed.

## Discrepancies

- The abstract says naming a server tool in the step "raised recovery on expired credentials to 84%", as a remedy. In Table 6 the correct step with no tool named ("Please re-authorize to continue.") also gives 84%, and the cause alone gives 82% (Table 4). The 84% is higher than the command (45%) and not higher than the vague step.
- §1 says "the more capable the model, the more often it stopped". The loss for GPT-6 Luna, the smallest model, is 26 points, larger than the 18 of GPT-5.5 (§5.1). The ordering is by release and size and is not monotone.
- The abstract states a rate-limit remedy as "naming a server tool in the step"; §4.2 and Table 5 name "the failed call" to repeat, and Table 6 shows an executable step that names a wrong tool also recovers 66% (§6).
