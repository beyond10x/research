## Question

How do coding agents that author pull requests change code review, and through which causal
mechanisms? The paper builds a theory of 26 constructs and 67 relationships from practitioner
writing, after a repository study whose trends changed direction with the analysis choice (§I, §II-B).

## Findings

### F1 · practice · review-governance-policy · E0
- practice: a review governance policy that mandates a careful pass, sets a checklist, or keeps accountability with people ("If your team bans 'the AI did it' as an excuse"), set by an organisation for review of agent-written code (§IV, P16).
- claim: such a policy raises review depth and so review effectiveness, and lowers review efficiency (P16).
- evidence: practitioner quotes only, for example G1872 "review quality improves because authors know they own the output" and G2527 "spends 45 minutes reviewing it". No count, no measurement (§IV). The paper calls the theory "proposed" and "not validated" (§IV).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E0 because the claim rests on what practitioners write, not on a measured policy. Better on effectiveness, worse on efficiency by the claim's own terms. The sources are open to vendor advocacy and hindsight, which the authors state (§III-A1).

### F2 · practice · risk-tiered-human-review-gating · E0
- practice: gate only material or sensitive changes for human sign-off and send routine ones through automated validation, against a blanket policy that gates every change (§IV, P17).
- claim: the effect of governance on review latency is contested and its sign is set by calibration: lower latency under the risk-tiered policy, higher under the blanket one (P17).
- evidence: quotes G2335 "only material changes require human sign-off; routine changes flow through automated validation", G3058 "The goal isn't to put human eyes on all the code", G2175 "Initially, yes. Enforcement and review add process" (§IV). No measurement.
- effect: mixed
- reader: asserted by practitioners. The motivating study (F9, F11) shows agent PRs that are reviewed unevenly by type, which the authors read as triage, but it does not test a policy.

### F3 · practice · automated-review-in-place-of-human-review · E0
- practice: fully automate code review with an AI reviewer, so that human review is bypassed (§IV, P8, P9).
- claim: full automation increases overall review throughput and decreases review latency (P8); its effect on code quality and code security is contested (P9).
- evidence: practitioner quotes for both sides: "CodeRabbit catches approximately 82% of planted bugs with a 15% false positive rate" (G2836) against "Automated review catches style issues. It misses the 'this will cause a race condition under load' kind of problems" (G1957) (§IV). The 82% and 15% are quoted from a source document, not measured by the authors.
- effect: mixed
- reader: the one number is second-hand from a vendor-related source. Throughput and latency gains are asserted, quality and security are left open by the authors themselves.

### F4 · practice · ai-assisted-human-review · E0
- practice: use AI to assist a human reviewer, for example by clearing line-level checks so the reviewer audits intent and architecture (§IV, P10, P11).
- claim: AI assistance raises reviewer capability and so review efficiency and effectiveness (P10); but a focus on high-level intent raises comprehension debt even when review depth is high (P11).
- evidence: quotes such as "When agents produce thousands of lines per hour, line-by-line review does not scale" (G2869) and "We are not reviewing the recipe. We are tasting the dish" (G2392). No measurement.
- effect: mixed
- reader: asserted; the paper presents both a benefit and a cost for the same practice and gives no size for either.

### F5 · observation · review-load-from-agent-code-volume · E0
- practice: none applied. Practitioners describe agents producing code faster than people can review it (§IV, P1 to P3).
- claim: higher review load decreases review depth and so efficiency and effectiveness (P1); decreases review motivation (P2); and raises the relative time spent reviewing, which lowers motivation (P3).
- evidence: quotes: "increase code generation speed by 10 to 25 times without proportionally increasing review capacity" (G2394), "if that engineer is reviewing 200 AI-generated PRs a sprint ... they're skimming, not reading" (G2048). No measurement.
- effect: hurts
- reader: the volume premise is the most repeated claim in the corpus, but the paper itself reports that repository studies disagree on whether agent PRs are larger or smaller (§V). The merge-time data in F11 does not test load.

### F6 · observation · surface-plausibility-and-skepticism-in-review · E0
- practice: none applied. Practitioners describe agent code as polished and idiomatic, and some reviewers as treating it with deliberate suspicion (§IV, P4 to P6).
- claim: surface plausibility decreases review depth and effectiveness (P4); skepticism toward AI code increases review depth and effectiveness and decreases efficiency (P5); skepticism can offset plausibility and is raised by seeing run-to-run output inconsistency (P6).
- evidence: quotes: "polish can lower the reviewer's guard" (G2222), "When AI submits polished code, reviewers pattern-match against 'looks professional' and approve faster" (G2613), "I read every line like it's a puzzle" (G3043). No measurement.
- effect: mixed
- reader: plausibility hurts and skepticism helps by the claim; the sign depends on the reviewer's disposition, which the authors name as a moderator (§VII). Faster approval is read as less scrutiny in the quotes; the motivating study cannot separate that from confidence.

### F7 · observation · code-opacity-in-agent-code-review · E0
- practice: none applied. Agent code arrives without the design rationale a human author would hold (§IV, P7).
- claim: opacity decreases review efficiency, review effectiveness and review motivation (P7).
- evidence: quotes: "One reason AI-generated code is harder to review is that you're reconstructing intent" (G2066), "You can't meaningfully review code you didn't write and don't understand" (G2413). No measurement.
- effect: hurts
- reader: asserted. The three channels (efficiency, effectiveness, motivation) are the authors' decomposition of the quotes, which they say they imposed by reading in context (§VI).

### F8 · observation · comprehension-debt-and-reviewer-skill-loop · E0
- practice: none applied. Shallow review of agent code is said to leave nobody with a working model of the code (§IV, P11 to P15).
- claim: lower review depth limits growth in reviewer skill (P12); less time coding plus low-quality reviews lower review skill (P13); depth builds collective ownership and knowledge transfer and automated review undermines them (P14); low depth and opacity raise comprehension debt, which hurts maintainability, ownership, knowledge transfer and review skill, forming negative feedback loops (P15).
- evidence: quotes: "If it is not understood, it is not owned" (G2161), "juniors who skip the 'read the diff carefully' step never build the mental model" (G2810), "skill atrophy from reduced practice writing code manually" (G2501). No measurement.
- effect: hurts
- reader: the loops are the paper's central new claim and are the least tested part: no delay, size or time scale is given. The three comprehension-debt sources it cites are blog posts (refs 15 to 17).

### F9 · observation · human-review-of-agent-pr · E3
- practice: none applied. Merged agent-associated PRs in 2,860 sampled repositories that also contain human PRs, with and without any human review, by month (§II-B, Appendix 1).
- claim: the share of merged agent PRs with no human review falls from over 50% in mid-2025 toward the human baseline of about 14% by early 2026 (§II-B).
- evidence: overall agentic no-review rate over 50% early in the window to about 12% by 2026-02 against a stable human rate of about 14% (Appendix 1, Fig. 1); repositories that merged all agent PRs with no review: 34% in 2025-05, 13% in 2026-02 (Fig. 3); by PR type, agentic no-review test 69%, refactor 41%, bug fix 25%, human feature 8% to CI 14% (Appendix 1). Agent n=8,662 and human n=179,066 merged PRs in the per-project means (Fig. 5).
- effect: helps
- reader: E3, because agent and human PRs are compared with no control and no randomisation. The comparison is within repositories that use both. "Agent" means an account classed by rules (branch names, logins), which can miss agents and mislabel bots (Appendix 1). Public GitHub only, no non-adopting control repositories.

### F10 · observation · author-only-review-of-agent-pr · E3
- practice: none applied. Who takes part in the review thread of a merged PR, by author group (§II-B, Appendix 1).
- claim: review of agent PRs is "less independent and involves less between-human collaboration" (§II-B takeaway).
- evidence: only the PR author in the thread, 40.1% of agentic against 21.5% of human PRs; a single other reviewer 4.6% against 18.7%; multiple people 20.8% against 29.3% (Appendix 1, Fig. 5). Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test per project: k = 1,553, V = 418,564, p < 0.001, r = 0.42. Per-project mean no-review 34.5% agentic against 30.5% human (Fig. 5).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3, within-repository pairing but no control. "Author-only" counts the person who invoked the agent as the author, which F12 shows is a choice. The per-project mean no-review rates (34.5% against 30.5%) differ little, so the "less independent" claim rests on who takes part, not on whether review happens.

### F11 · observation · agent-pr-time-to-merge-and-review-discussion · E3
- practice: none applied. Median time to merge and the volume of review comments, agent against human PRs (§II-B, Appendix 1).
- claim: agent PRs "move through review substantially faster, with less discussion per line changed, though the speed of agentic PR review is slowing down" (takeaway).
- evidence: median time to merge is lower for agent PRs in every month; agent median about 20 minutes in 2025-05 to 2 to 3 hours by early 2026, human median about 5 to 8 hours, so the relative gap goes from more than tenfold to roughly threefold (Fig. 4). No review comments at all: 50.2% of agentic against 37.9% of human PRs; more than 20 comments per thousand modified lines: 27.8% against 34.5%.
- effect: mixed
- reader: faster merge is better on latency and thinner discussion is worse on review depth, if depth is read from comments, which the paper says its traces cannot show (Appendix 1 limits). Task type and PR size are not controlled; agent PRs are likely different kinds of changes.

### F12 · observation · operationalisation-sensitivity-of-agent-review-metrics · E3
- practice: none applied. The same data summarised in two defensible ways (§II-B).
- claim: "the direction of these trends flips under different but equally defensible analysis choices", so trends "establish what is changing, not why" (abstract, §II-B).
- evidence: per-project mean over all merged PRs (Fig. 5) gives about a quarter of agent PRs with any human beyond the author; conditioning on reviewed PRs and binning per-project-month rates (Fig. 6) gives roughly half of project-months with no author-only review. If the invoking human counts as an independent second reviewer, independent review is more common for agent PRs than for human PRs, and if the human counts as the author it "has sharply declined" (Appendix 1).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3 because two analyses of one dataset are compared. It supports a caution on reading review metrics of agent PRs, and it motivates the theory; it does not show that any review practice works.

### F13 · practice · llm-coded-grey-literature-theory-building · E2
- practice: build a theory from practitioner writing with an LLM pipeline for the mechanical steps (corpus collection, a relevance judge, open coding by three coder agents with neutral, critical and appreciative lenses, a codebook of 4,838 codes), and hand-made axial and selective coding by the authors (§III-A).
- claim: LLMs scale the coding to thousands of documents at about US$0.35 per document; automating the theory step "proved ineffective" (§VI).
- evidence: the relevance judge (Gemini 2.5 Flash) kept 13,469 of 23,631 candidates (57%) and agreed with a stronger model on 885 re-judged documents at Cohen's κ = 0.75, 87.9% raw agreement (Appendix 3). Coding of 3,100 documents gave 4,838 codes and 109,951 quotes from 2,669 documents (§III-A). A direct causal extraction from the codebook gave 15,029 statements that were "shallow, redundant, and overlapping" and was not used (§VI, Appendix 5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E2 because only the judge has an agreement figure; the coding step and the theory step are not scored against a human baseline, and the extraction comparison is an author judgement. The authors suspect 3,100 documents exceeded what was needed and cannot show where saturation occurred (§VI). This is a research-method finding, not about running coding agents.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Sources are written in the age of generative AI and may be partly LLM-generated; the paper studies discourse about review, not verified practice (§III-A1).
- LLM coding and filtering can miscode, miss or hallucinate; residual error remains (§III-A1).
- The move from codebook to theory is author judgement, and another team could draw other constructs (§III-A1).
- Grey literature over-represents vocal early adopters and carries vendor advocacy and post-incident hindsight (§III-A1).
- The theory is "proposed ... not validated"; none of the relationships is confirmed (§IV, §VI).
- Motivating study: account classification of "agent-authored" PRs can mislabel and miss agents; the review measures do not show depth, difficulty or off-platform review; observational; public GitHub only; no non-adopting baseline (Appendix 1).

## Reader's notes

- Every theory relationship is tagged quote-grounded, read-into or by-definition (Appendix 5), but the paper gives no count per tier in the parts read. Part of the 67 relationships is by definition or read into the text, not stated by practitioners.
- Practitioner quotes were picked to support each proposition; the paper reports no count of documents that support, oppose or ignore a given proposition, and only the 3 contested edges carry opposing text (§III-A, Appendix 5).
- No finding here compares a review practice with and without it on equal tasks. Whether a risk-tiered policy lowers latency or an AI reviewer lowers quality is left as a hypothesis by the authors.
- The motivating study is the only quantitative part. Its agent labels rest on branch-name and login rules (Appendix 1), and the sample was drawn from repositories that already contain agent PRs.
- Practitioners writing about review are mostly writing about agents that open PRs on a shared repository; the theory does not cover review inside a single-developer agent session.

## Discrepancies

- §III-A says all calls use Gemini 2.5 Flash for coding; Appendix 4 says "All coding-stage LLM calls use Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite" and the segmenter uses Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite. Only the relevance judge is Gemini 2.5 Flash in Appendix 3.
- The abstract says agent-authored PRs are "reviewed less often"; the per-project mean no-review rate in Fig. 5 is 34.5% agentic against 30.5% human, and the end-of-window overall rate of agent PRs (about 12%) is below the human rate of about 14% (Appendix 1). The difference holds only in the early months and in who reviews.
- §IV says the theory comes from "roughly 2,700 practitioner sources"; the codebook holds codes from 2,669 documents (§III-A, Appendix 4).
