## Question

Can an "AI Software Factory" of targeting, evolutionary coding, reviewing and ops agents, fed by a shared
data substrate (the World Model), speed up the whole software lifecycle for Data Systems and for
coding tasks with an automatically measurable objective, and can its traces be used to improve
the model (Abstract, §1)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · evolutionary-agent-swarm-vs-llm-evolution-baselines · E3
- practice: Darwin hill-climbs a measurable objective with "tens to hundreds of coding agents" under an evolutionary loop; compared on circle_packing with MAP-Elites and with OpenEvolve (§2.3, §4).
- claim: "Darwin cost 22× less than MAP-elites [13] and 17× less than [17]" on circle_packing (§4); "up to 22× better token efficiency (vs [17])" (§1, Abstract).
- evidence: one problem, circle_packing; the multipliers above and nothing else in the text. "We omit a more complete ablation study we performed due to space limitations" (footnote 2, §2.3). Final solution quality of the baselines is not reported in the text.
- effect: helps
- reader: baselines are other systems, not Darwin with a component removed, so no control; one problem, no n, no variance, and the ablation that would isolate the agentic variator is withheld. The authors build and evaluate Darwin.

### F2 · practice · evolutionary-agent-swarm-on-oss-systems · E2
- practice: run Darwin on 45 widely deployed open-source systems, each with a $2,500 inference budget at public API prices; keep the best-performing mutation and report its gain over several workloads (§4).
- claim: "positive (though sometimes modest) improvements across every system we targeted"; "for the best-performing 20 systems we obtain an average 13.8% performance gain" (§4).
- evidence: 45 systems, $2,500 each, 20 best average 13.8% (§4, Figure 4; the per-system speedups are in the figure only). Cost "roughly $100K of tokens" (§4). Annual savings of $230M to $616M if the gains were deployed on 10% of production instances of the top-20 projects, "even if our estimates are off by 10×-100×" (§4).
- effect: helps
- reader: nothing to compare against: no other method on the same 45 systems and no human baseline. The 13.8% is an average over the 20 best systems chosen after the run, so it is selected. Each system gets one best mutation chosen by Darwin's own score, with no held-out workload stated. The dollar savings are an extrapolation by the authors with a stated uncertainty of 10x to 100x, not a measurement.

### F3 · practice · evolutionary-agent-swarm-in-product-teams · E1
- practice: engineers at Microsoft apply Darwin to seven internal codebases; Table 1 lists code expertise, Darwin expertise, gains and hours invested (§4).
- claim: "Darwin delivered high ROI in nearly every scenario we applied it" (§4).
- evidence: Table 1, one row per case. Dataverse: 2× throughput and 4× memory reduction, 2-4 h. Fabric Data Agent: 10% accuracy boost, 3 h. Fabric Data Warehouse: 2.6× GPU kernel speedup, 4 h ("40+ software variations" in hours). Xbox: 2× code generation speed-up, 16 h. Copilot CLI: 9% token reduction and 12% reduced cost, 40 h. NL2DAX: 63% prompt size reduction and 7% accuracy boost, 4 h. Experimental LSM tree: 1600× speedup, 3 h.
- effect: helps
- reader: seven cases, called "Anecdotal Evidence" by the authors. The authors say the first observation "might be biased by our ability to instinctively pick problems that are a good fit for Darwin" (§4). No failures are listed, so the denominator of "nearly every scenario" is not given. No baseline for any gain, and the gain metrics differ per row.

### F4 · practice · production-continuous-optimization-pipeline · E3
- practice: a dedicated performance team runs Autoperf (a manually curated World Model, Hotspan with production telemetry and Azure Profiler channels, a scale-out Darwin swarm, and an automated adversarial reviewing mechanism) on tens of repositories; its output goes to engineering review queues (§4).
- claim: "0.35 PRs per engineer-hour ... estimated to be 4.7× more than manual development, and 3× higher than today's best agentic coding tools" (§4); "3× engineering efficiency above agentic coding" (Abstract).
- evidence: 0.35 PRs per engineer-hour, counted over the application work only; 43% of the team's time went to application scenarios and 57% to infrastructure building and repo onboarding (§4). Gains of the PRs: 1.72× median and 10× at the 90th percentile (§4, Figure 5). Implied rates: about 0.074 per hour for manual and 0.117 for agentic tools (reader's arithmetic).
- effect: helps
- reader: the manual and agentic-tool rates are "estimated" with working-with-leadership methods the paper does not describe, so this is a comparison with no control on equal tasks. The unit is PRs, not merged PRs or shipped gain. Dividing by 43% of the time excludes the 57% spent on infrastructure and onboarding; per total engineer-hour the rate would be lower (reader's arithmetic: 0.35 x 0.43 is about 0.15 if the time share is applied). The comparison tasks are performance PRs, which Autoperf selects by measurable objective. The authors run and report their own system.

### F5 · practice · task-scoped-viewpoints-over-raw-corpus · E3
- practice: agents read and write the shared knowledge through "viewpoints" (MCP servers, APIs, AI-search endpoints) over materialised views, so each decision gets a compact task-scoped context instead of the raw corpus (§2.1).
- claim: token savings "8× on average and up to >100×" (§2.1, Figure 3); the viewpoints also give provenance, access control at retrieval and "sharpen focus".
- evidence: Figure 3 is a CDF of per-query retrieval context, in thousands of tokens, for a raw corpus (marked "est."), a summarised view and a graph viewpoint, with a 128K context line; only the 8× and >100× appear in the text. The task and the number of queries are not stated.
- effect: helps
- reader: the saving is in tokens, and the raw-corpus side is an estimate, not a run. Whether decision quality stayed equal is not shown, so "helps" is only about cost. The authors build the viewpoints.

### F6 · practice · preference-tuning-on-search-lineage · E3
- practice: turn Darwin lineages into preference pairs without human labels (two children of one parent, the higher-scoring edit is chosen, shared parent context is the prompt, as parent-to-child diffs), split train and eval by run, and fine-tune Qwen3.6 27B with offline DPO and a LoRA adapter (§3.2).
- claim: the tuned policy "reaches the base policy's best solution quality with 72.5% fewer mutator agents" and "a modestly higher valid score"; "we read these results as early but concrete evidence that end-to-end decision traces unlock real learning" (§3.2).
- evidence: circle_packing [12]: 11 against 40 mutator agents; sum of radii 2.631 against 2.618, +0.48%, which "closes by 75% the remaining distance to optimal" (§3.2). The number of runs, the size of the training set and variance are not stated.
- effect: helps
- reader: tuned against base on one problem is an A/B on equal tasks, but one task, no repeats stated, no variance, and the training lineages come from the same problem family as the evaluation; the authors call it "a single high-signal ablation" and "early". So E3, not E4. That the tuned policy has seen lineages of the same problem is not addressed beyond the split by run. The 72.5% is a matched-quality comparison, not a score gain; the score gain is 0.48%.

### F7 · practice · agent-session-as-mutation-operator · E0
- practice: each evolutionary mutation is a full bounded agent session in an isolated sandbox: clone the repository, take a targeted hypothesis, edit, run tests and benchmarks, debug, then reflect and emit structured hypotheses about what worked (§2.3).
- claim: candidates entering selection "are already locally debugged, dramatically reducing wasted evaluations" (§2.3); this is the "critical difference from prior LLM-driven evolutionary approaches".
- evidence: none given. The ablation that would measure it is withheld (footnote 2).
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted. F1 compares whole systems and cannot separate this design choice from the rest.

### F8 · practice · hypothesis-ledger-with-tabu-and-bandit-sampling · E0
- practice: keep hypotheses as first-class records with a lifecycle (creation, weighted sampling, refinement, matching, retirement); exclude already-explored hypotheses from sampling (tabu); adjust each hypothesis's weight by past usage and success with a contextual-bandit rule; seeder, refiner and matcher agents reshape the search (§2.3).
- claim: stops "dozens of concurrent agents" re-discovering the same optimisation; the swarm gets "persistent, cross-generation memory that prior systems lack" and a tunable mutation size (§2.3).
- evidence: none given.
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted. No count of duplicate work with and without the tabu rule.

### F9 · practice · expert-persona-reviewer-from-historical-reviews · E0
- practice: agents impersonate a subject-matter expert, guided by prompts extracted from the World Model, which was learned from historical reviews; they address the feedback before the human review (§2.4). Autoperf adds an automated adversarial reviewing step (§4).
- claim: "anecdotally shortened reviewing cycles"; "we are currently gathering more systematic evidence" (§2.4).
- evidence: none given. "Reviewing remains a mostly open challenge" (§2.4).
- effect: helps
- reader: an assertion by the authors, with no number; the paper itself calls it anecdotal.

### F10 · practice · evidence-ranked-target-selection · E0
- practice: Hotspan turns a vague goal into target metrics (priming), proposes targets from telemetry, code analysis and past pull requests (enumeration), dedups and boosts targets corroborated by several channels, ranks by expected ROI as frequency-weighted gain over token cost, stability risk and reviewer effort, then packages the target with an evaluation harness; engineers steer at priming and at packaging (§2.2).
- claim: automates the engineer's targeting workflow and "mitigates the Missing Data problem" through multi-channel evidence (§2.2).
- evidence: none given for Hotspan on its own; its only reported use is inside Autoperf (F4).
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted. No comparison with targets chosen by engineers or by a vanilla agent.

### F11 · practice · incident-graph-with-pheromone-update · E0
- practice: distil resolved incidents into a graph of problem, action and observation nodes; triage agents traverse the highest-evidence paths for a live incident; each investigation trace is admitted back through a clearing house, and an ant-colony rule reinforces successful paths and decays unused ones (§2.5, §3.1).
- claim: "the graph automatically reorganizes toward the trajectories that resolve production problems, continually improving AutoSRE" (§3.1).
- evidence: none in this paper; the mechanism is attributed to [19] (GraphMind) and earlier SRE results to [20], and "we have fewer concrete results in this submission" (§1).
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted here. The cited papers may hold the data.

### F12 · observation · tool-or-codebase-expertise-sufficient · E1
- practice: observed in the seven Table 1 cases, where engineers differ in codebase expertise and in Darwin expertise.
- claim: "either tool or codebase expertise alone were sufficient to obtain meaningful results in a matter of hours" (§4).
- evidence: Table 1. Dataverse: high code expertise, no Darwin expertise, 2-4 h. Xbox, Copilot CLI and NL2DAX: no code expertise, high Darwin expertise, 16 h, 40 h and 4 h. Experimental LSM tree: no code expertise and low Darwin expertise, 1600× speedup in 3 h.
- effect: helps
- reader: seven cases chosen by the authors, with no case of low expertise on both sides that failed; the Experimental LSM tree row has neither kind of expertise at a high level. Copilot CLI took 40 h, not "hours" in the sense of the Fabric rows.

## Limits stated by the authors

- The general version of the problem is hard; results hold for "narrower but important scenarios": Data Systems and Evolutionary Coding Tasks with an automatically measurable objective (§1).
- The self-improvement loop "is a more recent addition to our system, so we have fewer concrete results in this submission" (§1).
- The fine-tuning result is "early", from "a single high-signal ablation"; the on-policy version is ongoing work (§3.2).
- The "high ROI" observation "might be biased by our ability to instinctively pick problems that are a good fit for Darwin" (§4).
- Impact estimates "inherently hard"; the savings range may be off by 10x to 100x (§4).
- Review evidence is anecdotal; systematic evidence is being gathered (§2.4).
- Open challenges named: general-purpose world models, proving semantic equivalence and estimating gains, Darwin cost in noisy settings, automating privacy-preserving flighting, calibrating trust in World Model updates (§2.1 to §3.1).

## Reader's notes

- Short vision paper by the system's builders; every number is the authors' own deployment of their own system. The two largest claims (3× above agentic coding, 22× token efficiency) both come from comparisons that the paper does not describe: the agentic-tools baseline is an estimate, and the ablation behind the token claim is omitted.
- The 3× and 4.7× rates divide PRs by engineer-hours spent on application scenarios only; 57% of team time (infrastructure and onboarding) is outside the denominator.
- The 13.8% for the OSS set is over the 20 best of 45 systems; the other 25 systems are described only as positive "sometimes modest".
- Gains are measured by the same objective Darwin optimised, per its own evaluation harness; the paper does not report held-out workloads or checks that behaviour is preserved, apart from naming correctness proof as an open challenge (§2.2).
- None of the performance changes is reported as merged or shipped; the paper says Autoperf output enters "engineering review queues".
- Which model sits behind Darwin's agents, the number of runs per result, and the dates of the data are not stated.

## Discrepancies

- The token-efficiency comparison is stated three ways: "up to 22× better token efficiency (vs [17])" (§1), "Comparing Darwin to OpenEvolve we observe a 22× token efficiency" (§2.3), and "22× less than MAP-elites [13] and 17× less than [17]" (§4). [17] is OpenEvolve in the reference list, so the 22× is against OpenEvolve in §1 and §2.3 and against MAP-Elites in §4.
- "Either tool or codebase expertise alone were sufficient" (§4), while Table 1's Experimental LSM tree row lists no code expertise and low Darwin expertise, so neither kind is high; the claim holds only for rows that have one high level.
- §4 says Darwin obtained improvements "across every system we targeted" and then reports the average for "the best-performing 20 systems" only; the other 25 are not quantified in the text.
- "In a matter of hours" (§4) against 16 h and 40 h in Table 1 (Xbox, Copilot CLI).
