## Question

How do models, harnesses and tasks jointly shape coding-agent performance, which of five harness
components (tool registry, context compression, explicit planning, subagents, lazy skills) drive the
gain, and how do they change agent behaviour and token use (§3.2)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · harness-gain-shrinks-on-strong-models · E3
- practice: matched-model results from the public SWE-bench Verified leaderboard, with mini-SWE-agent as the base harness and the same model under a more complex harness (§5.1.1, Table 3). The paper's own runs are not involved.
- claim: "more advanced harnesses provide larger improvements over mini-SWE-agent for weaker models, while the margin becomes smaller for stronger models" (§5.1.1).
- evidence: Table 3, gain over mini-SWE-agent. SWE-agent +9.60 pp with Claude 3.7 Sonnet (base 52.80) and +4.07 with Claude 4 Sonnet (base 64.93); OpenHands +13.60 and +5.47; Tools +10.40, +7.47 and +5.60 with Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Claude 4 Sonnet and Claude 4 Opus (base 67.60).
- effect: helps
- reader: the gain is positive in every row and smaller on the stronger model; two or three models per harness. Leaderboard entries come from different submitters, dates and settings, and the stronger model's base score is also nearer the ceiling.

### F2 · observation · harness-gain-shrinks-on-strong-models · E3
- practice: the authors' own runs of OpenCode against mini-SWE-agent on 5 Qwen and 5 DeepSeek models, SWE-bench Pro, 300 random instances, 250 steps, temperature 0.0, non-thinking mode (§4.4, §5.1.1).
- claim: the benefit of the complex harness "generally decreases as model capability improves"; Qwen narrows smoothly, DeepSeek is non-monotonic, which the authors call a "threshold effect" (§5.1.1).
- evidence: OpenCode advantage over mini-SWE-agent 6.00 pp (Qwen3-Max) to 2.67 pp (Qwen3.7-Max) (RQ1.1 answer box); the bars read 40.0 and 37.3 at the Qwen3.7-Max end (Figure 5). DeepSeek-V4-Pro 34.3 (OpenCode) against 33.7 (mini-SWE-agent) (§5.1.1). DeepSeek-R1 gets little from OpenCode and V3 and V3.1 get more (text; values in Figure 6 only). Step counts per sample, Table 5: DeepSeek-V4-Pro 31.96 with OpenCode against 61.77 with mini-SWE-agent. Qwen tool calls with OpenCode rise from 39.87 to 72.23 over the model sequence while steps stay near 36 to 38 (Table 4).
- effect: mixed
- reader: a harness swap on equal instances for each model, but the claim is about a trend over five models, from one run per cell with no test or interval stated, so E3. OpenCode and mini-SWE-agent differ in many ways at once; nothing isolates what produces the gain. The DeepSeek pattern does not follow the claimed trend until the last model.

### F3 · observation · harness-gain-needs-capable-model-on-open-ended-tasks · E3
- practice: the same OpenCode against mini-SWE-agent comparison on ProgramBench (70 random of 200 instances: 20 easy, 21 medium, 29 hard) and on GitTaskBench (all 54) (§5.1.2).
- claim: on these open-ended tasks OpenCode "does not bring greater improvements to weaker models"; only the latest models (Qwen3.7-Max, DeepSeek-V4-Pro) "can consistently leverage the complex harness"; on GitTaskBench the turning points are Qwen3.6-Max and DeepSeek-V3.1 (§5.1.2).
- evidence: no per-model numbers in the text. Figure labels at the strongest models, with the series not named in the text: ProgramBench Qwen3.7-Max 52.3 and 47.8, DeepSeek-V4-Pro 48.2 and 41.7; GitTaskBench Qwen 74.1 and 72.2, DeepSeek 72.2 and 70.4 (Figures 7 to 10). On the full 200 ProgramBench instances OpenCode beats mini-SWE-agent by 8.80 pp (Qwen3.7-Max) and 7.34 pp (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) (Table 6).
- effect: mixed
- reader: the claim about weaker models rests on figures that the text does not quote. GitTaskBench has 54 instances, so a 2-point gap is about one instance (reader's arithmetic). The explanation that models were trained on SWE-style tasks is the authors' speculation (§5.1.2).

### F4 · practice · structured-file-tool-registry · E4
- practice: add a registry with `read_file`, `write_file`, `edit_file` and `glob` and native tool calling on top of the Bash-only mini-SWE-agent; compared on the same 200 ProgramBench instances (§3.1, §5.2.1).
- claim: the tool registry is among the two components with "the most stable and substantial gains" (RQ2.1 answer box).
- evidence: score +4.57 pp from 42.88 (Qwen3.7-Max) and +3.54 pp from 45.14 (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) (§5.2.1). Tool calls +16.06% and +12.29%; prompt tokens +8.08% and +19.02% (Table 7).
- effect: helps
- reader: an additive ablation on equal tasks, so E4, but one run per cell with no test, two models, one benchmark, and one baseline harness. The authors wrote the tools and decide the baseline.

### F5 · practice · todo-list-planning-tool · E4
- practice: add a `todo_write` tool with at most 10 items and one item in progress, the plan shown back in the prompt, and a reminder when the plan is stale (§3.1, §4.4).
- claim: planning gives "small gains" and "alone is insufficient to substantially improve repository generation" (§5.2.1).
- evidence: the text gives no number. The figure labels +1.36 pp (Qwen3.7-Max) and +0.77 pp (DeepSeek-V4-Pro), which the reader assigns to planning by elimination from the text (Figures 11 and 12). Prompt tokens +21.10% and response tokens +40.45% for Qwen; steps −1.86% and −1.07% (Table 7).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 by design; the gains are within what one run per cell could produce and no test is given, so "helps" is the paper's direction only.

### F6 · practice · lazy-skill-loading-catalog · E4
- practice: put only a catalog of 6 skills in the prompt (Terminal Workflow, Minimal Implementation, Build Diagnostics, Test Triage, CLI Fuzzing, Submission Review) and let the agent call `load_skill(name)` for the detailed text (§3.1, Table 1).
- claim: skills improve performance with effects "more moderate and model-dependent", a larger benefit for DeepSeek-V4-Pro than for Qwen3.7-Max (§5.2.1).
- evidence: the figure labels +0.81 pp (Qwen) and +3.07 pp (DeepSeek), assigned to skills by the reader from the text's ranking (Figures 11 and 12). Prompt tokens +13.21% and +3.48% (Table 7).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 by design; single run, no test. The skills were written by the authors for ProgramBench (CLI fuzzing, submission review), so a gain says little about generic skills. Whether the agents loaded the skills is not reported in the parts read.

### F7 · practice · context-compression-module · E4
- practice: add context compression: large tool outputs stored in the workspace with a path and a preview, snipping and compaction of old messages, LLM summarisation when still over the limit; 350K-token budget, the last 20 messages kept (§3.1, §4.4). Measured on score.
- claim: compression "degrades performance for both models" because ProgramBench needs detailed requirements and interface constraints kept over long trajectories (§5.2.1).
- evidence: score −4.86 pp (Qwen3.7-Max) and −3.85 pp (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) (§5.2.1, RQ3.1 text).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E4 by design, one run per cell. The mechanism (lost requirements) is the authors' reading; no trajectory shows it. One compression setting was tried.

### F8 · practice · context-compression-module · E4
- practice: the same compression module as F7; the measurement is tokens, steps and tool calls.
- claim: compression "reduces cost but hurts performance" (RQ3.1 answer box).
- evidence: prompt tokens −54.29% (Qwen3.7-Max, 3,978,148 to 1,818,344) and −77.02% (DeepSeek-V4-Pro, 10,065,204 to 2,312,701); steps −40.68% and −61.91% (Table 7).
- effect: helps
- reader: token counts, not money; the paper gives no price for this component. The fall in steps may come from agents ending runs early, which the paper does not separate.

### F9 · practice · task-specific-validation-subagents · E4
- practice: let the parent agent delegate to three subagent profiles for behaviour analysis, differential testing and submission review, each with fresh history, a restricted tool whitelist and no nested delegation (§3.1).
- claim: task-specific subagents give "the largest single-component gain" and reduce both excessive and insufficient probing (§5.2.1, §5.3.2).
- evidence: score +5.91 pp (Qwen3.7-Max) and +4.46 pp (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) (§5.2.1). Tool calls +115.76% and +73.10%; steps +35.69% and +33.94%; prompt tokens +7.35% and −9.54% (Table 7).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 by design, one run per cell. The profiles (behaviour analysis, differential testing, submission review) fit ProgramBench's setting of rebuilding a program from a reference binary, and were written by the authors for it; the general subagents of F10 differ in profile and tool set as well as in name, so what separates the two results is not isolated.

### F10 · practice · stage-isolated-sub-agents · E4
- practice: let the parent delegate to five general subagents organised by lifecycle stage (requirement_analysis, architecture_design, code_generation, test_generation, defect_repair), each with fresh history and a restricted tool set (§3.1).
- claim: general subagents "degrade performance" and "may introduce coordination overhead or produce artifacts that are not directly aligned with the fine-grained requirements" (§5.2.1).
- evidence: the text gives no number. The figure labels −0.70 pp (Qwen3.7-Max) and −2.31 pp (DeepSeek-V4-Pro), which the reader assigns by elimination (Figures 11 and 12). Tool calls +51.14% and +82.65%; DeepSeek-V4-Pro prompt tokens +51.86% (Table 7).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E4 by design. A drop of 0.70 pp on 200 instances from one run is small; the cause is a guess. The unqualified text claim rests on figure values the reader had to map.

### F11 · practice · modular-harness-with-selected-components · E4
- practice: enable all five components together in NanoHarness, including the two that hurt alone (§5.2.2).
- claim: NanoHarness "achieves the best performance among the variants for both models"; the gain is larger than any single component's (§5.2.2).
- evidence: ProgramBench score 42.88 to 50.25 (+7.37 pp) for Qwen3.7-Max and 45.14 to 51.35 (+6.21 pp) for DeepSeek-V4-Pro (§5.2.2, Table 6). Tool calls +131.18% and +83.58%; steps +28.38% and +43.14%; prompt tokens +16.37% and +7.26% (Table 7).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 against the mini-SWE-agent baseline on equal tasks; the authors built and tuned their own system, and a single run per cell without a test is reported. The abstract's gain is on one benchmark; generalisation is untested (§7).

### F12 · practice · modular-harness-with-selected-components · E3
- practice: compare NanoHarness with the product-level harnesses OpenCode and Claude Code on ProgramBench (Table 6).
- claim: a lightweight modular harness "can recover most of their performance gains" (§5.2.2).
- evidence: Table 6. Qwen3.7-Max: mini-SWE-agent 42.88, NanoHarness 50.25, OpenCode 51.68, Claude Code 52.33; DeepSeek-V4-Pro: 45.14, 51.35, 52.48, 52.76. Gaps to OpenCode and Claude Code 1.43 and 2.08 pp (Qwen), 1.13 and 1.41 pp (DeepSeek) (§5.2.2). NanoHarness holds 78% to 85% of the product harnesses' gain (reader's arithmetic from Table 6).
- effect: mixed
- reader: the three harnesses differ in many ways, so there is no control; NanoHarness trails both by 1.1 to 2.1 points and the paper gives no interval. Table 6 prints the differences as "%" where the text says pp. How Claude Code was run with Qwen and DeepSeek models is not stated in the parts read.

### F13 · observation · harness-feature-interactions · E2
- practice: the combined result of F11 set against the single-component results of F4 to F10 (§5.2.2).
- claim: "the combined improvement is not simply the sum of all individual gains"; components "are not independent", and the negative ones may add overhead when combined with the strong ones (§5.2.2).
- evidence: full NanoHarness +7.37 pp (Qwen) and +6.21 pp (DeepSeek) against the best single component +5.91 and +4.46 (§5.2.1, §5.2.2). The sum of the six single-component figure labels is +7.09 pp (Qwen) and +5.68 pp (DeepSeek) (reader's arithmetic from Figures 11 and 12); no leave-one-out run exists.
- effect: mixed
- reader: the reader's sum is close to the combined result, so the data do not show non-additivity; the paper gives no test of it. The interaction mechanism is asserted. Components were only added to the baseline, never removed from the full set.

### F14 · practice · exploration-probing-regularised-by-harness · E1
- practice: compare the number of probes of the reference binary per trajectory, by harness configuration, on Qwen3.7-Max and ProgramBench; plus two named cases (§5.3.2, Figure 13).
- claim: mini-SWE-agent fails by excessive probing (many shallow probes, budget used up) or insufficient probing (20 or fewer probes, then smoke tests only); task-specific subagents reduce both, and NanoHarness "regularizes exploration" (§5.3.2).
- evidence: jqlang__jq.b33a763: 133 probes and 1602/6491 tests (24.68%) with mini-SWE-agent against 43 probes and 4425/6491 (68.17%) with NanoHarness. lfos__calcurse.49180d5: 3 probes and 1231/1994 (61.74%) against 45 probes and 1499/1994 (75.18%). The probe distributions are in Figure 13 only.
- effect: helps
- reader: two chosen cases and a figure; the number of failed trajectories sorted into each failure mode is not given. The categories were made by manual inspection.

### F15 · observation · harness-choice-spread-on-leaderboard · E3
- practice: the public SWE-bench Verified leaderboard, read two ways: the spread of scores over harnesses at one model, and over models at one harness (§1, Figure 1).
- claim: harness design "can have an impact comparable to model choice" (§1).
- evidence: Figure 1: with Claude 3.5 Sonnet (2024-10-16) fixed, the harness range is 19.4 points; with mini-SWE-agent fixed, the model range across Claude models is 22.8 points (§1).
- effect: helps
- reader: leaderboard entries differ in submitter, date and settings; the harness set and the model set were chosen by the authors; no control. "helps" means the better harness scores higher; the finding is a spread.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Only five components are studied; other mechanisms may matter (§7).
- Only the Qwen and DeepSeek families are used; the authors consider ten variants a mitigation (§7).
- The main experiments are on ProgramBench; the conclusions may not generalise to other datasets (§7).
- Cost of the study: over 10B (Qwen) and 20B (DeepSeek) prompt tokens, an estimated cost of over $7,000 (§7).

## Reader's notes

- No number of repetitions, seeds, intervals or tests is reported in the parts read; temperature is 0.0 (§4.4). Every difference of 1 to 3 points is one run.
- The metric called "Score" on ProgramBench is not defined in the parts read; the two cases in §5.3.2 suggest a share of tests passed.
- The ProgramBench step limit was cut from 1,000 to 300 to save cost (§4.4). Whether steps taken inside a subagent count against it is not stated, and subagent rounds have their own limit of 50.
- Components were added one at a time to the baseline, never removed from the full configuration, so interactions are not tested.
- The task-specific subagents and some skills (CLI fuzzing, submission review, differential testing) are written for ProgramBench's reference-binary setting; the component ranking may not carry over to issue repair. The paper's own RQ1 shows that the harness gain differs by task.
- All models run in non-thinking mode (§4.3); no closed-weight model is used.
- The authors built NanoHarness and ran the comparison with the product harnesses; their prompts, skill texts and subagent profiles are not given in the parts read.

## Discrepancies

- Table 6 prints "+7.37%", "+8.80%" and the like; the text and the abstract give the same values as percentage points.
- The abstract and §5.2.1 say general subagents "can hurt" and "degrade performance for both models"; the text quotes no number for them, and the figure labels that fit them are −0.70 and −2.31 points (reader's mapping).
- §5.2.2 says the combined gain is "not simply the sum" of the single gains; the sum of the single-component figure labels, 7.09 and 5.68 points, is close to the combined 7.37 and 6.21 (reader's arithmetic).
