## Question

How do the AI tools of early 2025 change the time experienced open-source developers need to complete
real issues in mature repositories they know well (abstract, §1)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: for each issue a coin flip allows or forbids generative AI tools; when allowed, the developer may use any tool or none, in practice mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 or 3.7 Sonnet (§2.2, C.3.6).
- claim: "allowing AI actually increases completion time by 19%" for these developers on these repositories (abstract, §3).
- evidence: 246 issues, 136 allowed and 110 disallowed (§3); estimate from a log-linear regression on self-reported implementation time with forecast difficulty as a covariate (§2.3). The raw difference in times is 34% (fn 12). Subsets: 24% for developers using comparable IDEs in both arms (C.3.1); 20% without the 25 block-randomised issues (E.3); 21% for developers with no dropped issues (C.3.3); 25% using screen-recording length against 24% self-reported on the same subset (C.3.4). Mean post-review time 15 minutes allowed against 9 disallowed, not significant (C.2.7). AI was not used in 16.4% of allowed cases with labelled recordings (C.3.2).
- effect: hurts
- reader: randomised at the issue level, so E4. The interval of the 19% is shown in Figure 1 only and is not in the text read. 16 developers; not blinded (C.2.1). The outcome is time, with quality held by the repositories' review. The tools are those of February to June 2025.

### F2 · observation · self-reported-speedup · E4
- practice: none; an observation. Developers forecast the effect of AI before the study and estimate it afterwards; experts forecast it from a description of the study (§2.4).
- claim: developers and experts "drastically overestimate the usefulness of AI on developer productivity, even after they have spent many hours using the tools" (§4).
- evidence: developers forecast 24% less time before and estimate 20% less after, against the measured 19% more (§3.1). 34 economics experts forecast 39% less and 54 ML experts 38% less (§3.1, Table 6). Forecast and actual time per issue correlate at 0.64 (allowed) and 0.59 (disallowed), so relative difficulty is judged well (§3.1). 69% of developers kept using Cursor after the study (C.2.6).
- effect: hurts
- reader: graded E4 because the reference value is the randomised estimate of F1; the beliefs themselves are survey answers from 16 developers. It bears on every paper in this store that measures benefit by asking developers.

### F3 · observation · developer-chooses-tasks-for-ai · E3
- practice: none applied; a subgroup analysis. Issues are grouped by the speedup the developer forecast for them before assignment (E.2).
- claim: on issues in the top third of forecast speedup (at least 33%) "developers are not slowed down by AI, and slowdown monotonically decreases as the forecasted speedup increases" (E.2, Figure 18).
- evidence: Figure 18; the values are in the figure and not in the text. The tertiles are imbalanced (Figure 18 caption).
- effect: helps
- reader: a post-hoc subgroup of a randomised study; no n per group in the text. It suggests developers can tell which tasks suit AI even though they misjudge the size of the effect.

### F4 · observation · agent-on-familiar-code · E3
- practice: none applied; a subgroup analysis by the developer's prior exposure to the task (rated 1 to 5 before the issue) and need for external resources (rated 1 to 3) (C.1.2).
- claim: "moderate evidence that developers are slowed down more on issues where they have high prior task exposure" and where they need fewer external resources (C.1.2, Figure 7).
- evidence: Figure 7, values not in the text; collected for the second half of the issues only (Figure 7 caption). Developers average 5 years and 1,500 commits on their repository (§2.1).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the paper's own label is "moderate evidence", and §3.3 warns the study is not powered for subgroup comparisons.

### F5 · observation · agent-in-large-mature-repository · E1
- practice: none applied; the setting. Repositories average about 10 years of age and more than 1,100,000 lines of code (C.1.3).
- claim: "developers report AI performs worse in large and complex environments" (Table 1, C.1.3); the results are "consistent with small greenfield projects or development in unfamiliar codebases seeing substantial speedup" (§4.1).
- evidence: developer quotes (C.1.3). No comparison with small or new repositories inside the study.
- effect: hurts
- reader: the paper marks this "we hypothesize". The contrast with greenfield work is drawn against other papers, not tested here.

### F6 · observation · implicit-repository-context · E1
- practice: none applied; an observation about what the tools lacked.
- claim: "developers report AI doesn't utilize important tacit knowledge or context" of the repository (Table 1, C.1.5).
- evidence: developer quotes (C.1.5). No measurement.
- effect: hurts
- reader: the paper marks this "we hypothesize". Whether written context files would close the gap is not tested.

### F7 · practice · review-of-ai-output · E2
- practice: developers review and rework what the tool generates (C.1.4).
- claim: low reliability of AI output costs time: developers "accept <44% of AI generations" and spend "approximately 9% of their time reviewing and cleaning AI generated outputs" (Table 1, C.1.4).
- evidence: acceptance below 44% from Cursor analytics for 13 developers (C.1.4, §2.4); 9% of time on 44 issues with valid labelled recordings (C.1.4); 75% report reading every line of AI code, 56% often make major changes, 100% modify it (C.1.4); 4% of time waiting on generations (C.2.9).
- effect: hurts
- reader: descriptive shares inside the AI-allowed arm; the text read gives no minutes per activity for the two arms side by side (Figures 19 to 21).

### F8 · observation · tool-experience · E3
- practice: none applied; a subgroup analysis by prior experience with Cursor, Copilot and web LLMs, and by hours of Cursor use during the study (C.2.8).
- claim: "we don't see meaningful differences between developers based on prior experience with AI tooling"; no clear learning effect over the first 50 hours of Cursor use (C.2.8, Figures 10 to 12).
- evidence: 44% of developers had used Cursor before (C.2.8). Similar slowdown when up to the first 8 AI-allowed issues are excluded (Figure 11 caption). Above 50 hours the figure shows a speedup for 1 developer; a second developer reported later moves that bucket "from roughly 25% speedup to roughly 0%" (fn 15).
- effect: none
- reader: the paper says it is "underpowered to draw strong conclusions" here. Experience beyond 50 hours rests on 1 or 2 people.

### F9 · practice · unattended-agent-on-study-issues · E0
- practice: fully autonomous agents on Claude 3.7 Sonnet attempt issues from repositories in the study (§4.1).
- claim: they "can often correctly implement the core functionality" but "fail to fully satisfy all requirements (typically leaving out important documentation, failing linting/styling rules, and leaving out key unit or integration tests)" (§4.1).
- evidence: none in this paper; announced as "preliminary evidence (forthcoming)".
- effect: mixed
- reader: a pointer to later work, not a result.

## Limits stated by the authors

- The slowdown factors are "specific to the setting we study"; the results "do not imply that current AI systems are not useful in many realistic, economically relevant settings" (§1).
- High familiarity with the repository and the size and maturity of the repositories both contribute and "do not apply in many software development settings" (§4.1).
- Future models, better prompting or scaffolding, or fine-tuning "could yield positive speedup in this setting" (§1).
- The factor analysis is "speculative, suggestive evidence"; the study is "not powered for statistically significant multiple comparisons when subsetting" (§3.3).
- The study is not blinded; some developers report overusing AI because of the experiment (C.2.1).
- Issues were capped at about 2 hours, so they are shorter than average work; review and design discussion are not measured (C.2.2).
- Developers who rely heavily on AI may have been less likely to take part (C.2.5).
- Qualitative evidence came from probing questions and may be biased (§2.4).

## Reader's notes

- The practice tested is AI assistance inside an editor with a human at the keyboard, in early 2025. Cursor's agent mode was used in 55% of AI-allowed issues and chat in 48% (C.2.10). It is not a test of unattended agents.
- Of 21 candidate factors the paper rates 5 as likely contributors, 10 as unclear and 6 as unlikely (§3.3, Table 1). Only the 5 likely ones and 2 of the others are filed above.
- Valid labelled recordings are counted as 74 in §2.4 and §3.2, 44 AI-allowed in C.1.4 and C.3.2, and 54 AI-disallowed in C.3.2. 44 + 54 = 98 (reader's arithmetic); the text read does not reconcile the three numbers.
- The model shares in C.3.6 (25%, 34%, 23%, 11%, 3%, 2%, and 16.4% with no model) sum to about 114% (reader's arithmetic); the text does not say whether one issue counts under several models.
- The developers were paid $150 per hour (§2.1) and the Cursor Pro subscriptions were provided by the authors (§2.2.1).

## Discrepancies

- The arXiv abstract page says 20 properties of the setting were evaluated; the PDF abstract and §1 say 21.
- C.2.1: the text says Figure 8 "doesn't show a clear effect" by self-reported usage style; the caption of Figure 8 says developers who were experimenting or using as much AI as possible "see greater slowdown".
- C.3.4: imputing 1 hour of post-review time for AI-allowed issues is reported with a 14% slowdown and the reverse imputation with 23%, "respectively". Adding time to the AI-allowed arm should raise the slowdown above 19%, not lower it (reader's reasoning; the paper does not show the arithmetic).
- C.3.3 gives a 42% slowdown for developers with no uncompleted AI-disallowed issues and 21% for the other two no-dropout subsets; Table 1 summarises this as "similar slowdown".
- C.2.3 in the text: 47% more code "per hour of AI-disallowed forecasts"; Table 1: "per forecasted hour in AI-allowed issues".
- Table 4's difference column (9 and 4 minutes) against its rounded means (116 − 106 and 87 − 82) (reader's arithmetic); rounding would explain it.
