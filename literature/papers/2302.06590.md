## Question

Does access to GitHub Copilot change how fast, and how often, professional developers complete a
standardised programming task, and for whom (Introduction)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: give randomly assigned developers GitHub Copilot and a 1-minute introduction video, against a control group without it that may use internet search and Stack Overflow; task is an HTTP server in JavaScript, scored by 12 visible tests and timed from creation of the repository to the first commit that passes all 12 (§Study Design).
- claim: the treated group "completed the task 55.8% faster than the control group" (Abstract).
- evidence: among those who completed, mean time 71.17 minutes treated against 160.89 minutes control; t-test p = 0.0017; 95% confidence interval for the improvement [21%, 89%]. Four outliers above 300 minutes, all in the control group; the result "remains robust" without them (§Results).
- effect: helps
- reader: randomised assignment on one task, so E4. The comparison is conditional on finishing the task: 35 completed of 95 assigned, and the text does not give the group sizes of the analysed sample or say that non-completion was balanced between groups. The authors are from GitHub and Microsoft and evaluate their own product. Single narrow task with a visible test suite.

### F2 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: as F1; the measurement is task success, the share of participants who adequately completed the task (§Study Design).
- claim: the treated group's success rate is higher, but not significantly (§Results).
- evidence: success rate 7 percentage points higher than control; 95% confidence interval [-0.11, 0.25] (§Results).
- effect: none
- reader: E4 by design. The interval is wide and contains zero, so a null here is not evidence of no effect. How "completed" is defined for the success rate is not stated beyond the 12 passing tests.

### F3 · observation · seniority-and-ai-speedup · E3
- practice: none applied; the treatment effect is regressed on covariates, including years of programming experience (Table 1, §Results).
- claim: "developers with less programming experience ... benefit more from Copilot" (§Results, Abstract).
- evidence: Table 1, programming experience (years): estimate 8.23, SE 4.36, t = 1.90, p = 0.0629. The outcome of the regression is not named in the text. The estimate is positive while the other two significant covariates are negative.
- effect: helps
- reader: the subgroup comparison is not randomised and the interaction is not significant at 0.05. The paper states the direction as a result without that caveat. With 35 completers and 8 covariates the regression is thin. The effect value records the direction the authors claim, for the less experienced.

### F4 · observation · daily-coding-hours-and-ai-speedup · E3
- practice: none applied; the treatment effect is regressed on covariates, including hours of programming per day (Table 1, §Results).
- claim: developers "with heavy coding load (hours of coding per day)" benefit more from Copilot (§Results).
- evidence: Table 1, hours of programming per day: estimate -11.70, SE 4.74, t = -2.47, p = 0.0168 (nominal, no multiple-comparison correction stated).
- effect: helps
- reader: hours per day are self-reported. One of 8 covariates; the paper does not correct for 8 tests. Sign convention is inferred from the authors' reading, since the outcome of the regression is not named.

### F5 · observation · age-and-ai-speedup · E3
- practice: none applied; the treatment effect is regressed on covariates, including an age-group indicator for 25 to 44 (Table 1, §Results).
- claim: "older programmers" benefit more; the Table 1 note says "the age group above 25" (Introduction, §Results).
- evidence: Table 1, age 25-44: estimate -74.55, SE 33.52, t = -2.22, p = 0.0303. Most participants are aged 25 to 34 (§Results).
- effect: helps
- reader: the reference group is the participants outside 25 to 44, presumably under 25, and their number is not given. "Older" in the abstract-level text means 25 to 44, which is most of the sample. Nominal p, no correction for multiple covariates.

### F6 · observation · self-reported-speedup · E3
- practice: none applied; after the task, treated developers estimated how much Copilot gained them, and control developers, after a 1-minute demo video, estimated the gain they would have had (§Study Design, §Results).
- claim: self-estimates underestimate the measured gain (§Results).
- evidence: both groups estimated a 35% increase in productivity on average, against the measured 55.8% (§Results, Figure 7 for the distribution, not in the text).
- effect: helps
- reader: compared with a measurement, not a control. The 35% is an estimate of "productivity gain" and the 55.8% is a time reduction; the two are not the same quantity (see Discrepancies). The control group estimated without using the tool.

### F7 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: as F1; the measurement is the highest monthly price at which a developer would want to be notified about the release of Copilot, called the irrelevant price (§Results).
- claim: the treated group's higher willingness to pay is "indirect evidence" that it benefited (§Results).
- evidence: average $27.25 per month treated against $16.91 per month control; difference "statistically significant at the 95% level"; test not named (§Results, Figure 8).
- effect: helps
- reader: randomised, so E4 on stated willingness to pay, a perception outcome. It is a stated price, not a purchase, and it is an upper bound on willingness to pay by the authors' own description.

## Limits stated by the authors

- One standardised task rather than collaboration on large proprietary or open-source projects; benefits may vary across tasks and languages (§Discussion).
- Code quality is not examined; AI help could raise it or lower it if the programmer pays less attention (§Discussion).
- The heterogeneous effects need to persist in further studies (§Discussion).

## Reader's notes

- Only 35 of 95 recruited developers appear in the analysis; the paper gives no reason for the 60 who did not. Treated developers who finish more slowly or abandon would drop out of a completion-time comparison.
- Five treated participants did not finish sign-up and started without Copilot (§Study Design). The text does not say whether they are in the treated group of the analysis.
- Payment depended on completion time on a published scale (Figure 1, §Study Design), so participants were paid to be fast on 12 visible tests. The result is speed against a test suite, not correctness beyond it.
- Copilot of 2022 is inline code completion, not an autonomous agent; nothing here bears on unattended agent work except as the earliest randomised baseline of speed.
- The control group could use search and Stack Overflow, so the contrast is Copilot against ordinary web help, not against no help.
- The heterogeneity regression names neither its outcome variable nor the sign convention; the direction in F3 to F5 follows the authors' reading.

## Discrepancies

- The Abstract and Introduction say the treated group was 55.8% faster, a reduction in completion time. The Discussion calls it "a 55.8% increase in productivity", and §Results compares the 35% self-estimate with "the 55.8% increase in their revealed productivity". A 55.8% time reduction is a speed ratio of 2.26 (160.89 / 71.17, reader's arithmetic), a 126% increase in speed, not 55.8%.
- §Study Design gives the start of the experiment as May 15, 2022. The note to Figure 1 says the Upwork posting started May 25th 2022, after that date.
- The Introduction and §Results say older programmers, and "aged between 25 and 44" benefit more; the note to Table 1 says "the age group above 25".
- The Introduction states that developers with less experience benefited the most without a qualifier; Table 1 gives p = 0.0629 for experience.
- "Thirty-five developers from both the treated and control groups completed the task and survey" does not say whether 35 is the total or per group.
