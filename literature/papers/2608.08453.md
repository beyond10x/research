## Question

Why do public `SKILL.md` files fail to be reusable beyond the task, repository or platform that produced them: which defects are common, which limit reuse, and which signals go with fewer defects (§1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · skill-defect-prevalence · E2
- practice: static analysis of 138,133 public skills with 31 detectors: 14 Tier 1 checks from the official specification (R1 routing metadata, R2 body content, R3 resource organisation) and 17 Tier 2 checks from best-practice literature (R4 prohibited content, R5 behavioural safety, R6 portability, R7 persona and scope) (§3.2, §4.2).
- claim: ordinary packaging defects, not exotic attacks, dominate; "the dominant failures are ordinary packaging problems rather than exotic attacks: weak routing metadata, bloated or non-actionable bodies, and poor resource organization" (Abstract, §4.2).
- evidence: 89.3% of skills trigger at least one Tier 1 detector and 91.8% at least one defect of any tier; mean 2.5 defects, median 2 (§4.2). Under lenient and strict thresholds on a 10,000-skill sample the share is 88.8% to 94.6% (§3.3). By category: R1 routing 67.0%, R2 body 51.4%, R3 resources 36.9%, R4 prohibited content 16.7%, R5 safety 9.8%, R7 scope 6.7%, R6 portability 5.8% (Figure 2, labels in extracted text). Top individual defects: R1.3 missing trigger guidance 52.3%, R2.4 name repeated as heading 44.3%, R3.2 too many inline examples 32.1% (§4.2).
- effect: hurts
- reader: a count of detector hits with nothing to compare against, so E2. The detectors are the authors' own regexes. Only fixtures with one positive and one negative case per detector were run; the authors name a manual precision audit of the safety detectors as the check they did not run (§6). The 91.8% mixes cosmetic and severe defects (§6). The paper never shows that a detected defect makes a skill fail at a task, except for R1 in F2. `hurts` here records the presence of defects, not a measured harm.

### F2 · observation · valid-routing-metadata-in-skill · E3
- practice: skills split by whether the detector finds a routing-metadata defect (R1: missing, too short, too long, missing "when", non-functional description, routing text in the body); a BM25 index over frontmatter descriptions only; queries derived from repository metadata or from the name and path (§3.4, §4.3).
- claim: "skills with valid routing metadata are retrieved more reliably from startup descriptions than skills with routing defects" (Abstract); BM25 is "a lower-bound probe" and an LLM selector may shrink the gap (§3.4).
- evidence: Table 1, 20,000 sampled skills. Metadata queries: R1-clean (n = 6,514) hit@1 88.5%, MRR 0.906; R1-defective (n = 13,226) 82.6%, 0.855. Name/path queries: R1-clean (n = 6,505) 26.7%, 0.347; R1-defective (n = 13,331) 24.3%, 0.322 (§4.3, Table 1). No significance test is stated for Table 1.
- effect: helps
- reader: groups are formed by an observed property, not assigned, so E3. Differences are 5.9 and 2.4 points hit@1 (reader's arithmetic). The index holds descriptions only, and R1-defective includes skills with a missing or very short description, which a lexical index over descriptions will retrieve badly by construction; how the metadata queries were derived from a skill that has no description is not stated in the parts read. It is not an agent run: shipped harnesses use an LLM selector (§3.4). Effect of R1 on task success is not measured.

### F3 · observation · spec-aware-skill-description · E3
- practice: skills whose description follows the pattern "[Verb] [what]. Use when [trigger]" (called specification-aware) compared with skills whose description does not (§5, Stage 1, §4.4).
- claim: "Specification awareness is the strongest quality correlate we identified" (§5).
- evidence: spec-aware skills average 1.83 detected defects against 3.00 for spec-unaware skills, Cliff's delta = −0.40, a medium effect (§4.4, §5).
- effect: helps
- reader: the outcome is the authors' detector count, and the spec-aware pattern is close to the R1.3 detector itself (missing trigger guidance, the top defect at 52.3%), so part of the gap is by construction. Not assigned, so E3. No task outcome.

### F4 · observation · ai-marked-skill-defects · E3
- practice: skills with an explicit AI-generation marker (n = 19,857) compared with unmarked skills (n = 118,276) on detected defects (§4.4).
- claim: AI-marked skills show more safety and portability problems; the authors read it as "a signal that the explicit-marker subset warrants closer review at generation time, not as evidence that AI-authored skills are intrinsically worse" (§4.4).
- evidence: mean defects 3.23 against 2.34, a 38.0% increase (Mann-Whitney p < 10^-300, Cliff's delta = +0.28, small); defect-free 5.0% against 8.8%. Behavioural safety defects (R5) 18.9% against 8.2% (2.3x, chi-square 2,209, phi = 0.13); portability (R6) 12.8% against 4.6% (2.8x) (§4.4, Figure 4). At least 14.4% of all skills carry a marker, "a conservative lower bound" (§4.1).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the authors state the confounds: only some generators self-disclose, sloppier authors may ship the marker, some platforms stamp markers; a propensity-matched analysis is left to future work (§4.4). The unmarked group contains unmarked AI output. Marker shows how a file was made, not how well it routes.

### F5 · observation · skill-size-and-defects · E3
- practice: defect counts by skill size, and co-occurrence of defects (§4.4).
- claim: defect density rises with skill size; defects cluster across categories (§4.4).
- evidence: Spearman rho = 0.508 between size and defects; skills over 500 lines average 4.76 defects, skills under 50 lines 1.48. 33.0% of defective skills are confined to one category; safety and portability defects co-occur at 2.19x the chance rate (§4.4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: size and detector count are not independent: R2.1 (over 500 lines), R3.1 and R3.2 (inline code, examples) are size-driven detectors, so some of the correlation is built in. No task outcome. Not assigned, so E3.

### F6 · observation · skill-defects-by-platform · E3
- practice: defects compared across platforms inferred from file paths (§4.1, §4.4).
- claim: "statistically significant quality variation across platforms" (§4.4).
- evidence: Kruskal-Wallis H = 594.17, p < 10^-125. Cursor skills 1.55 average defects and 13.5% defect-free; OpenClaw marketplace skills 2.29 and 3.1%. Cursor differs from Generic, OpenClaw and Claude Code (Bonferroni-corrected Mann-Whitney, all adjusted p < 10^-34). Only 41.7% of skills can be attributed to a platform; 58.3% sit in generic `skills/` paths and are excluded (§4.1, §6). Codex, Claude Code and Gemini CLI values are in Figure 3 only.
- effect: mixed
- reader: no direction is claimed for a practice; one platform is best and another worst on the same measure, hence `mixed` (see the schema misfit in the final message). Platform is inferred from paths, and curation model and AI-marker rate are confounded with it (§4.4).

### F7 · practice · lint-gate-for-skill-files · E2
- practice: a three-rule deterministic linter (G1 description of at least 30 characters with "Use when [trigger]", G4 no name repeated as H1 heading, G7 externalise code and examples) run before repair or deployment; evaluated by simulating 12 cumulative linter configurations on the dataset's detected defects (§5, Figure 5, Table 2).
- claim: "a clear elbow at three guidelines"; a lightweight linter catches most defect instances (§5).
- evidence: G1 alone covers 33.4% of observed defect instances; adding G4 and G7 raises coverage to 71.9% and flags 85.9% of skills. All 12 guidelines cover 99.4% of defect instances. Adding G9 and G10 lifts coverage of critical defects (routing plus safety) from 74.7% to 98.6% (§5). The guideline order is by marginal coverage, not by Table 2's phase order (Figure 5 caption).
- effect: helps
- reader: the simulation measures how much of the detectors' own output the same rules cover, so it is close to circular; no run with agents or authors using the linter, and no task outcome. E2. The guidelines are the detectors renamed.

### F8 · practice · llm-repair-of-skill-defects · E2
- practice: give an LLM the detected defect list and the relevant guidelines and ask it to repair the skill (GPT-4o-mini, 200 defective skills) (§5 Stage 3, §6).
- claim: structural defects are repaired reliably, content-reduction defects are not, and some safety defects are not repaired, which motivates safety checks as non-bypassable gates after repair; heavily defective skills "should be regenerated rather than repaired" (§5).
- evidence: 58.4% of detected defects fixed while 0.06 new detected defects per skill are introduced; 0% fix rate for bypass (R5.2) and prompt-injection (R5.3) defects, on small per-category subsamples (§5, §6). The authors use the per-category numbers "only to motivate the safety-gating stage" (§6).
- effect: mixed
- reader: no comparison arm (no human repair, no repair without the defect list), so E2; "fixed" means the same detectors no longer fire, which a rewrite can achieve without keeping the skill useful. One small model, one run. The four-stage workflow the paper proposes (spec-aware prompting, linting, repair, safety gating) was not evaluated as a whole.

### F9 · observation · skill-defects-in-github-issues · E2
- practice: 761 relevant issues from 12 agent-platform repositories, found with 28 skill-related queries (6,233 filtered down), classified by defect category (§3.1, §5).
- claim: the defect categories correspond to failures that users report (§3.2, §5).
- evidence: 303 of 761 issues fit R1 to R7. Routing 69, body or content 75, safety 62 plus 108 security-scan disputes, scope or persona 12 (§5). Of 108 security-scan false-positive appeals on ClawHub, 63 (58.3%) were triggered by credential patterns in legitimate security-auditing skills (§5). Cases named: skills that never activate despite matching descriptions, 28 plugin skills totalling 34K tokens with 4+ minute cold starts, published skills shipping live API keys (§5).
- effect: hurts
- reader: counts of reported issues, no comparison, so E2. 458 of the 761 issues are not classified and the classifier is not described in the parts read. The taxonomy was cross-validated against the same issue set that built it (§3.2), so the match is partly circular. Issues over-represent visible failures.

### F10 · observation · project-specific-knowledge-in-skills · E1
- practice: manual reading of 419 skills with zero detected defects among 2,406 skills from repositories with at least 10,000 GitHub stars (§4.5).
- claim: exemplary skills "encode local procedural knowledge (specific to a repository, framework, or service) rather than reproducing tutorial content that an LLM could already generate on demand" (§4.5).
- evidence: two styles shown by one case each: React's verify skill (24 lines, imperative commands and common mistakes) and Discourse's service-authoring skill (220 lines, eight-phase workflow with gate conditions and audit checklists) (§4.5). No count of how many of the 419 show the trait.
- effect: helps
- reader: the authors call it "a qualitative observation" and not a quantified finding (§4.5). The group is defined by detector pass in popular repositories, with no task outcome, so "high quality" is not measured. Traits 1, 2, 3 and 5 are definitional under the detectors, as the authors state (§4.5).

## Limits stated by the authors

- Detectors are regexes; R5.3 (prompt injection) and R7.1 (persona redefinition) can fire on legitimate security-audit and role skills; a precision audit of the safety detectors was not run; per-category R5 and R7 prevalence is an upper bound (§6).
- The 91.8% bundles cosmetic and severe defects; the 89.3% Tier 1 number is reported throughout so the headline is not read as "92% of skills are broken" (§6).
- BM25 is not production routing; the gap may shrink under an LLM selector; no end-to-end task benchmark for R2 to R7 (§3.4, §6).
- Defect counts are static-analysis findings, not evidence of task-time failure; deviations from the recommended format can work in practice (§6).
- The repair experiment uses GPT-4o-mini and 200 skills; per-category fix rates rest on small subsamples (§6).
- Platform and AI-marker analyses are associations; the authoring process is not observed; 58.3% of skills cannot be attributed to a platform; data is public GitHub and one registry; the top 5 repositories hold 23.5% of skills (§4.4, §6).
- The "conversation-first provenance" explanation (skills saved from chat sessions) is labelled a hypothesis and is untested (§5).
- The specification is evolving (§6).

## Reader's notes

- The title asks what keeps skills from being reusable, but reuse is never measured in a second task, platform or session. The outcomes are detector counts and one lexical retrieval test.
- The 12 guidelines in Table 2 are the 31 detectors regrouped, then validated against the same detectors (F7). Nothing in the paper tests whether following G1 to G12 raises task success; the cited SkillsBench result (curated skills +16.2 points, self-generated skills no benefit, §1, §2.2) is another group's.
- Defect prevalence is by file, deduplicated by SHA-256 only; near-duplicates and one prolific repository (17,284 skills, 12.9%) are in the aggregate. Per-repository statistics are said to be reported (§3.1) but none appear in the parts read.
- The authors designed the taxonomy, the detectors, the guidelines and the evaluation of all of them, so independence is false for the practice findings (F7, F8) even though the skills studied are other people's.
- Crawl dates are not stated; the specification and documentation were retrieved on 2026-05-04 (References).

## Discrepancies

- Table 1's group sizes do not add up to the 20,000 sampled skills: 6,514 + 13,226 = 19,740 for metadata queries and 6,505 + 13,331 = 19,836 for name/path queries (reader's arithmetic). The two query types have different group sizes for the same skills. Not explained in the parts read.
- Figure 2 gives 36.9% for R3 and 16.7% for R4; Table 2 gives 37.0% for G7 (R3.1 to R3.3) and 16.8% for G8 (R4.1 to R4.4), both labelled "affected". Rounding is the likely cause.
- The metadata-query result of Table 1 is described as holding "under the stricter name/path-only query proxy" (§4.3), but the 24.3% and 26.7% hit@1 there are about a third of the metadata-query values, so the two probes differ in difficulty, not in strictness of the same test; the text does not say how the name/path query is built.
