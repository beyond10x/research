## Question

Can one planning intervention, placed at the agent's first source-code edit, steer a coding agent
as well as repeated monitoring does, at lower cost, both during a live run and on a recorded failed
run (§1, §4)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · plan-before-first-edit · E4
- practice: PAUSE online in SWE-agent. When the Executor (Qwen3-coder) issues its first file-editor request on a source-code path, the harness blocks it. A separate Planner (GPT-5.6 Sol, no tools, one call) reads the task and the transcript so far and returns one repair plan (likely root cause, code to change, intended repair). The plan is appended as a user message and the same Executor resumes with its history and workspace. The interceptor is then off (§3.2, §3.3). Compared with the unmodified Executor on all tasks (§4.2).
- claim: "PAUSE improves resolution on both benchmarks while reducing Executor token use" (§4.2).
- evidence: Table 3. Verified, 251/500 (50.2%) to 325/500 (65.0%), +14.8 points, 74 more tasks. Multilingual, 85/300 (28.3%) to 129/300 (43.0%), +14.7 points, 44 more tasks, p = 4 × 10−7. Executor tokens 595.5M to 564.2M on Verified and 469.3M to 459.9M on Multilingual; Planner adds 9.0M and 8.5M tokens (§4.2). Planner cost $26.13 and $23.68; Executor cost not counted (§4.1.2). The abstract writes these gains as "16.8%", "14.7%": they are percentage points.
- effect: helps
- reader: an A/B against the unmodified Executor on the same tasks, so E4. One attempt per task at temperature 0 (Table 2). The authors built and evaluated PAUSE and also re-implemented its rivals. The Planner (GPT-5.6 Sol) is a much stronger model than the Executor (Qwen3-coder 30B-A3B), so the result is a strong model planning for a weak one. Benchmarks are public and may be in a Planner's training data (§5.2.1). Score is the benchmark's tests only. The first-edit boundary is a repository path rule (§5.2.3).

### F2 · practice · plan-before-first-edit · E4
- practice: the same online PAUSE in three harnesses (SWE-agent, Pi, OpenHands) with Qwen3-coder and GPT-5.6 Sol fixed, each against its own unmodified Executor on 500 Verified tasks (§4.4).
- claim: "PAUSE improves resolution in all three harnesses"; "gains persist without explicit think content, while their magnitude and the effect on Executor tokens vary with the harness" (§4.4).
- evidence: Table 6. SWE-agent 50.2 to 65.0 (+14.8); Pi 185/500 (37.0) to 215/500 (43.0), +6.0; OpenHands 227/500 (45.4) to 311/500 (62.2), +16.8. OpenHands: 106 tasks gained, 22 lost, p = 2 × 10−14. Planner cost $0.037 to $0.052 per task, Planner tokens under 2% of Executor tokens. Executor tokens fall 5.3% in SWE-agent and rise 4.9% in OpenHands and Pi. OpenHands strata: 20.6% to 40.4% on the 223 recorded failures in the offline cohort, 16.3% to 44.9% on 49 other failures, 75.9% to 87.3% on 228 recorded successes (39 gained, 13 lost).
- effect: helps
- reader: as F1. Same authors, same Planner. In Pi the gain is smallest; the authors suggest, without measuring, that the Executor sometimes edited source through shell commands before its first edit call, so no plan was inserted (§4.4, §5.1). Executor-token effect has the opposite sign in two of three harnesses, so the F1 saving is not general.

### F3 · practice · rule-triggered-advisor · E4
- practice: LivePlan, as run by the authors with GPT-5.6 Sol as advisor: a deterministic monitor detects repetition, stagnation or skipped phases and then requests corrective guidance, repeatedly. Same Executor, SWE-agent, all tasks, matched Executor budgets (§2.2, §4.2).
- claim: PAUSE "achieves higher observed resolution at similar Planner cost" than LivePlan, "a gain of 3.2 percentage points that is not statistically significant" (§4.2).
- evidence: Table 3. LivePlan against the unmodified Executor: Verified 309/500 (61.8), +11.6; Multilingual 118/300 (39.3), +11.0. PAUSE against LivePlan: Verified 325 against 309, p = 0.11; Multilingual 129 against 118, +3.7 points, p = 0.18. Planner calls per task 0.93 for PAUSE against 2.33 for LivePlan; Planner tokens 9.0M against 9.2M, cost $26.13 against $26.10 on Verified; Multilingual $23.68 against $16.41. Executor tokens 7.6% lower for PAUSE on Multilingual (459.9M against 497.5M).
- effect: helps
- reader: the effect is LivePlan's own, against the unmodified Executor, on equal tasks: E4. The direct PAUSE against LivePlan difference is not significant on either benchmark, so it is a tie at this n. The authors implemented LivePlan themselves, with a 3,000-token advisor limit against PAUSE's 8,192 (Table 2). Authors evaluating their own system against a re-implementation.

### F4 · practice · periodic-critic-feedback · E4
- practice: SICA overseer, as run by the authors with GPT-5.6 Sol: a model reviews the trajectory on a schedule and decides whether to interject. Same Executor, SWE-agent, all tasks (§2.2, §4.2).
- claim: "A single intervention can thus provide repair benefits comparable to repeated oversight with far less planning and execution" (§4.2).
- evidence: Table 3. SICA against the unmodified Executor: Verified 338/500 (67.6), +17.4; Multilingual 133/300 (44.3), +16.0. Against PAUSE: +2.6 points (13 tasks) on Verified, p = 0.18; +1.3 points (133 against 129) on Multilingual, p = 0.71. SICA uses 21 times the Planner calls, Planner cost $231.71 against $26.13 (8.9 times) and Executor tokens 1,056.4M against 564.2M (1.9 times) on Verified; $159.63 against $23.68 (6.7 times) and 1,003.5M against 459.9M (2.2 times) on Multilingual.
- effect: mixed
- reader: higher resolution than the baseline and higher than PAUSE by a non-significant margin, at several times the cost, so `mixed` (better resolution, worse cost). SICA's Executor tokens are 77% above the 595.5M baseline (reader's arithmetic from Table 3); the paper does not say why. Re-implementation by the authors of the rival.

### F5 · practice · planner-model-strength · E4
- practice: at one restored checkpoint (last think before the first source-code edit) of recorded OpenHands failures, vary who writes the plan: no plan, the Executor's own model as a separate Planner, and four other Planner models. Same history, workspace and remaining budget (§4.3).
- claim: "The choice of Planner matters"; "Introducing a planning role therefore does not by itself ensure a large recovery gain" (§4.3).
- evidence: Table 4, recovered failures out of 223 (Qwen3-coder) and 174 (Devstral-small). No plan 9 (4.0%) and 14 (8.0%). Executor model as Planner 12 (5.4%) and 20 (11.5%). Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite 22 (9.9%) and 12 (6.9%). Gemini 3.8 Flash 31 (13.9%) and 29 (16.7%). GPT-5.6 Sol 79 (35.4%) and 53 (30.5%).
- effect: mixed
- reader: the manipulated variable is the Planner on identical checkpoints: E4. No significance test is stated for Table 4. One run per cell. On Devstral, Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite recovers fewer (12) than no plan (14), which the text does not mention. Only the strongest model gives a large gain, so what the data shows is the effect of one strong Planner, not of planning as such.

### F6 · practice · planner-model-strength · E4
- practice: in the full-benchmark offline run on the 178 originally solved tasks that could be replayed under both Planners, count regressions with a weak Planner (Qwen3-30B-Thinking), with GPT-5.6 Sol, and with a no-plan resume as control (§4.3).
- claim: "weaker guidance can steer the Executor away from repairs it would otherwise complete"; "GPT-5.6 Sol guidance recovers failed runs without breaking more successful runs than continuation alone" (§4.3).
- evidence: regressions 10 of 178 (5.6%) with GPT-5.6 Sol, 26 (14.6%) with Qwen3-30B-Thinking (p < 0.01 between Planners), 10 with no plan (p = 0.81 against Sol). Of the other 50 originally solved tasks, 13 never request a source-code edit and 37 could not be replayed (§4.3).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the hurt is for the weak Planner (16 more regressions than no plan). The no-plan control shows that resumption alone produces as many regressions as the strong Planner, so resume itself is not free. One run per task, so small differences may be noise (§5.2.1).

### F7 · practice · replan-without-new-observation · E4
- practice: at the same restored checkpoint, flag the selected think as a possible cause of failure and ask the Executor to reconsider (self-reconsideration), with no plan from a Planner (§4.3).
- claim: "a cue to revisit the selected reasoning yields limited recovery in this evaluation" (§4.3).
- evidence: Table 4. Recovered 13 of 223 (5.8%) and 17 of 174 (9.8%), against no plan 9 (4.0%) and 14 (8.0%), and Sol plan 79 (35.4%) and 53 (30.5%).
- effect: none
- reader: E4 by design, but no test is stated for 13 against 9; the difference is 4 tasks. One run per cell.

### F8 · practice · offline-plan-injection-into-failed-run · E4
- practice: offline PAUSE. Take a recorded failed OpenHands trajectory with an explicit think before the first source-code edit, rebuild the history, replay the prefix in a fresh container to restore the workspace, pass a fidelity check, append a plan from GPT-5.6 Sol and resume the same Executor with the remaining budget. Compared with resuming with no plan and with the unmodified Executor over 500 tasks, where recoveries are added and observed regressions subtracted (§3.4, §4.1.2, §4.3).
- claim: offline PAUSE "can make productive use of a failed investigation by supplying new repair guidance" (§4.3).
- evidence: Table 4: recovers 79 of 223 eligible Qwen3-coder failures (35.4%) and 53 of 174 Devstral-small failures (30.5%), against 9 and 14 with no plan. Table 5: net resolution 291/500 (58.2%) against the baseline 228/500 (45.6%), +12.6 points. SHERLOC (diagnostic pass, Qwen3-30B-Thinking planning) 211/500 (42.2%), and PAUSE with the same Planner 217/500 (43.4%); both below the baseline. Eligible cohort: 223 of the Qwen3-coder failures; 49 failures were excluded from replay (§4.4).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4 for the no-plan control at the same checkpoint. The 58.2% against 45.6% is a before-and-after on the same tasks with regressions subtracted, so it is a conservative net. Eligibility depends on a think action and a faithful replay, so the cohort is not all failures (§5.2.2). The result is for GPT-5.6 Sol only; with the weak Planner offline PAUSE is below the baseline (F6). The 12.6 points are for a 30B Executor and a frontier Planner.

### F9 · practice · state-preserving-restart-vs-cold-restart · E4
- practice: with the same GPT-5.6 Sol plan, resume the recorded failed run from its restored history and workspace with the remaining budget, against restart from the initial repository with the full budget (§4.3).
- claim: "resuming uses 33–41% fewer Executor tokens than restarting at full-benchmark scale" with similar resolution (§4.3).
- evidence: on the 223 failures, 79 recovered with resume and 81 with restart; restart needs 2.0 times the Executor calls (6,020 more) and 22% more Executor tokens (56.1M). Table 5, 500 tasks: Sol resume 291/500 (58.2%) with 347.4M Executor tokens against restart 293/500 (58.6%) with 519.1M, a saving of 171.7M (33%). Qwen3-30B-Thinking: resume 217 and 375.7M against restart 207 and 641.6M, a saving of 265.9M (41%). Including the original recorded run, Sol resume uses 1,139.3M Executor tokens against SHERLOC's 703.9M.
- effect: helps
- reader: the saving counts only the continuation; the recorded failed run is a sunk cost the paper excludes and then adds back in one comparison (§4.3, §5.1). Resume and restart differ in both retained state and remaining budget, which the authors state. Full-benchmark savings are for one Executor (Qwen3-coder).

### F10 · practice · plan-insertion-timing · E4
- practice: restore the same recorded failures at four other points and give each a GPT-5.6 Sol plan from its own prefix, with the same number of fresh Executor iterations: after 20% of recorded actions, immediately before the first source-code edit, after 90% of actions, and at a later source-code edit (§4.5).
- claim: "no alternative pause position ... recovers more tasks than the default offline checkpoint, and an early cut shows a trend toward fewer recoveries" (§4.5).
- evidence: Table 7, 161 tasks graded in every arm: PAUSE checkpoint 56 (34.8%); 20% cut 43 (26.7%); immediately before the edit 50 (31.1%); 90% cut 50 (31.1%). Paired against PAUSE, only-arm against only-PAUSE tasks: 20% cut 15 against 26 (207 tasks, p = 0.12); immediate pre-edit cut 14 against 20 (196 tasks, p = 0.39); 90% cut 16 against 20 (167 tasks, p = 0.62). A later source-code edit: 15 recovered at each checkpoint on 29 paired runs (17 of 46 candidates failed restoration).
- effect: none
- reader: E4 by design, but none of the contrasts is significant, and the authors say similar counts "do not establish equivalence" (§5.2.4). The effect is therefore `none` at this n, although the paper's framing favours the first-edit point. Each arm's plan differs, so the cut is tested together with its plan. Qwen3-coder on Verified only. The 20% and 90% cuts depend on run length, unknown in a live run (§5.2.3).

### F11 · practice · reasoning-text-in-planner-input · E4
- practice: remove the Executor's explicit think content from the Planner's input in OpenHands (the only harness with a think tool), online (§4.4).
- claim: "reads, commands, and their outputs give the Planner sufficient evidence for a useful plan" (§4.4).
- evidence: Table 6. Think stripped 318/500 (63.6%, +18.2) against 311/500 (62.2%, +16.8) with the full transcript; Planner cost $18.79 against $20.38. The two variants differ on 71 tasks, split 39 to 32, p = 0.48.
- effect: none
- reader: one run each. The claim that other harnesses without think tools gain is cross-harness and confounded with the harness.

### F12 · practice · successful-run-guidance-donor · E2
- practice: insert pre-edit reasoning from a successful run of the same task as "donor guidance" into a failed trajectory at the boundary, three attempts per donor and recipient pair (§4.5).
- claim: "Successful continuations remain possible at the pre-edit boundary" (§4.5).
- evidence: at least one success in three attempts for 64 of 97 pairs (66.0%), all three for 41 (42.3%); the rate of at least one success ranges from 58.8% to 69.0% across the four donor and recipient combinations (§4.5). No comparison condition.
- effect: helps
- reader: no control, so E2. The donor guidance comes from a successful run on the same task, so it carries the solution; the number bounds what is possible, not what a Planner delivers. The paper says this ("assesses the possibility").

### F13 · observation · planning-gain-by-task-difficulty-and-language · E3
- practice: none applied; the SWE-agent results of F1, F3 and F4 are split by task difficulty on Verified (easy under 15 min n=194, medium 15 to 60 min n=261, hard 1 h or more n=45) and by language on Multilingual (§4.2, Figures 3 and 4).
- claim: PAUSE gains on hard tasks and in eight of nine languages; Rust is the exception, shared by all three methods (§4.2).
- evidence: hard Verified tasks, 11.1% baseline, PAUSE 26.7%, SICA 17.8%, Planner cost $3.06 against $29.53. SICA leads PAUSE by 1.5 points on easy and 5.4 on medium tasks at 8.3 and 9.0 times the Planner cost. Rust: PAUSE 16 of 43 against baseline 19; LivePlan 18 and SICA 17. Ruby, PHP and JavaScript add 14, 10 and 7 tasks, 31 of the 44 added. C++ (12) and TypeScript (10) are too small to read.
- effect: mixed
- reader: post-hoc subgroups, no test stated; the 45-task hard group is small, and 26.7% against 17.8% is 12 against 8 tasks (reader's arithmetic). Gains by language are counts of 1 to 14 tasks.

## Limits stated by the authors

- The Planner cannot inspect the workspace or run commands, so it can propose hypotheses it cannot check (§5.1).
- One plan only; it is not refreshed when new test results arrive (§5.1).
- Needs a harness that exposes the first source-code edit request before execution and a path rule for the repository; trajectories that change source through shell commands get no intervention (§5.1).
- Offline recovery needs a recorded trajectory with a think step and a faithfully restorable state; the original run's cost remains part of the total (§5.1).
- The benchmarks are public and may be in a Planner's training data; checks cover direct exposure only (§5.2.1).
- Only Verified and Multilingual; the pause-position ablation is offline, Qwen3-coder and Verified only; offline results cover only eligible failures (§5.2.2).
- The first source-code edit rests on an action type and a benchmark-specific path rule (§5.2.3).
- One attempt per task for the main comparisons; the RQ4 paired comparisons use 167 to 207 tasks and none reaches 0.05; "similar observed recovery counts do not establish equivalence" (§5.2.4).

## Reader's notes

- Every effect rests on one Executor family: Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B, with Devstral-Small-2507 only in the offline recovery table. The Planner in every headline number is GPT-5.6 Sol. Both Gemini Planners and the Executor-model Planner give much smaller or no recovery (F5). The result is "a frontier model planning for a weak one", not "single planning for agents".
- The paper does not report whether the Executor follows the plan or ignores it, or what the plan says, only resolution and tokens.
- Executor cost is not counted in any dollar figure (§4.1.2); the Executor-token counts are reported separately.
- The observation that the first source-code edit has a median of 60.0% of trajectory progress, in 476 of 500 Qwen3-coder trajectories, 80.5% after the midpoint, 24 runs without an edit, and file reads 31% of actions before it (§1, Figure 1), is a prevalence with no claimed effect and is not entered as a finding.
- Rust is the only language where all three guidance methods fall below the baseline, and the paper gives no cause.
- The reference list names the PAUSE authors' own earlier work (SWE-Manager, arXiv:2601.22956, same group).

## Discrepancies

- The abstract and §1 write the gains as "16.8%", "14.7%", "3.2%", "3.7%" and "improves resolution by 14.8%"; §4 gives them as percentage points (Table 3, Table 6).
- §4.2 and the abstract call the Planner cost against LivePlan "similar"; on Multilingual Table 3 gives $23.68 against $16.41, 44% higher (reader's arithmetic) and 8.5M against 5.9M Planner tokens.
- §1 says "Our experiments confirm" that earlier intervention gives too little to ground a plan and later intervention must overturn the agent's own changes. §4.5 finds none of the three cuts significantly different from PAUSE (p = 0.12, 0.39 and 0.62) and §5.2.4 calls the early-cut result a trend.
