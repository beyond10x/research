## Question

Can a main agent with two interacting subagents, a reviewer and a critic of that review, improve
agentic coding and code review over single-agent refinement and larger multi-agent teams, and what
goes wrong when the two agents interact (§1)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · reviewer-critic-pair-before-edit · E4
- practice: Adversarial Review (AR): the main agent writes the artifact, a reviewer subagent reviews it, a fresh critic subagent audits the review, the two exchange review text (cap 5 inner rounds) while the artifact is frozen, and the main agent edits only once the review is consistent; stops at once if both agree there is no flaw. On LiveCodeBench it is compared with the same tasks under five other methods built up one step at a time (§3.5, Figure 1).
- claim: AR "achieves the highest pass rate among tested methods, outperforming a five-agent baseline while using only three agents" (Abstract, §3.5).
- evidence: Table 1, pass over 105 tasks and pass-on-hard over 57: AR 87% and 43/57 (75%) with 3 agents; MARS 82% and 39/57 (68%) with 5 agents; Single-reviewer 77% and 36/57; Zero-shot and Self-Refine 77% and 35/57; Two-reviewers 75% and 34/57. AR against MARS +5 points (§3.5). In tasks, AR against MARS is 5 over all and 4 on hard (reader's arithmetic).
- effect: helps
- reader: removing the critic gives the Single-reviewer method, so the comparison is an ablation on equal tasks, E4. One run per method is implied and no variance or seed is given; the authors themselves call 75% to 77% "within noise" (§3.3), and AR's lead over MARS is 5 tasks. The authors built and chose AR on this benchmark, so the win is on the data it was developed on. Authors evaluate their own method.

### F2 · practice · self-review-prompts · E4
- practice: Self-Refine: the same agent critiques its own code and revises it, against zero-shot generation on LiveCodeBench (§3.1).
- claim: "the improvements are barely noticeable"; the critic and the generator are the same model "making the same mistakes" (§3.1).
- evidence: Table 1: Self-Refine 77% and 35/57 hard, Zero-shot 77% and 35/57 hard.
- effect: none
- reader: identical counts in both columns, one run, 105 tasks; the paper tests one self-critique prompt (A.1.4, not read), so this says nothing about other self-review prompts. Same-model critic is the stated mechanism and was not tested against a different-model critic.

### F3 · practice · fresh-context-reviewer-agent · E4
- practice: a separate model call reviews the main agent's code once and the main agent edits once (Single-reviewer), against Zero-shot on LiveCodeBench (§3.2).
- claim: "Performance stays in the same cluster as Zero-shot"; one reviewer "can approve everything without checking, or flag many small nits" (§3.2).
- evidence: Table 1: Single-reviewer 77% and 36/57 hard, Zero-shot 77% and 35/57.
- effect: none
- reader: one hard task apart, one run. The high-variance explanation of the reviewer is the authors' hypothesis and no per-review data is given in the parts read.

### F4 · practice · multi-agent-review-ensemble · E4
- practice: add independent reviewers whose reviews are merged. Two-reviewers: R1 and R2 review independently and the main agent edits on the union (§3.3). MARS: three parallel reviewers with a structured accept or reject output and a meta-reviewer, two rounds at most, 5 agents (§3.4). Compared on LiveCodeBench with Single-reviewer, and on SWE-PRBench and SWE-bench Verified with the other methods.
- claim: "adding independent reviewers alone does not reliably improve LCB"; MARS "is the first method whose pass-rate is clearly higher" but "the gain is small relative to the token cost" of five agents (§3.4, §7).
- evidence: LiveCodeBench, Table 1: Two-reviewers 75% and 34/57 against Single-reviewer 77% and 36/57; MARS 82% and 39/57. SWE-PRBench, Table 2 (F1, N = 100): Two-reviewers 0.503, MARS 0.501, Single-reviewer 0.495. SWE-bench Verified, Table 3 (N = 500): MARS 72.6% against Zero-shot 71.6%.
- effect: mixed
- reader: Two-reviewers is flat to slightly lower on LiveCodeBench; MARS gains 5 points there and about 0.006 F1 and 1.0 point (5 of 500 tasks, reader's arithmetic) on the other two, with no spread given. Mixed is the reader's reading of three small, unreplicated comparisons. The text's description of MARS in §3.4 does not match Table 1 (see Discrepancies). The authors implemented the baselines.

### F5 · practice · reviewer-critic-pair-before-edit · E4
- practice: the AR reviewer-critic loop as in F1, applied unchanged to review-only work on SWE-PRBench (R and C, with the critic choosing AGREE or DISAGREE), against Single-reviewer, Two-reviewers and MARS on the same 100 PR diffs (§4.1, §4.2).
- claim: "AR's LCB win does not transfer naively": naive AR is "the lowest of the four methods in the subset" (§4, §4.2).
- evidence: Table 2, F1 over comments matched to human review comments by a GPT-5.2 judge (kappa 0.75 against human annotators): AR 0.457, Single-reviewer 0.495, MARS 0.501, Two-reviewers 0.503; N = 100 each.
- effect: hurts
- reader: the loss is 0.038 F1 against the single reviewer, one run, no interval. The judge rewards similarity to human comments and may penalise valid comments that differ (§7). Ablation on equal PRs, so E4.

### F6 · observation · false-consensus-between-agents · E1
- practice: two cases from SWE-PRBench where AR's reviewer and critic agreed without enough evidence (§4.3). Case A: the reviewer writes one real bug and two to three hedged concerns, the critic agrees with all and adds another speculative bug, and the judge marks 3 of 5 comments as fabricated (AR F1 = 0.250). Case B: the reviewer approves, the critic raises a real concern that the authors checked by hand, the reviewer rebuts with a confident argument that cites no code, the critic flips to AGREE and the real bug is dropped (AR F1 = 0.286, MARS F1 = 0.667 on that PR).
- claim: "when two LLM agents are asked to agree on a joint output, they tend to agree with each other. They do not always find the truth"; the authors call this false consensus and say it is structural (§4.5, §7).
- evidence: the two cases above (§4.3), no count of how often either pattern occurs.
- effect: hurts
- reader: two hand-picked cases, no rate. The claim of a structural mechanism is the authors' reading of the traces. This is the paper's reason for F7.

### F7 · practice · evidence-typed-critic-verdict · E4
- practice: a prompt change to AR only, same agents and wiring: the critic must answer AGREE, DISAGREE EVIDENCE (cites code that contradicts a flag) or DISAGREE CONCERN (an objection that cites no contradicting code); the reviewer must keep, revise or drop the flag according to the verdict, and on a CONCERN must cite code that confirms or refutes the bug (§4.4).
- claim: "AR with text constraint has F1 = 0.533, the highest among the four-method subset"; "the protocol must include explicit pushback" (§4.5).
- evidence: Table 2, F1, N = 100: AR with text constraint 0.533, against naive AR 0.457, Two-reviewers 0.503, MARS 0.501, Single-reviewer 0.495.
- effect: helps
- reader: an ablation of the prompt on equal PRs, E4. The prompt was written after reading the failures of naive AR on this same benchmark (§4.3, §4.4) and only one iteration was run, so the 0.533 is a tuned result on the evaluation set, and the 0.03 to 0.04 lead over the other methods has no interval. The variant was not run on LiveCodeBench or SWE-bench Verified (A.3).

### F8 · practice · reviewer-critic-pair-before-edit · E4
- practice: AR written as a plain-text SKILL.md that Claude Code follows itself, inlined in the task prompt; the agent decides when to start R and C as Task subagents and when to edit. The concrete workflow writes a plan (`APPROACH.md`), reviews the plan, implements, then reviews the diff, each review with at most 5 inner and 2 outer rounds; all sub-agents on Sonnet 4.5. Compared with Zero-shot (issue prompt only) and MARS (as a skill) on 500 SWE-bench Verified issues, about 30 minutes wall-clock each (§5.1, A.3, A.3.5, A.3.6). The baseline two-verdict AR is used, not the text-constraint variant (A.3).
- claim: "AR also shows improvements over the baselines on repository-level coding tasks" and "still improves performance when expressed only as a SKILL.md protocol" (Abstract, §7).
- evidence: Table 3, pass@1 through the official Docker harness, N = 500: AR 75.2%, MARS 72.6%, Zero-shot 71.6%. AR against Zero-shot is 18 tasks and against MARS 13 tasks (reader's arithmetic).
- effect: helps
- reader: the Zero-shot arm is the control on equal tasks, E4. One run per arm and no interval; 3.6 points is within what rerunning an agent can move. AR's arm also has a plan-first step and a plan review that neither Zero-shot nor MARS has (MARS reviews the diff only, A.3.3), so the gain cannot be assigned to the reviewer-critic pair alone. Only 3 methods were run because a full run is estimated at over 300 hours each (§5.1). The harness and prompts belong to the authors.

### F9 · practice · reviewer-critic-pair-before-edit · E4
- practice: as F8; the measurement is tokens per task.
- claim: AR "trad[es] off token-efficiency for performance gains"; it uses more tokens than Zero-shot on all three benchmarks and is on the cost-quality Pareto frontier of each (§5.2, §6).
- evidence: SWE-bench Verified, AR "about 4.5×" the tokens of Zero-shot (§5.2). The per-benchmark median-token values are in Figure 2 only; LiveCodeBench token medians are "estimated from per-method call counts" (Figure 2 caption).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the 4.5 times is the only token ratio in the text. "Pareto frontier" is relative to 3 to 6 methods that the authors chose, and no dollar cost or latency is reported. LiveCodeBench token counts are estimates.

### F10 · observation · review-loop-callee-level-repair · E1
- practice: SWE-bench Verified task matplotlib-20826. Zero-shot (45-line patch) and MARS (50-line patch) re-apply tick visibility settings in the caller after `Axis.clear()` has reset them and fail the hidden tests. AR's reviewer made the same mistake in the first round, the critic caught it, and AR patched the callee (`axis.py`, about 20 lines), the only patch that passes (§5.3).
- claim: the reviewer-critic loop "helps the main agent inspect one frame deeper in the call chain, moving from symptom repair to root-cause repair" (§5.3).
- evidence: one task, patch sizes of about 20, 45 and 50 lines (§5.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: one hand-chosen case among 500 with no count of how often AR changed the patch locus. The mechanism is read from one trace.

### F11 · observation · review-loop-scope-creep · E1
- practice: SWE-bench Verified task astropy-14182. Zero-shot passes with a 24-line patch. AR writes a 32-line patch that also removes a class variable and adds a `read()` method; in planning the reviewer asked "what if the user calls read() separately?", the critic did not reject it as out of scope, the main agent implemented it, and the patch fails the hidden tests (§5.3).
- claim: "The reviewer-critic loop can improve root-cause analysis, but it can also turn plausible concerns into unnecessary edits"; this is "the main failure mode of AR on SWE-bench Verified" (§5.3).
- evidence: one task, patch sizes 24 and 32 lines (§5.3).
- effect: hurts
- reader: one case, with no rate; calling it the main failure mode rests on this case and no tally. The authors suggest a scope-checking critic as future work (§7), which is untested.

## Limits stated by the authors

- The three benchmarks do not cover all coding tasks or agent scaffolds; the Python-orchestrated runs and the skills-form run answer different questions (§7).
- SWE-PRBench scoring uses an LLM judge that matches generated comments to human review comments and "may penalize valid comments that differ from the human review" (§7).
- SWE-bench Verified measures hidden-test passing and "does not fully measure maintainability, minimality, or long-term code quality"; larger evaluations are needed to validate AR as a deployed review assistant (§7).
- AR "is not a free improvement": more tokens everywhere, wasted on easy tasks, and extra review may raise over-editing when the scope is narrow (§7).
- The text constraint "may not be the best nor the final solution"; LLMs can forget or ignore instructions as the context fills (§7).
- Claims are "empirical, not formal"; reviewer-critic loops are not claimed to be better than independent reviewers, debate or single-agent refinement in general (§7).
- Only three methods were run on SWE-bench Verified because of run time (§5.1).

## Reader's notes

- No run count, seeds, temperature, confidence intervals or significance tests appear in the parts read. Every comparison is a single table cell. LiveCodeBench differences of 1 to 5 points are 1 to 5 tasks of 105.
- Both AR and its prompt variant were designed on the same benchmarks they are scored on: AR was built step by step on LiveCodeBench "each step motivated by a measurable failure" (§1), and the text constraint came from failures on SWE-PRBench (§4.3, §4.4).
- The LiveCodeBench and SWE-PRBench numbers come from a Python orchestrator that fixes every step; the SWE-bench Verified number comes from an agent that chooses when to call R and C. The paper does not report how often the agent actually ran R and C, or skipped them, in the 500 runs.
- The SWE-bench Verified AR arm adds a written plan and a plan review before any edit (A.3.5). That is a second treatment inside the same arm.
- Table 2 is headed "review-relevant subset" but N = 100 equals the number of PR diffs in the benchmark (§4.1); the paper does not say how the subset is chosen.
- The baselines (MARS, Single-reviewer, Two-reviewers) are the authors' reimplementations in their own harness and prompts, not the published systems.
- Token cost is reported as a ratio and in a log-scale figure only; no money or wall-clock cost is given. The wrapper sets a 30-minute budget for every arm (A.3.1).
- Prompts are in the appendix, but code and data are not released yet.

## Discrepancies

- §3.4 says MARS gains "+5pp on pass/105 (85%)" and "+3 hard tasks (43/57) over Single-reviewer". Table 1 gives MARS 82% and 39/57, and 43/57 and 87% are AR's row. Over Single-reviewer's 36/57, 39 is +3 and 43 is +7 (reader's arithmetic); 85% would be +8 over 77%.
- The Abstract says AR "achieves the highest pass rate" and §8 says AR "achieves the highest measured score among the methods we test on all three benchmarks". Table 2 has naive AR lowest on SWE-PRBench; the highest there is "AR with text constraint", which was not run on the other two benchmarks (A.3).
- §5.2 says AR uses about 4.5 times the tokens of Zero-shot and refers to "Section 6" for it; Section 6 gives no ratio and only plots medians in Figure 2.
- §3.5 gives the inner-round cap (5) and no outer-round cap; the appendix gives at most 2 outer rounds per phase on SWE-bench Verified (A.3.5).
