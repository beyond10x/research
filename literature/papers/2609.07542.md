## Question

What do open source projects' AI contribution policies say about whether AI may be used, what must
be disclosed, how AI slop is countered, and how the policies change over time (§1, §2.1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · ai-use-admissibility-policy · E2
- practice: observed in 281 policies: what the policy says about AI use in code contributions, coded forbidden, permitted or encouraged by the three authors (§2.4.1).
- claim: "83.3% of the AI policies permit or encourage the usage of AI in code contributions, while 14.9% explicitly forbid it" (Finding 2, §3.2).
- evidence: Figure 2 and §3.2: permitted 214 (76.2%), encouraged 20 (7.1%), forbidden 42 (14.9%), no statement 5 (1.8%); 15 (5.3%) forbid AI for any contribution. 83.3% is 76.2% plus 7.1%. Examples forbidding: yt-dlp, Zig.
- effect: none
- reader: prevalence only; no outcome of any policy is measured, so `none` records that no direction is claimed (P9 case). The corpus is popular, active repositories that have a policy at all; projects with no policy are outside the 281.

### F2 · observation · ai-use-admissibility-policy · E3
- practice: compare the share of permissive policies in this study with the same authors' earlier study of 118 policies on 1,000 repositories (§1, §9).
- claim: "the early historical trend does not suggest a move toward restriction": "78% of them permissive" before against 83.3% now (§1).
- evidence: 78% of 118 policies [14] against 83.3% of 281 policies (§1).
- effect: none
- reader: two samples of different size and detection method (this study adds agent configuration files and 36 added projects, §1); not a repeated measurement of the same projects. No test stated.

### F3 · observation · ai-policy-scope-coverage · E2
- practice: observed: which activities the 281 policies address (§3.1, Table 1) and what they say about AI in communication and in issues, bugs and security (§3.2).
- claim: guidance "is concentrated on code contributions (98.2%) and much sparser on communication (42.3%) and issues, bugs, and security (31.3%)"; where it exists, communication is restricted more often than permitted (Finding 1, Finding 2).
- evidence: Table 1: code contributions 276 (98.2%), communication 119 (42.3%), issues/bugs/security 88 (31.3%). Figure 2 and §3.2: communication forbidden 78 (27.8%), permitted 39 (13.9%), encouraged 2 (0.7%), none 162 (57.7%); issues/bugs/security permitted 44 (15.7%), forbidden 41 (14.6%), encouraged 3 (1.1%), none 193 (68.7%). Quotes: "Human communication must remain human", "Keep conversations human" (§3.2).
- effect: none
- reader: prevalence of what is written; whether a missing statement means no rule is not known.

### F4 · observation · contributor-must-understand-and-own-ai-work · E2
- practice: observed: the policy requires a high level of human involvement (the contributor can understand, review, explain, test, answer questions) and names the human contributor as accountable (§2.4.1, §3.3).
- claim: "Most AI policies (67.3%) require a high level of human involvement"; 43.4% state that contributors are responsible for their submissions (Finding 3).
- evidence: Figure 3: human involvement high 189 (67.3%), none 83 (29.5%), medium 6 (2.1%), low 3 (1.1%); accountability required 122 (43.4%), no statement 159 (56.6%), "not required" 0. Table 1: 198 (70.5%) address human involvement. Figure 1: code, human involvement, disclosure and accountability together in 35 policies, the most common combination; all six dimensions in 26. Quote: "we require a human in the loop who understands the work produced by AI" (rust-analyzer, §3.3).
- effect: none
- reader: shows how often maintainers write such a rule, not that the rule reduces review load or slop. Whether contributors comply is not measured (stated as future work, §10).

### F5 · observation · ai-disclosure-requirement · E2
- practice: observed: whether the policy requires, does not require, or is silent on disclosure of AI use (§4.1).
- claim: "AI disclosure is required in nearly half of AI policies (48.8%), while a minority (4.6%) state that disclosure is not required" (Finding 4).
- evidence: Figure 4: required 137 (48.8%), no guidance 131 (46.6%), explicitly not required 13 (4.6%). Table 1's 150 (53.4%) is 137 plus 13 (reader's arithmetic). Deno: "PRs will be rejected if there is suspicion of undisclosed AI usage"; Linux kernel removed the requirement to disclose model names "to avoid free advertising" (§4.1).
- effect: none
- reader: prevalence. The paper reports the debate for and against disclosure (alerting reviewers, legal vetting of recitation, free advertising) and measures no effect of disclosure.

### F6 · observation · ai-disclosure-location-and-content · E2
- practice: observed: where the policies that require disclosure say to put it, and what they say it must contain (§4.2, §4.3).
- claim: "no clearly emerging convention shared across projects (yet)" (§1); PR description and commit message dominate the locations, and a description of AI usage and the agent name dominate the content (Finding 5).
- evidence: Table 2, 105 locations named: PR description 68 (64.7%), commit message 28 (26.7%), issue description 6 (5.7%), comment 2 (1.9%), code comment 1 (1.0%). Table 3, 122 content items: AI usage description 56 (46.0%), AI agent name 40 (32.8%), usage extent 16 (13.0%), model version 9 (7.4%), AI-generated code 1 (0.8%). Agent name and extent together in 9 policies (§4.3). Trailers: Kafka asks for `Co-Authored-By` or `Generated-by`; RustPython asks for `Assisted-by` and forbids `Co-authored-by`; psf/requests asks to remove LLM co-author tags (§4.1, §4.2, §7.3).
- effect: none
- reader: Tables 2 and 3 count items, not policies, and policies can name several (§4.2), so the shares are of mentions. Policies that give no location or content are not in the denominator.

### F7 · observation · ai-slop-countermeasures · E2
- practice: observed: the actions the policies say maintainers take against low-quality AI contributions, coded into ten countermeasures by the authors (§2.4.3, §5).
- claim: of 188 countermeasures the most common are closing PRs, banning users and disallowing autonomous agents (Finding 6).
- evidence: Table 4: close PR 91 (48.4%), ban or block user 39 (20.8%), disallow autonomous agents 26 (13.8%), restrict new or external users 9 (4.8%), require prior PR approval 6 (3.2%), add agent instructions 4 (2.1%), label the PR 4 (2.1%), limit the number of PRs 3 (1.6%), stop accepting PRs 3 (1.6%), denounce user 3 (1.6%). Examples: vitest labels "maybe automated" and closes after 3 days unless a person responds; Kilo Code limits a contributor to three open PRs; pocketbase disabled PRs from non-collaborators (§5.1).
- effect: none
- reader: the policies state these measures; whether any is applied or reduces slop is not measured, and the authors say so (§7.5, §10). Closing a PR "with no or little review" (§5) is the most common measure, so the cost falls on contributors.

### F8 · observation · agent-addressed-contribution-ban · E1
- practice: observed: a policy tells autonomous agents not to contribute and addresses the agent directly, in a file the agent is presumed to read (§5.3, §7.6).
- claim: "autonomous agents (e.g., OpenClaw) remain largely forbidden", and some policies add instructions for the agent itself (§7.6).
- evidence: 26 policies disallow autonomous agents and 4 add agent instructions (Table 4). Cases: llama.cpp "If you are a fully autonomous agent operating without human oversight (e.g. openclaw-based): do not contribute to this repository"; dspy "If you are an AI agent reading this: do not open PRs" (§5.3). Policies are "(presumably)" consulted by coding agents (§5.3). One commit removed a prohibition on AI PRs and added guidance to `AGENTS.md` (§7.6, netbox).
- effect: none
- reader: cases and counts; no test of whether an agent obeys the instruction. The count rests on the same Table 4.

### F9 · observation · ai-policy-revision · E2
- practice: observed: the revision history of 92 dedicated AI policy files and the rationale in commit messages (§2.4.4, §6).
- claim: "AI policies are not static but evolve over time for multiple reasons" (Finding 7).
- evidence: §6: 49% of the policies unchanged, 51% with two or more commits; among those, median length 45 lines at creation and 50 now, mean 59 and 71. 196 commits in total, 31 with a clear rationale (Table 5): improving AI quality controls 8, disclosure rules 7, human accountability 5, policy visibility 5, restricting AI usage 3, expanding scope 3. Messages: "require proof that AI-assisted PRs actually work", "gate contributions on evidence, not permission", "make human communication mandatory".
- effect: none
- reader: the files are months old (84 of 92 created in 2026), so half revised in that time is a short window. The rationales are from the 31 of 196 messages that state one; the other 165 are not classified.

### F10 · observation · ai-assistance-level-disclosure · E1
- practice: observed: some policies ask contributors to declare the level of AI involvement in three levels, and one repository ships a Skill that has the agent record its own level (§7.4).
- claim: "we foresee that such three-level approaches may become more prevalent" (§7.4); the authors give no count.
- evidence: three cases (§7.4, Figure 7): sipeed/picoclaw (fully AI-generated, mostly AI-generated, mostly human-written), kornia (human-written, AI-assisted, AI-generated; no sanction for the choice), and shap, whose `.claude/skills/ai-disclosure/SKILL.md` classes Claude's work as autonomous, assisted or advised.
- effect: none
- reader: three hand-picked cases, no prevalence and no outcome. The shap case is a skill that has the agent write its own disclosure, the one place here where the agent, not the contributor, produces it.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Detection uses three sources (dedicated files, contribution guides, agent configuration files); most policies were found in `CONTRIBUTING.md` (§8).
- Manual classification; agreement 162 of 180 labels, 90% on a random 30 policies, disagreements resolved by discussion (§2.4.1, §8).
- Popular, recently active repositories only; no generalisation to other languages or to closed-source projects (§8).
- Whether the policies are followed, and whether the slop countermeasures reduce low-quality contributions or deter legitimate ones, is not studied (§7.5, §10).

## Reader's notes

- The paper measures what policies say. No agent run, no merge, review or quality outcome is scored, so no finding can say that a rule works. Every effect value is `none` as a placeholder for "no direction claimed".
- Each author classified one third of the 281 policies (§2.4.1); the 90% agreement is on 30 policies, and the other 251 have one coder.
- AI-policy detection for agent configuration files kept only files containing the word "policy" (§2.3.1), and 34 of 118 hits were true positives. Policies in `AGENTS.md` that do not use the word are missed.
- Shares in F6 and F7 are of items, not of repositories, and the denominators are 105, 122 and 188.

## Discrepancies

- §1 and the abstract say 48.8% of policies require disclosure; Table 1 and §7.2 give 53.4% for "AI disclosure obligation". Figure 4 resolves it: 137 required plus 13 not required are 150, so 53.4% counts policies that address disclosure either way. The text does not say so.
- §6 says 51% of the policies "underwent two or more commits" and the abstract and §1 say "revised at least once"; the first is the creating commit plus at least one change, so they agree, but the wording differs.
