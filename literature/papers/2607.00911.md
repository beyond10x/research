## Question

How are agent skills (`SKILL.md` bundles) written and packaged, what do they contain, and how are they
customised when copied from a public registry into a repository and maintained afterwards (§III)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · registry-skill-adopted-by-copy · E2
- practice: for 2,462 skills in personal repositories that match a skills.sh skill by name and text similarity, the local copy was compared with the registry skill at the adoption version; no treatment was applied (§III-D, §VI-A).
- claim: "adoption largely behaves as a one-time copy"; "reuse is largely a one-time copy operation: most reused skills remain near-verbatim, 53% are never modified after adoption" (§VI-A, abstract).
- evidence: 3,709 linked pairs over 1,673 registry skills (8.5% of the registry), 70.3% of pairs at similarity of at least 0.99 (§III-A). Among the 2,462 pairs of software-engineering skills, 1,841 were near-verbatim and 621 altered (§VI-A). 1,167 of 2,462 (47.4%) had any local change after adoption, against 8,386 of 13,782 (60.8%) of locally authored skills, median 2 follow-up commits; the 53% is 1,295 of 2,462 (reader's arithmetic) (§VI-A). One-year commit window to 2026-05-04.
- effect: none
- reader: counts of a condition nobody assigned, so E2; the 47.4% against 60.8% contrast is between two groups that differ in more than reuse. No outcome (success, defects) is measured, so no direction is claimed; `none` is used for want of a descriptive value. The linkage needs a name match and misses renamed or heavily edited copies, so the near-verbatim share is probably high by construction (§VIII, internal validity: authors say the link count is conservative).

### F2 · observation · upstream-changes-not-pulled-into-copied-skill · E2
- practice: for copied skills, the registry skill's history after the adoption date was compared with the local copy (§VI-A).
- claim: developers "rarely synchronise with upstream revisions" (§VI-A).
- evidence: of 1,295 reused skills never locally updated, 40.2% had their registry counterpart updated at least once after adoption. Of 689 reused skills that were updated locally and whose registry skill also changed, 62.3% (429) diverged independently rather than taking in the upstream change, tested at a 0.99 body-similarity threshold at the first post-adoption upstream update (§VI-A).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the copy falls behind the registry, which the authors treat as a risk because an agent executes stale guidance (§II-B, §VII-B). No case of an executed stale line or a failure is given, so the harm is the authors' inference. The comparison point is the first upstream update only.

### F3 · observation · skill-edits-add-more-than-remove · E3
- practice: each coded key point of a skill change was labelled add, remove or modify, and counted separately for customisation (copied skills) and evolution (locally authored skills, first version against last version up to 2026-05-04) (§III-D, §VII-A).
- claim: "Maintenance is thus predominantly additive, accruing instructions far more than pruning them" (§VII-A).
- evidence: excluding modifications, additions outnumber removals 2.7 to 1 (586 against 221). Customisation 1.1 to 1, evolution 6.1 to 1. Developers mainly add quality-verification steps, constraints and best-practice recommendations; the only frequently deleted content is licences and authorship claims (§VII-A). Hard-enforcement edits are 16.5% of customisation and 27.8% of evolution skills, mostly appended prohibitions (Table III, §VI-B).
- effect: none
- reader: a comparison of two settings in a coded sample (188 and 256 skills), not a controlled one. The evolution diff compares only the first and last version, which the authors say hides intermediate additions, removals and reversals (§III-D, §VIII). No outcome of the growth, such as longer files or worse agent behaviour, is measured, so no direction is claimed.

### F4 · observation · stable-interaction-and-recovery-rules-in-skill · E3
- practice: the content type touched by each key point of a change was recorded against the content categories of F6 and the share of changed skills per category was reported (§VII-A).
- claim: "the instructions that shape a skill's user interaction, runtime monitoring, and failure handling are rarely updated", so adopters and maintainers "inherit these rules almost verbatim"; the authors call this a "behavioural contract" (§VII-A, §VII-B).
- evidence: share of changed skills in customisation and in evolution: interacting with the user 1.6% and 1.2%, runtime-state monitoring 0.5% and 0.0%, handling failures 5.9% and 7.1%. Defining scope and activation 38.8% and 42.0%, documenting tool and script usage 30.3% and 36.1% (§VII-A).
- effect: none
- reader: the content type per key point was aligned by two authors with no Cohen's kappa computed (§III-D). A low edit rate can mean the rules are sound or that nobody checks them; the paper reports no outcome. In the content sample the same categories are common (interacting with the user 63% of 180 skills, handling failures 50%; Table II), so they are widely written and rarely changed. `none` is used because no direction is claimed.

### F5 · observation · customisation-and-evolution-edit-profiles · E3
- practice: 188 sampled copied-and-altered skills (customisation) were coded against 256 sampled locally authored skills (evolution) into 60 codes and 6 themes, and each theme was compared with a two-sided Fisher's exact test, Benjamini-Hochberg correction and Cohen's h (§III-D, Table III).
- claim: "Customisation re-grounds a skill to its host project, whereas evolution adds new inline domain knowledge"; reworking operational specifications and adapting knowledge and resources are the most frequent edit types (abstract, §IX).
- evidence: Table III, customisation against evolution. Reformatting layout 48.9% against 20.4% (h 0.61); repackaging and porting to the host platform 12.2% against 0.0%; managing provenance metadata 17.6% against 5.9%; polishing as a theme 55.9% against 30.6%. Reshaping the workflow 20.2% against 38.4%; revising inline knowledge 32.5% evolution against 19.7%; adapting to renames and tool substitutions 5.3% against 24.3% (h 0.57); supplying activation metadata 19.1% against 33.3%. Reworking operational specifications 56.9% against 61.6%, adapting knowledge and resources 46.3% against 50.2%, metadata 40.4% against 43.1%: no significant difference. Example of customisation: an Android skill replaced Hilt with Koin to match the project stack (§VI-B).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3, two groups compared without a control, with a significance test. The authors say the tests ignore clustering by repository and the dependence among labels, and read the result as patterns in the sample (§III-D). The groups differ in design as well as in setting: a diff at adoption against a first-to-last diff. The Table III percentages for evolution do not match n = 256 (see Discrepancies).

### F6 · observation · skill-content-themes · E2
- practice: thematic analysis of 180 `SKILL.md` documents, 10 per software-engineering knowledge area, yielding 69 codes, 17 subthemes and 6 themes (§III-C, §V-B, Table II).
- claim: a typical skill covers four themes (scope, execution, conduct, domain knowledge) in every area, while output quality and user/agent coordination depend on the area (§V-B).
- evidence: share of the 180 skills per subtheme, Table II. Defining scope and activation 100%; sequencing and branching the workflow 68%; documenting tool and script usage 76%; recommending best practices 77%; constraining and prohibiting 45%; pointing to references 72%; interacting with the user 63%, with consent gates as the example; handling failures and recovering 50%; verifying and evaluating output 33%; delegating and handing off work 24%; monitoring state 23%. Theme totals: scoping 100%, execution lifecycle 89%, output quality 69%, conduct 81%, grounding 85%, coordination 68%. Only half the skills in Software Design, Configuration Management, Security and Engineering Foundations address output quality; coordination ranges from 20% in Security to 100% in Requirements (§V-B).
- effect: none
- reader: the sample is balanced by area, so the shares are not corpus prevalence, as the authors say (§V-B). The areas come from an LLM label. No task outcome is measured; the shares say what skills contain, not what works. `none` is used because no direction is claimed.

### F7 · observation · skill-length-by-channel · E3
- practice: `SKILL.md` token length and Markdown header count of registry skills were compared with personal-use skills (§V-A, Figure 3).
- claim: "Centralised skills are systematically longer and more sectioned than their personal-use counterparts" (§V-A).
- evidence: median 1,678 tokens against 1,114 and 19 headers against 13 (registry against personal); Mann-Whitney U p < 0.001, Cliff's delta 0.23 and 0.25, small effects (§V-A). Level-3 headers in 79.8% of registry skills and 67.9% of personal ones.
- effect: none
- reader: a comparison of two populations that differ in author, purpose and selection (registry skills have at least 20 installs; personal ones come from repositories with at least 10 stars). It says nothing about whether length helps. The effect sizes are small by the authors' own label.

### F8 · observation · skill-spec-conformance · E3
- practice: 17 checkable provisions of Anthropic's public skill specification (7 mandatory, 10 optional) were tested on both populations (§III-C, Table I).
- claim: both populations conform well on mandatory clauses; "looser discipline outside curated marketplaces" on optional ones (§V-A).
- evidence: Table I, registry against personal. Mandatory provisions at 95.04% to 100% in both, name equals directory name 97.65% against 91.23%. Optional fields present in 3.18% to 16.06% (license 16.06% against 9.08%, allowed-tools 14.95% against 12.47%); `references/` 30.99% against 17.00%, `scripts/` 14.42% against 8.37%. Body of at most 500 lines: 84.54% against 93.67%, so registry skills break the recommended length more often.
- effect: mixed
- reader: shares of files that follow a spec, not of skills that work; no skill was run. Mixed because registry skills conform more on directory name and use more optional features, and conform less on body length.

### F9 · observation · skill-topics-by-channel · E3
- practice: each skill was assigned by an LLM (Qwen3.6-27b, temperature 0) to one of 18 SWEBOK knowledge areas or to non-SE, and the distributions of the two channels were compared (§III-B, §IV, Figure 2).
- claim: registry skills lean toward "broadly reusable, general-purpose coding capabilities", personal-use skills toward "project-specific lifecycle activities" (§IV, §VII-A).
- evidence: Software Construction 28.3% (registry) against 22.1% (personal); Design 4.3% against 2.5%; Security 3.8% against 2.3%; Configuration Management 4.1% against 9.4%; Testing 4.9% against 6.4%; Quality 3.8% against 5.7%; Maintenance 2.6% against 3.9%. Non-SE 28.5% against 29.8% (§IV). Classifier against a gold set of 285 skills: Cohen's kappa 0.82 (§III-B).
- effect: mixed
- reader: the gold set is balanced by predicted label, so the authors say it validates category-level quality and not corpus-level accuracy or the Non-SE share (§VIII). The registry/personal gap in Configuration Management and Testing is read by the authors as supply and demand; no adoption data tests that (§VII-A, "not evidenced by adoption patterns").

### F10 · practice · upstream-change-alerts-for-copied-skills · E0
- practice: users of a registry skill configure alerts for upstream changes, periodically validate referenced paths and versions, and test bundled scripts as workflows change (§VII-B).
- claim: these keep a copied skill from going stale, since "over half (53%) receive no updates after adoption" (§VII-B).
- evidence: none for the practice. The 53% is from F1.
- effect: helps
- reader: a recommendation derived from the descriptive findings and not tried. Nothing here shows that alerts get followed or that stale skills cause failures.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Heavily edited or renamed skills are missed by the name-based linkage, and reuse is assumed to run from registry to personal copy; the link count is conservative, and some skills called locally authored may be undetected reuse (§VIII).
- The evolution diff covers only the first and last version of a skill and hides intermediate changes (§III-D, §VIII).
- Knowledge-area labels come from an LLM and are sensitive to prompt, decoding and model version; reported proportions are classifier-based estimates; the gold set is stratified by predicted label (§VIII).
- The 20-install threshold and the cap of 18 skills per repository exclude less popular skills and in-house collections; one registry only; the study is a snapshot to 2026-05-04 (§VIII).
- Statistical tests ignore clustering within repositories and dependence among labels; the comparison is exploratory (§III-D).
- Content shares come from a sample balanced by knowledge area and are not corpus prevalence (§V-B).
- A skill is forced into one knowledge area (§VIII).

## Reader's notes

- No agent was run and no task outcome was measured. Every finding is about what skill files contain or how they change, so none bears directly on whether a skill, an edit or a rule helps an agent.
- "Updated" means at least one commit touching the skill's files after the introducing commit, in a one-year window. A single commit to an asset counts (the paper separates 5,978 skills with a changed `SKILL.md` from 8,386 updated skills, §III-D).
- Skills in personal repositories need at least 10 stars and 20 installs for registry ones, so the sample is of repositories others found worth starring. Repositories with more than 18 skills were dropped.
- Changes were coded by two authors, who reconciled disagreements by discussion; agreement for the change coding is not reported as a number (§III-D, §VIII).
- The 90% confidence level with a plus or minus 10% margin sizes each stratum of the coded sample, so the sample supports shares of changed skills to about that precision (§III-D).

## Discrepancies

- Table III's evolution percentages do not match 256 coded skills: 157 reworking-specification skills are printed as 61.6% (157/256 is 61.3%), 110 metadata skills as 43.1% (43.0%), 128 as 50.2% (50.0%), and 101 as 39.6% (39.5%). The printed values fit a denominator of 255 (reader's arithmetic from Table III against §III-D's 256).
- §VI-B gives Cohen's h for repackaging and porting as 0.72; Table III gives 0.71.
