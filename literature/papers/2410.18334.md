## Question

Do developers' beliefs about generative AI coding tools and about themselves change after three weeks
of first-time Copilot use, what do they use it for, and does it move telemetry such as code changes
and pull requests (§1)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: engineers with no prior Copilot use were randomised (blocked on gender and on "I like" and "I trust AI coding tools") into a treatment group given Copilot for 3 weeks and a control group told not to use generative AI coding tools; telemetry for 6 weeks before and 3 weeks during was compared with a difference-in-differences model (§3.5, §4.2.1).
- claim: "having access to the Gen AI tool made no statistically significant difference to developer telemetry metrics" (§4.2.1).
- evidence: Table 2, difference-in-differences coefficient, p-value, power. CodeChanges -89.9, p 0.5, power 0.06. PullRequests 0.1, p 0.9, power 1.0. PullRequestHours 2.6, p 0.8, power 0.2. DevelopmentMins -21.1, p 0.6, power 1.0. EmailMins -7.5, p 0.6, power 1.0. BuildMins -52.0, p 0.8, power 1.0. Group sizes in the telemetry analysis are not stated.
- effect: none
- reader: randomised, so E4. The result is a null from a small, short, partly non-compliant trial: the authors report that only 75% of both groups were fully compliant, a quarter of the control group tried generative AI tools and a quarter of the treatment group never used them (§4.2.1). A power of 0.06 for code changes means the test could not have seen an effect on that metric; powers of 1.0 next to p-values of 0.8 and 0.9 are not explained. Treatment and control already differed before the study in several metrics (§4.2.1).

### F2 · practice · ai-tool-introduction-attitude · E3
- practice: three weeks of Copilot use in daily work; the Likert items asked before and after, compared with a paired t-test (§4.2).
- claim: Copilot use "significantly increased developers' beliefs that these tools are useful and enjoyable" (§4.2, abstract).
- evidence: "I like using AI coding tools" 2.72 before and 3.61 after, p < 0.0001. "AI coding tools are useful" 2.93 and 3.51, p = 0.001. "AI coding tools generate correct code" 2.71 and 3.0, p = 0.051, stated for the treatment group (§4.2). Scale of 1 to 5 is inferred from the other items (reader's inference).
- effect: helps
- reader: E3. The text compares before and after; the paper does not say whether the control group's change was subtracted, or whether these three paired tests cover all participants or only the treatment group. The control group's change on the unique-skills item shows that groups can move without treatment (see F3). Outcomes are self-reported with original unvalidated questions (§5), and the authors work at the vendor of the tool.

### F3 · practice · ai-tool-introduction-attitude · E3
- practice: as F2; the outcomes are trust in the tools and how developers feel at work.
- claim: "no statistically significant changes in other variables, including how developers felt at work or in their level of trust for GenAI coding tools" (§4.2).
- evidence: no statistics are given for these items. Pre-study, about 20% of both experienced and inexperienced groups trusted the code and thought it correct, and closer to 30% thought the tools reliable (§4.1.2). Satisfaction correlated with liking and usefulness at r = 0.691 and with trustworthiness at r = 0.606; a regression found utility a stronger predictor of satisfaction than trust (§4.2).
- effect: none
- reader: E3, same design as F2, and a null without a test shown. The authors read it as developers who enjoy the tool and still check its output; the paper has no measure of checking behaviour, only the survey and quotes.

### F4 · observation · tool-experience · E3
- practice: none applied; intake-survey answers of engineers with Copilot experience (a couple of weeks or more) against those without (§4.1.2).
- claim: experienced users were more likely to think AI coding tools are useful and to like them; their trust in the tools did not differ (§4.1.2).
- evidence: 86% of experienced users agree the tools are useful against 44% of those without experience; 72% against 43% like using them; each p < 0.05 (§4.1.2). Trust, correctness and reliability: no significant difference (§4.1.2).
- effect: helps
- reader: E3, a comparison of self-selected groups. Engineers who liked the tool may be the ones who kept using it, so the direction of the link is open. Outcome is attitude, not task success.

### F5 · observation · low-risk-tasks-for-ai · E1
- practice: none applied; diary and closing-survey verbatims on which tasks Copilot helped with (§4.3.1, §4.4).
- claim: "developers experienced significant productivity benefits when using generative AI for low-risk tasks, such as generating documentation, comments, and repetitive code, as well as for creative processes like idea generation"; where errors are hard to detect, such as generated GUIDs, they "did not report similar productivity gains" (§4.4).
- evidence: quoted diary entries about documentation, comments, boilerplate and brainstorming (P155, P129, P27, §4.3.1) and one entry about a GUID that was copied from the next entry in a config file (P129, §4.3.2). In the closing survey the most common answer on how work changed was saved time or higher productivity, the second reduced boilerplate (§4.4); no count is given. The same task was reported as useful by one developer and useless by another, debugging in particular (P262 and P197, §4.3.1).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E1. The word "significant" is not backed by a test; the evidence is quotes and an unquantified ranking of answer categories. Self-reported productivity.

### F6 · observation · plausible-wrong-output · E1
- practice: none applied; challenges reported in diaries (§4.3.2).
- claim: the top issues were code that appears correct but is not, the need to validate generated code negating the productivity gain, and Copilot not working in all contexts, codebases and languages (§4.3.2).
- evidence: quotes only. P129 found property names that were "similar to the actual names, but not quite right" and says wrong names are harder to find in code already written than in code being typed. P154: suggestions are "deceivingly smart". P129 on the GUID case: "Now I don't know if I can trust it, which means it won't really save me time" (§4.3.2). The paper cites older work that humans are poor at monitoring tasks (Mackworth 1950, reference 16) to expect review to stay hard (§4.4).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E1. The ranking of "top issues" gives no counts. Good for hypotheses on review cost; no measurement of how many defects were missed.

### F7 · observation · uneven-language-and-file-support · E1
- practice: none applied; diary complaints by file type and language (§4.3.2).
- claim: Copilot "does not work on all languages equally" and was reported as poor on less common languages and on config files (§4.3.2).
- evidence: quotes. P289 on Android `bp` and `mk` files: "it was just guessing". P31 on `.json` files: suggestions "are never relevant". P289 on editing yaml and json config for a new build type: "gets more wrong than right" (§4.3.1, §4.3.2). Most participants wrote C# (53%), C++ (17%) or JavaScript or TypeScript (17%) (§3.3).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E1, three participants. The tool is a 2023 completion tool; the finding is dated.

### F8 · practice · agent-as-search-replacement · E1
- practice: developers asked the tool questions in the IDE instead of searching the web or documentation (§4.3.1, §4.4).
- claim: "a very common use case reported in the diaries" that "had not been mentioned much in the intake"; several closing-survey answers said it kept them in the IDE and largely replaced web search (§4.3.1, §4.4).
- evidence: quotes (P197, P119, P212). Share of diaries or people not given. GenAI use was reported in 56% of the diaries of the treatment and continuing groups (§4.3.1). Treatment-group diaries also record days with no use because of meetings, an unsupported language or codebase, or a design phase (§4.3.1).
- effect: helps
- reader: E1. "Very common" has no number; the benefit stated is speed and convenience as the developers perceive it.

### F9 · observation · adoption-barriers · E2
- practice: none applied; coded open-text reasons from intake respondents who had heard of Copilot but used it little (§4.1.1).
- claim: access alone did not lead to use; the authors recommend telling people they have access, explaining how to get it, and giving time to learn (§4.1.1).
- evidence: of all participants, 45% had heard of Copilot and not tried it and 4% had not heard of it (§4.1.1). Reasons: too busy 17%, do not think it will work well 12%, technical blocker 10%, no need 10%, did not know it was available 8%, no instructions on access 8%; 6% did not want to pay although the company provided it free (§4.1.1).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E2, shares of coded answers with nothing to compare against. The recommendation is the authors' and was not tried in the study.

### F10 · practice · developer-training-in-ai-tools · E0
- practice: organisations train developers on best practices for using the AI coding tool, including validating generated code, and update testing infrastructure for AI-assisted development (§6).
- claim: effectiveness "is directly related to how educated the developers are in using them" (P72, quoted in §6); the authors recommend prioritising this education (§6).
- evidence: one participant's quote and the authors' recommendation; no training was tested. One participant said there "weren't really any usage instructions" and saw no impact (P157, §4.4); another said it needs to be used iteratively, on a small problem at a time (P27, §4.3.1).
- effect: helps
- reader: E0, an opinion backed by quotes. The study gave the treatment group instructions to install and use Copilot (§3.2) and varied nothing else.

### F11 · observation · self-reported-speedup · E2
- practice: none applied beyond Copilot access; coded open-text answers of the final survey on how the tool changed work and feelings about work (§4.4).
- claim: "84% reporting some kind of positive change in how they work" and "66% of developers reported a change in how they feel about work" (§4.4 summary, abstract).
- evidence: 99 verbatims on work gave 129 codes, 84% of the codes positive and 16% negative ("no change", slowing, not useful for the role) (§4.4). For feelings, 88 verbatims, of which 38% said no change or no change yet; the most common change was higher work satisfaction, then less boring work as boilerplate left their plate (§4.4). One participant now writes more explicit code to help the tool (P131, §4.4).
- effect: helps
- reader: E2, self-reports with nothing to compare against. The 84% is a share of codes, not of people (see Discrepancies). Answers came from the compliant treatment and continuing groups; non-responders and the 122 of 228 intake engineers who are not in the final population are not in it (reader's arithmetic).

## Limits stated by the authors

- Self-reported surveys and diaries carry social desirability and recall bias (§5).
- The survey questions were original and not validated before use (§5).
- One company, though with teams in several countries and cultures (§5).
- The study may have been too short; the authors cite work that puts the point where users report gains at 11 weeks, and suggest 6 months to a year (§4.2.1).
- Telemetry could not be adjusted for sprint planning, on-call duty and leave; compliance was about 75%, which shrank the groups (§4.2.1).

## Reader's notes

- Groups were randomised before exclusions; the final 106 are those who did a diary, answered the final survey and complied (§3.3). Post-randomisation exclusion on compliance can unbalance the groups; Table 1 shows 3 women in control and 10 in treatment, and the authors say their balancing "was somewhat impacted" (§3.5).
- The "continuing" group is not randomised; it is a comparison group of prior users (§3.5).
- The authors justify the parallel-trends assumption of the difference-in-differences model by pre-study differences in levels between the groups (§4.2.1). Parallel trends concerns the trend, not the level; the paper shows no pre-trend test.
- The study measures beliefs and usage. It has no outcome on code quality, defects, review load or task completion.
- The tool studied is in-IDE completion and chat; no agent loop is involved. Findings on review cost and on task types transfer to agentic work only as hypotheses.
- Three of the four authors are at Microsoft, and the study ran inside Microsoft (§3.1). The paper does not state who owns GitHub Copilot.

## Discrepancies

- The abstract says "84% of participants reported positive changes in their daily work practices"; §4.4 gives 84% of 129 codes from 99 verbatims.
- The abstract and §4.4 summary say 66% reported a change in feelings about work; §4.4 says 38% of 88 verbatims reported no change, which leaves 62% (reader's arithmetic).
- The four research questions are listed in §1 with RQ4 before RQ3, and the section summaries label the use-case results RQ3 and the impact results RQ4, the reverse of §1's wording.
- §3.5 says a chi-square test found no difference between treatment and control, while Table 1 has 3 women of 29 in control and 10 of 35 in treatment.
- §3.3 defines the final population of 106 as compliant with treatment; §4.2.1 says only 75% of both groups were fully compliant. The telemetry population may differ from the final population; the paper does not say.
- Table 2 reports a power of 1.0 for PullRequests (coefficient 0.1, p = 0.9) and 0.06 for CodeChanges; the paper does not say how power was computed.
