## Question

Do programmers with access to an LLM code-completion assistant write less secure code than programmers
without it, in a low-level C task (§1)? RQ1: does the assistant help novices write functional code; RQ2:
is the security-bug rate acceptable against unassisted code; RQ3: where do the bugs in assisted code
originate (§1).

## Findings

### F1 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: give half of randomly divided student participants a Codex (code-cushman-001) completion extension in a cloud IDE and none to the other half, on one 11-function C linked-list assignment with 12 given tests (§3.1 to §3.4).
- claim: "the AI code assistant does help the users write better code in terms of functionality": a "small but consistent advantage" that does not reach significance (§4.2).
- evidence: 58 completers, 29 and 29 (Table 1). Submissions that did not compile: 19 of 58 (32.8%), 9 of 30 assisted and 10 of 28 control (§4.2). Average lines of code 280.9 assisted against 247.5 control. The four functionality measures are in Figure 7 only. No comparison reaches p < 0.05 (Fisher's exact test for completion, Welch's t-test for the rest) (§4.2).
- effect: helps
- reader: random assignment into two arms on equal tasks, so E4. Direction is in the authors' words and the data do not separate the arms: nothing is significant at n = 29 per arm, and the group sizes given for compilation (30 and 28) differ from Table 1 (29 and 29). 2302.06590 F2 has the same pattern and is filed `none`; here the paper's own conclusion is `helps`.

### F2 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E4
- practice: manually audit the submitted code of both arms for CWEs and test whether the assisted arm's bug rate per line of code is no more than 10% above the control's, with a non-inferiority test (§3.7, §4.3).
- claim: "AI-assisted users produce critical security bugs at a rate no greater than 10% higher than the control" and "LLMs do not introduce new security risks" in this setting (Abstract, §5.1).
- evidence: severe CWEs (MITRE top 25) per line, assisted fewer than control in all four cases, "up to a 22% lower mean" over functions that pass tests (§4.3.3). Non-inferiority, delta = 10%: p = 0.04 over compiling functions, p = 0.06 over functions that pass unit tests (Figure 8 caption). The all-CWE comparisons are in Figure 8 only.
- effect: none
- reader: randomised arms, so E4, but the strong sentence in the abstract holds at p = 0.04 for one of the two metrics and p = 0.06 for the other, while §4.3.3 calls both significant; §4.3.6 calls RQ2 "less conclusive" than RQ1. The threshold of 10% is the authors' choice (§3.7). The audit was by three of the authors, who read copies blinded to group (§4.3.1) and also designed the study. One task, one language, 58 students, so "no new risk" is a statement about this setting only.

### F3 · observation · security-effect-varies-by-function-type · E3
- practice: the per-function severe-CWE rates of the two arms are compared across the 11 functions (§4.3.4, Table 3).
- claim: "LLMs may sometimes reduce the incidence rates of bugs, and sometimes increase them"; assisted users have more bugs in functions that do input and output on the list, control users in functions with pointer manipulation or complex logic (§4.3.4, §4.3.6).
- evidence: Table 3 totals, severe CWEs per compiling function: control 2.09 (290 CWEs in 139 functions), assisted 2.21 (451 in 204). Over passing functions: control 2.14 (180 in 84), assisted 2.24 (278 in 124). By function, assisted has fewer in list_find_highest_price_item_position (0.63 against 1.00, †) and list_deduplicate (0.93 against 1.67, †), more in list_update_item_at_pos (3.67 against 2.71, †) and list_add_item_at_pos (3.38 against 3.05, †). CWE-416 (use after free) is "more prevalent" in assisted; CWE-787 about equal in both (§4.3.5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: no control on which functions people wrote: the arms submit different numbers of compiling functions per function (for example 26 against 20 for list_add_item_at_pos), so the rates are over different self-selected subsets. The per-function rate in Table 3 is higher for assisted in the totals, while per-line rates in F2 are lower. The paper does not reconcile the two metrics. The marks (†) are tests the paper does not name in the parts read.

### F4 · observation · bugs-mostly-human-written-in-assisted-code · E3
- practice: each of the 564 security bugs found in the assisted arm's final code was coded as originating from a Codex suggestion (verbatim or modified) or written by the human, using a tool that matches logged suggestions to the final file (§4.4).
- claim: "humans introduced 356 of the bugs in our dataset (63%), while only 36% were introduced by the LLM"; the rate at which the LLM introduces vulnerabilities "is similar to the rate at which they are introduced by humans" (§4.4).
- evidence: Table 4. Share of non-template lines: human 60%, Codex 16%, approximate match 18%. Share of bugs: human 63%, Codex 16%, approximate 20%. Matching by exact string, then edit distance up to 50% (§4.4).
- effect: none
- reader: the comparison is within one arm (bug share against line share) with no control. The 564 in §4.4 is not the 451 assisted CWEs of Table 3 (§4.3.4, §4.3.5 give no reconciliation). The approximate class (edited suggestions) has no clear owner, and human lines were typed with suggestions on screen, so "human-written" is not independent of the assistant.

### F5 · observation · buggy-suggestions-propagate-into-final-code · E2
- practice: for one bug (CWE-416, copying the item_name pointer instead of the string) the suggestion log and the final files of the assisted arm are scanned to see where the bug first appears and whether it is accepted or kept (§4.4, Table 5).
- claim: "in most cases it comes from the LLM suggestion originally, and even when it appears in the document first, the LLM will go on to suggest the bug"; users who accepted the most buggy suggestions had the most such bugs in their final files; users "do not use the extra productivity benefits to fix bugs" (§4.4, §5.1, §6).
- evidence: Table 5, 24 participants. Largest: participant c23b with 20 buggy suggestions, 10 accepted, 5 in the final file; 36de with 69 buggy suggestions, 5 accepted, 4 in the final file. 4 participants (1f1c, 2125, a5ba, dc47) wrote the bug themselves and accepted none.
- effect: hurts
- reader: one bug in one function pair; no statistic. Accepting more suggestions also means writing more code, so the count of bugs in the file rises for that reason alone. Participants were not asked why they accepted or kept a suggestion.

### F6 · practice · compile-retry-unattended-generation · E3
- practice: generate each function with a Codex model without a human ("autopilot"): one function at a time, up to 10 completions until the result compiles, previous output kept in the prompt; 30 solutions, 10 from each of 3 Codex models (§3.5).
- claim: autopilot code is of "relatively high quality" for a simple task; it outperforms both human groups on functions implemented and compiled "by design" and is close on tests (§4.2, §5.1).
- evidence: on basic tests slightly under the assisted arm and on the 43 expanded tests slightly over (§4.2, values in Figure 7 only). Security audited for 5 of 30 solutions only, all code-cushman-001: severe CWEs per function 2.02 over compiling functions (99 in 49) and 2.39 over passing (43 in 18), against control 2.09 and 2.14 (Table 3). CWE-787 "far less prevalent" in autopilot (§4.3.5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: compared with the human arms with no random assignment, and the 5 audited solutions are 5 of 30 and from the model with the smallest context window of the 3 (§3.5), so the security numbers are too thin to rank. The compile check is a mechanical loop around the model, not an agent with tests. The authors added a comment listing the struct members, since without it "an unassisted LLM must guess the member names" (§3.5).

### F7 · practice · ai-tools-allowed · E3
- practice: as F1; the measurement is time to submit.
- claim: the assisted group wrote and submitted faster (§4.2, Figure 12 in the Appendix, "for interest").
- evidence: Figure 12 plots the completion times of the two arms over 20 days; values are in the figure only, and no test is stated. The deadline was 14 days and "a small minority" of both arms needed more (Figure 12 caption).
- effect: helps
- reader: E3 because only a figure is given and the authors did not test it; the time is the submission date over a two-week window with a US$50 payment, not working time.

### F8 · practice · manual-cwe-audit-of-generated-code · E2
- practice: three of the authors read blinded copies of the submitted code together, annotating security bugs function by function, guided by compiler logs and the test suites (§4.3.1).
- claim: manual analysis is "thorough" but "does not scale well" (§4.3.1).
- evidence: about 22 hours over one week, 66 person-hours (§4.3.1), for the 58 user submissions and 5 Codex answers (§4.3). The audit produced 67 unique bug classes. No inter-rater check was done because annotation was collective (§4.3.1).
- effect: mixed
- reader: describes the cost of the method the study depends on; there is no comparison with another audit method on the same code. Collective annotation means no measure of reliability.

### F9 · observation · static-analysis-unfit-for-c-security-audit · E0
- practice: CodeQL static analysis and fuzzing were considered for finding the security bugs of the submissions and not used (§4.3).
- claim: CodeQL "gave rates of false positives and negatives too high for our purposes"; fuzzing gave records "difficult to deduplicate", and a crash early on a path hides later bugs on it (§4.3).
- evidence: the claim only. No rates and no number of runs are given.
- effect: hurts
- reader: asserted, E0. It matters because the paper's security numbers come only from the manual audit; no automated tool was run on the same code, so the paper gives no measure of what a tool would have found.

## Limits stated by the authors

- The participants are students, not professional developers; cited work found no difference in security-aware coding (§5.2).
- The measured defect density of 0.15 bugs per line is above the 0.07 figure the authors cite, which they call reasonable given the time limit (§5.2).
- The assignment was made hard on purpose (unusual API, C); other languages and tasks may differ (§5.2).
- The IDE took snapshots every 60 seconds rather than every keypress, which limits fine-grained analysis (§5.2).
- Studies "with larger sample sizes and diverse user groups are warranted" (§5.1).
- The 10% non-inferiority threshold has no accepted value and is the authors' choice (§3.7).

## Reader's notes

- Participants were told in recruitment that they would be split into an LLM arm and a no-LLM arm and that security would be among the metrics (Appendix); this could change how much the assisted arm checked suggestions.
- Participants worked unsupervised for two weeks on their own machines and could use the Internet; no one could check that the control arm did not use another AI tool (§3.2, §3.3).
- Of 105 who signed up, 58 completed code for analysis (§3.2). The paper gives no per-arm count of those who dropped out and no check that dropout was balanced.
- The model, code-cushman-001, with max_tokens 64 and temperature 0.6, is a 2022 completion model; the suggestions are short and inline (§3.4). The result says nothing about agent-mode editing.
- Two metrics are used for security, bugs per line (F2) and bugs per function (F3), and they point in different directions in the totals.
- The authors built the VS Code extension around the Codex API and ran the study; OpenAI supplied API access (Acknowledgments) and did not run the evaluation.

## Discrepancies

- The abstract and §5.1 say assisted users write severe bugs at a rate "no greater than 10% more" than control. §4.3.3 says the severe-CWE comparisons "are also statistically significant using non-inferiority tests"; the Figure 8 caption gives p = 0.04 and p = 0.06 (reader's reading of the caption; the second is above 0.05). §4.3.6 calls RQ2 "less conclusive".
- §4.2 gives 9 of 30 and 10 of 28 non-compiling submissions for the assisted and control arms; Table 1 gives 29 and 29 participants.
- §4.4 counts 564 vulnerabilities; Table 3 sums to 451 severe CWEs for the assisted arm over compiling functions (reader's arithmetic from the table's totals), and no other count of 564 is given in the parts read.
- The Table 3 caption says both "Yellow" and "Blue" cells mark a 10% difference, and §4.3.4 says "highlighted in blue" for both directions.
- Table 4's line shares add to 94%; the rest is not named.
- §4.3.3 says "For all four cases ... the 'assisted' group has fewer bugs", yet Table 3's per-function totals are higher for the assisted arm (2.21 against 2.09; 2.24 against 2.14).
