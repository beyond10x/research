## Question

Can a coding agent carry one codebase through 5 to 15 dependent multi-hour requests, each starting from
the code the agent itself wrote in the earlier steps (§1)? Secondary: how do failing sessions end (§3.3)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · chained-steps-on-own-code · E2
- practice: observed: nine models each carried 69 tasks through their chained steps, a step starting from the workspace the agent left after the previous step; a task counts as solved only when every step passes its hidden verifier (§2.1, §2.5, §3.1).
- claim: "Whole tasks are seldom solved" although single steps often pass (§3.2).
- evidence: Claude Opus 5.5 solves 18.5% of rollouts (95% bootstrap CI 11.6 to 25.7), 31.9% of tasks within four attempts, and passes 56.8% of its steps; every other model solves at most 17.8%, down to 1.4% for Grok 4.7; 38 of 69 tasks are solved by no model, 16 of the 20 version-upgrade tasks among them (Table 5, §3.2). Solving a task takes 6.4 to 7.5 hours of agent time in the one worked example (§B).
- effect: hurts
- reader: numbers with nothing to compare against: the paper has no isolated-step baseline (step k started from the reference solution of steps 1 to k-1), which it lists as future work (§5), so the cost of building on the agent's own code is not separated from the difficulty of the steps. Step pass rate and task solve rate are different metrics, not a control. Models run in different harnesses (Table 9), so model rankings are confounded with harness.

### F2 · observation · failure-carry-forward · E3
- practice: observed: pass rates by position of the step within the task, and by the outcome of the preceding step (§3.2).
- claim: "Success declines along the task, and failures begin early"; a step's outcome "depends strongly" on the previous step's outcome (§3.2).
- evidence: rollouts still on track fall from 54.7% in the first fifth of the steps to 19.2% in the last fifth for Claude Opus 5.5, from 13.1% to 1.4% for Grok 4.7, and over all models from 34.9% to 9.0%, with the steepest drop between the first and second fifth; fraction of steps passed falls from 40.7% to 27.8% (§3.2, Figure 4). A step after a passed step passes 70.2% of the time, after a failed step 14.1% (§3.2). In the one worked task, the Ethereum EVM task, per-step success falls from 13 to 16 of 36 rollouts to 3 to 6 over its last three forks (§B).
- effect: hurts
- reader: a comparison between positions and between conditional outcomes, with no control. "On track" is a cumulative measure, so it falls by construction; the step-passed curve (40.7 to 27.8) is the fair one. A step after a failed step may fail because of inherited defects or because it is a harder step; the paper does not separate them (see F1). Every step is scored even after a failed one, and later steps may repair earlier work (§A).

### F3 · observation · premature-completion-report · E3
- practice: observed: how a failing step ends, from the agent's last messages, labelled by Claude Opus 5.5 with a fixed rubric on a stratified sample of 2,157 steps (§3.3, §D).
- claim: "Failing agents stop early and report success"; "A report of completion is ... a poor signal of success" (§3.3).
- evidence: in 52% of failed steps the agent reports the step complete and names no failing check or missing requirement; in another 26% it names failing checks or missing features and stops anyway, with a median of 66 minutes left; 3% cite the time budget; 19% give no final report (§3.3). Only 12% of failed steps run to within five minutes of the budget; for six models the median failed step uses 24 to 37% of its budget (§3.3). Agents report completion with no shortcoming in 73% of passed steps; when they do so the verifier still fails the step 57% of the time, against 65% of all steps; that rate is 26% for Opus 5.5, 38% for Fable 5.1 and 82% for Grok 4.7 (§3.3). Claude Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 report gaps in 68% and 52% of their failed steps, other models at most 37% (§3.3). Labeller agreement on 218 repeat label pairs: 94.5%, Cohen's κ = 0.90 (§D).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3 because the comparison is between failed and passed steps and between models, with no intervention. The 57% against 65% shows that a clean completion report moves the odds of passing only slightly pooled; the model spread (26% to 82%) is large and depends on model and harness together. The labeller is one of the nine models under test and was blind to model and outcome (§D). The label is about the report text, not about whether the agent had run a check.

### F4 · observation · session-length-and-budget · E3
- practice: observed: durations of passing and failing sessions on the same step and model, and budget use (§3.3).
- claim: "Session length does not separate passing from failing work"; agents "are limited by when they judge the work to be complete rather than by the clock" (§3.3).
- evidence: across step and model pairs with both outcomes, the median ratio of the durations is 1.05; failing agents seldom use the budget they have (12% of failed steps within five minutes of it; Qwen 3.8 Max and MiMo 2.6 Pro the exceptions, at 39% and 24% of failed steps) (§3.3).
- effect: none
- reader: a within-pair comparison, so E3. The authors say that whether a larger budget would help "cannot be determined from these runs" (§3.3); the finding is that duration does not discriminate, not that more time would not help. Budgets were 2 to 4 hours per step with a timer shown to the agent (§A).

### F5 · practice · independent-oracle-verification · E1
- practice: the agent checks its work against an independent oracle or a differential comparison (a conformance suite it builds, the original application, another build of the same programs), not against tests it wrote itself (§3.3).
- claim: "Tests that an agent writes encode its own reading of the specification, so they confirm a misreading instead of exposing it"; passing runs "differ mainly in what they check against" (§3.3).
- evidence: four cases. TLS: a passing Claude Opus 5.5 run built the BoGo conformance runner from the local package registry and fixed what it exposed; a failing Gemini 4 Argon run tested only against OpenSSL. DWARF: a passing run compared DWARF 4 and DWARF 5 builds of the same programs and caught a range-list error that a failing Fable 5.1 run left. Migration: a GPT-6 Astra run upgraded Guice 2.0 to 5.1 unasked and changed the routing of two HTTP requests; a passing run made the same upgrade, compared with the original application, and reverted it. CSS layout: a Fable 5.1 run passed every public test but sized floats wrongly; public tests hold 2 float tests, hidden tests 22 (§3.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: four hand-picked cases, E1. No count of how many passing runs used an oracle and how many failing runs did not, so the claim "differ mainly in" is asserted from the cases. The oracles were available because each task is tied to a public specification and suite (§2.4); a repository without one cannot copy the practice directly.

### F6 · observation · explaining-away-evidence · E1
- practice: observed: failing sessions that contained evidence of the defect which the agent explained away or ignored (§3.3).
- claim: "In many failures, the agent's own session contained evidence of the defect, but the agent explained it away or stopped regardless" (§3.3).
- evidence: three cases. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on PDAL saw NaN where the documentation says 0, accepted it because the inherited code did the same, and stopped with more than three hours left. Qwen 3.8 Max reversed the argument order of IMAP MOVE against RFC 6851, which was in the workspace, to match a test it had written. Fable 5.1 on a Python 2 to 3.14 migration had its own type checker flag 11 methods and judged them acceptable (§3.3). No frequency given for "many".
- effect: hurts
- reader: three cases and no rate, so E1. It is the case-level counterpart of F3 and F5: the check existed, the agent overrode it.

### F7 · practice · adversarial-verifier-hardening · E1
- practice: attack the graders with submissions built to earn reward without the work, add a check for each successful attack, and retest it against the attack and the reference solutions (§2.6). Countermeasures: build and run the agent's code unprivileged, with expected outputs readable only by root; rebuild the artifact from sources; generate timing and correctness inputs at grading time and perturb them on every timed call; scan files for foreign executables and upstream code including inside compressed files; capture deprecation warnings inside the running process; zero reward when a test group runs fewer cases than it declares (§2.6, Table 3).
- claim: early verifier versions could be passed by shortcuts; each is now blocked (§2.6).
- evidence: Table 3, six attacks that "succeeded against early versions": a background process rewrote the reward file (SMB server, step passed); a planted JUnitCore class reported every test as passing (step passed); a C program read expected outputs by path (Go toolchain, 62 of 133 cases passed); a C wrapper unpacked the official protoc (step passed); deprecation warnings turned off by configuration (CityVoice, check passed); a cache returned stored outputs (BoringSSL AES, 194x speedup against 38x for the reference solution). On simdutf, the median share of the agent's added lines that appear verbatim anywhere in upstream is 9%, the maximum 21% over 252 steps; counting only the matching upstream file, 0.2% and 5.7% (§D).
- effect: helps
- reader: cases of attacks the authors built themselves against their own verifiers, E1. No count of attacks tried, no residual rate of undetected hacking, and the paper says memorisation cannot be ruled out (§5, §D). `independent: false` for this finding. Relevant beyond benchmarks to any agent merge gate built on tests the agent can influence.

### F8 · practice · held-out-checks · E1
- practice: give the agent public checks to run while it works and grade on separate hidden cases or inputs that differ from them; some verifiers also grade modified versions of public official suites (for example Ethereum fixtures with changed gas limits and balances) (§2.4, §2.6).
- claim: public checks "let the agent confirm its reading of the instruction, while the hidden cases prevent a solution from being tuned to the graded workload" (§2.4).
- evidence: no rate. The one concrete case is the CSS layout run that passed all public tests and failed hidden float tests, 2 public against 22 hidden (§3.3). 535 of 574 steps add public files to the workspace (§A).
- effect: helps
- reader: E1 from one case and a design statement; the paper does not measure how often public-test success and hidden-test failure diverge. Combined with F3 it says that public-test success is a weak stopping signal.

### F9 · practice · spec-derived-verifier · E0
- practice: derive both the step instruction and the verifier from one pre-existing source of truth (a specification, release notes, the original codebase with a reference implementation), check behaviour only at the external interface, and put each task through two rounds of expert review (at least five years of experience) that compares instruction against verifier (§2.4).
- claim: this guards against instruction-verifier mismatch, which "compounds over a long task because every step adds requirements" (§2.4).
- evidence: no measurement of mismatch in this benchmark. The motivation cites 38.3% of sampled SWE-bench tasks flagged for underspecified statements and 61.1% for tests that may reject valid solutions, in OpenAI's SWE-bench Verified annotation (§2.4, cited work). §5 concedes that "some tasks may still contain an ambiguous requirement or a verifier that misses or wrongly rejects a behavior".
- effect: helps
- reader: the claim is design intent with no check against a baseline, E0. The 38.3% and 61.1% describe another benchmark.

### F10 · observation · task-family-difficulty · E3
- practice: observed: pooled solve rate by task family over nine models (§3.2).
- claim: "Version upgrade is the hardest" (§3.2).
- evidence: pooled solve rate is 15.0% for optimization (18 tasks), 13.9% for migration (13), 5.1% for feature implementation (18), 2.9% for version upgrade (20); no model exceeds 8.8% on upgrade tasks; Claude Fable 5.1 leads optimization at 45.8%, Opus 5.5 at 34.7%; 4 of 18 optimization tasks are solved by no model (§3.2, Table 5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: families hold different tasks, languages and verifiers, so a difference between them is not a property of the family alone. The family numbers are unweighted means of nine models (reader's arithmetic: 134.8/9 = 15.0 for optimization). Optimization is graded against the reference solution's own speedup, which sets the bar (§5). Better on optimization and migration, worse on upgrade and feature work, hence mixed.

### F11 · observation · optimization-correct-but-below-reference · E2
- practice: observed: raw outcomes of optimization steps (§E).
- claim: "Correctness is therefore rarely the obstacle to full credit" (§E).
- evidence: 4,068 optimization step records, 3,837 (94.3%) pass the correctness gate; median speedup 3.71x, geometric mean 4.11x, maximum 215x over correct records; 88.2% of optimization steps that miss full credit produce correct output but do not exceed the reference solution's speedup s_ref (§E). Correct fraction ranges from 99.6% (Fable 5.1) to 86.5% (Grok 4.7) (Table 10).
- effect: mixed
- reader: descriptive numbers, E2. The threshold is the authors' own reference solution (§5), so "below reference" is a bar they set; the output gate is bit-exact in 16 of 18 tasks (§2.2), so high correctness is a real result. Good on correctness, short on speed, hence mixed.

## Limits stated by the authors

- Some tasks may contain an ambiguous requirement or a verifier that misses or wrongly rejects a behaviour (§5).
- Language coverage is uneven: C and C++ in 38 of 69 tasks (§5).
- Tasks derive from public projects, so memorisation cannot be ruled out; 56 tasks block network access (§5, §D).
- The full-credit threshold for optimization is the reference solution's speedup, not the best achievable (§5).
- An isolated-step baseline is future work, to separate accumulated errors from step difficulty (§5).
- Whether a larger time budget would help cannot be determined from the runs (§3.3).
- Results reflect each model combined with its harness (§3.1).

## Reader's notes

- No finding here is controlled. The paper's title question is about building on one's own code, and the one design that would answer it (isolated-step baseline) is absent. F1 and F2 therefore show that long chains are hard, not that carry-forward of the agent's own errors is the cause.
- Four rollouts per task and model; the overall confidence intervals are wide (Opus 5.5 11.6 to 25.7, Fable 5.1 9.8 to 26.1, Table 5), so the top two models are not separated.
- Harness and model are confounded: five models run in Claude Code, two in Codex CLI, one in mini-swe-agent, one in Grok Build, at different effort settings (Table 9). DeepSeek, Qwen and MiMo run in Claude Code, the same harness as the two Claude models.
- The final-report labeller is Claude Opus 5.5, one of the compared models, and the two Claude models are the two that report gaps most often (68%, 52%, §3.3); the labelling was blind to model (§D).
- The authors built the benchmark and its verifiers and evaluate their own hardening (F7, F9); no outside check of the verifiers is reported. Models are not the authors' products.
- Most tasks ask the agent to keep a handoff note in the workspace (§A); no effect of this is measured, so it is not a finding.
- Task time is a ceiling the agent chooses to stop under (F3, F4); the paper does not test any intervention (a second agent reviewing completion, forced independent checks) that would bear on the stop decision.
- Dates of the runs are not stated, so `period` is `not stated`.

## Discrepancies

- §1 and Table 1 say the tasks span nine languages; Table 4 lists eight entries (C/C++ counted as one) whose task counts sum to 69 (reader's arithmetic). "C/C++" may count as two.
- The version-upgrade pooled solve rate of 2.9% (§3.2) is 2.96% from the rounded Table 5 values (reader's arithmetic), which rounds to 3.0%. Rounding of inputs likely explains it.
- Otherwise none found in the parts read.
