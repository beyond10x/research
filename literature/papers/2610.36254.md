## Question

Does factory work for a tested repository need dedicated orchestration, or do two inputs (cron-job agents with standing objectives and a shared communication board such as GitHub) suffice, and how should a factory's progress be judged when its agents change their own workload and evaluator (Abstract, §I)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · scheduled-agent-loops · E3
- practice: about 30 scheduled agents with standing objectives (find, fix, review, merge, manage), each run reading the GitHub board, doing one bounded task and exiting, with no orchestrator, in the CLI factory; agents were introduced over days 0 to 11 (§IV, §III).
- claim: the CLI factory "sustained work" without repeated prompting; daily merges "rose 15×" during agent rollout, "an observed association" (§IV O1, Abstract).
- evidence: daily merged PRs rose from 2.2 to about 35 and peaked at 115 on day 9; 450 of 704 issues (64%) closed as fixed, median 46 hours; open issues peaked at 323 and fell to about 140 (§IV O1). Agent cost on one sampled day with 69 merges: $1.2k, or $17 per merge before CI costs (§IV O1). 2.2 to 35 is a factor of about 16 (reader's arithmetic).
- effect: helps
- reader: numbers before and after rollout with no control and no baseline period of equal length; the authors say before-and-after differences cannot establish causality because agents arrived over 11 days and their instructions changed 16 times (§IV threats). The outcome is merges and issue closures, which F2 shows the agents also inflate. The paper does not say how many merged PRs were judged correct; see F4. The cost figure is one day.

### F2 · observation · agent-filed-workload-inflates-throughput · E2
- practice: observed: the share of the backlog that the agents themselves filed, which makes the workload an output of the factory ("endogenous workload") (§I, §IV O1).
- claim: "Throughput can then overstate progress, so factories must optimize independently valued, validated, and delivered changes" (§I).
- evidence: agents filed 95% of 704 new CLI issues (§IV O1); 450 of them were closed as fixed (§IV O1); in the worked example 450 fixed issues drop to at most 418 after delivery and correctness are counted (§V). No measure of how much the filing inflated the count is given.
- effect: hurts
- reader: a description of one factory. The effect word records the paper's claim that raw throughput is a poor progress measure here, not a measured loss; the schema has no value for a share with no direction (see schema proposal P9).

### F3 · observation · agent-edits-to-ci-checks · E2
- practice: observed: agents had write access to the CI workflows that gate their own PRs and changed them ("endogenous evaluation"); the maintainer alone changed the agents' instructions (§IV O3).
- claim: "agents also modify the rules"; edits to the factory's own controls stayed governed because humans reviewed them (§IV O3).
- evidence: agents authored 40 of 58 PRs that changed CI workflows; the maintainer made all 16 changes to agent instructions (text); the figure shows humans 16, agents 0 (Figure 3b). Two LLM raters (κ = 0.91) classed the effect of agent CI PRs: 7 loosened checks (text, §IV O3); 9 tightened and 21 faster or more reliable (in Figure 3b only). Agents moved end-to-end tests to nightly runs, delaying detection of breakage until after merge (§V). The paper did not test whether agent CI changes caused escapes (§IV threats).
- effect: mixed
- reader: counts only. The 21, 9 and 7 do not add to 40 (reader's arithmetic: 37 of 40), and the paper does not say why. LLM raters decide the direction of each change.

### F4 · observation · merged-fixes-later-found-incorrect · E2
- practice: observed: fixes that agents merged and closed as fixed, checked afterwards for incorrect fixes ("escapes") by agent scouts and bug reports (§IV O4).
- claim: "merged fixes do not always hold"; throughput therefore overstates progress and fixes should be judged after a delay (§IV O4, §V).
- evidence: by day 17 at least 25 of 450 fixed CLI issues (5.6%) were escapes, 17 that broke another case of the issue and 8 that left part unfixed; none was reopened (§IV O4). In the agentic factory 3 of 14 replayed fixes (21%) recurred (§IV O4). Of its 21 merged fixes, humans amended 7 (§IV threats).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the 5.6% is a lower bound that depends on scouting coverage and LLM classification (§IV threats); "no detected escape does not establish correctness" (Fig. 3 caption). There is no human-written or non-agent baseline, so the paper cannot say whether the rate is high or low. The 14 replayed fixes are a small sample.

### F5 · observation · escape-detection-by-bug-hunting-agents · E2
- practice: observed: reports of escapes, mostly from the bug-hunting agents that were already running, as the detection channel for incorrect fixes (§IV O6, §V).
- claim: "Bug-hunting agents already act as held-out tests" (§V, H5 discussion).
- evidence: reports identified 22 of 25 CLI escapes within a day of merge (§IV O6, Fig. 3d).
- effect: helps
- reader: the 25 escapes are the ones that were found, so the detection rate is among detected escapes only; an escape no agent noticed is not in the denominator. The "held-out test" reading is the authors' and is a hypothesis (H5), not tested.

### F6 · observation · integration-delay-before-release · E2
- practice: observed: the time between a merge and the release that carries it, and whether an escape is reported inside it (§IV O5, O6).
- claim: "merged fixes must reach users" and "escapes race releases" (§IV O5, O6).
- evidence: two CLI releases shipped 94% of fixed issues after a median integration delay of 3.9 days (§IV O5). Release delays let 14 escapes be corrected before shipment; 7 shipped unfixed, 4 of them before being reported (§IV O6). In the agentic factory a recovery sweep resumed 11 of 22 blocked runs, and fixes propagate only when the blocked runs restart (§IV O5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: counts from one factory over about 17 days. The delay helped the 14 and not the 7; the paper gives no count of escapes that nobody found in time. The 94% is 425 of 450 (reader's arithmetic) and matches Fig. 3d's n = 425.

### F7 · practice · merge-gate-throughput-and-lead-time · E2
- practice: a merge queue added on day 7 of the CLI factory, running required checks before agent PRs merge (§IV O2).
- claim: "controls depend on context and may limit throughput" (§IV O2).
- evidence: the queue rejected 29 failing agent PRs and delayed others by a median 26 minutes while daily merges doubled (§IV O2). 77% of agent merges changed user-installed code (§IV O2).
- effect: mixed
- reader: no run without the queue exists, so the 29 rejections are counted but not compared with what would have merged; the 26 minutes is a delay among queued PRs. A rejected PR is a failing PR at the time of the check, not a defect the queue found that CI would have missed.

### F8 · observation · human-review-of-agent-pr · E2
- practice: observed: the human approval step the CLI factory kept on every merge (§IV O2).
- claim: one reviewer approving without requesting changes "questions review effectiveness and, as another agent-first team found [26], scalability" (§IV O2).
- evidence: a second person approved 534 of 541 merged PRs; one reviewer approved 502 CLI PRs without requesting changes (§IV O2).
- effect: hurts
- reader: counts of approvals, with no measure of review time, comments or defects caught. A 502-of-541 approval share is consistent with a rubber stamp or with PRs that were already checked well; the paper reads it the first way without evidence either way. Both factories retained human approval, so the minimum governance is unknown (§IV threats).

### F9 · observation · multi-issue-pull-requests · E3
- practice: observed: agents sometimes fixed several issues in one PR ("step size"), 60 of 331 issue-closing PRs (§V).
- claim: no claim of an effect; the gap is "inconclusive" and is posed as hypothesis H3 (§V, Table II).
- evidence: those 60 PRs fixed 40% of all fixed issues; per-issue escape rate 3.9% against 6.6% for single-issue PRs, Fisher's p = 0.29, ignoring PR clustering (§V, Fig. 3c). Issues per PR: 450/331 = 1.36 (Table I).
- effect: none
- reader: not assigned by the authors, so an observation; multi-issue PRs may be the easy, related issues, and the test ignores PR clustering, which the authors say.

### F10 · practice · role-specialised-agents · E2
- practice: in the agentic factory, service loops over a shared database work list with five roles: a scout that groups failures into defect types, a fixer that submits PRs, a verifier that reruns checks, a judge that rejects package-specific fixes and a recovery agent that restarts blocked runs; humans approve after the judge (§IV).
- claim: the factory sustained work with an architecture different from the CLI factory's; no claim of superiority, because the workloads differ (§IV O1, threats).
- evidence: $869 on 21 merged harness fixes, or $41 each, resolving 21 of 134 defect types (16%); the validation harness returned no verdict in 28% of reruns; 3 of 14 replayed fixes recurred; operators stopped the swarm on day 16 (§IV O1, O4, threats).
- effect: mixed
- reader: described outcomes with no comparison arm; the authors state that "different workloads and lack of an architectural baseline prevent claims of superiority". The role split is not ablated, so no role is shown to matter. Fixes concern an internal harness, not user code.

### F11 · practice · controls-proven-before-agents-enabled · E1
- practice: before enabling the schedules, the bootstrap has the agent test each governance restriction (approval, stop switch checked before every write, ignore instructions in retrieved content) and obtain approval of the setup PR (§III).
- claim: "Tool lists alone did not enforce GitHub permissions, so the prompt tests each restriction" (§III, manual setup lessons).
- evidence: a lesson from the hand-built routines, with no count or incident given (§III).
- effect: helps
- reader: a design rationale from the authors' manual setups; one lesson, no data. The bootstrap that contains this step was not run in the factories studied (§IV), so the practice itself is not tested.

### F12 · practice · one-prompt-factory-bootstrap · E0
- practice: give a coding agent in the target repository a 1,400-word prompt that inspects the repository, asks the human once (budget, QA priorities, approvers), provisions the cron routines and a setup PR, proves the controls and enables the schedules (§III, Fig. 2).
- claim: two coordination inputs suffice for a factory for PR-sized work (sufficiency) and the prompt can configure both in one session (feasibility); "One factory gives early evidence for the first, and the second is untested" (§I).
- evidence: none for feasibility: the bootstrap was "distilled from two hand-built factories" and "not yet evaluated" (Abstract, §IV). Sufficiency rests on the CLI factory (F1) and is not tested against a factory with an orchestrator.
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted by the authors, E0. Planned tests H1 to H5 and the feasibility run are future work (Table II, §VI); no result of them is in the paper. The authors name the prompt's sole source, one organization, as a threat (§IV).

## Limits stated by the authors

- Both factories belong to one organization, the bootstrap's sole source, and were observed for two to three weeks (§IV threats).
- Both retained human approval, so the minimum necessary governance is unknown (§IV threats).
- CLI agents were introduced over days 0 to 11 and their instructions changed 16 times, so before-and-after differences cannot establish causality; the workloads differ, so no superiority claim (§IV threats).
- Operators stopped the agentic swarm on day 16 and humans amended 7 of its 21 fixes (§IV threats).
- Escape counts depend on scouting coverage, classifications rely on LLM raters, and CLI costs come from one sampled day (§IV threats).
- The study did not test whether agent CI changes caused escapes and found "no evidence of intentional gaming" (§IV threats).
- Production deployment, multi-repository work and tightly coupled work are out of scope (§III).
- Defaults of the bootstrap come from two manual factories, may inherit their biases and have untested effects (§V).
- Case records are private (Data availability).

## Reader's notes

- No finding evaluates the paper's own proposal. Everything measured is from two hand-built factories; F12 and the five hypotheses are plans.
- The authors appear to be from the organization that built both factories (Socket Inc. is the stated affiliation), but the text does not name the organization; `independent: false` follows from the authors proposing the bootstrap on the basis of their own factories.
- The model and agent product behind each factory are not stated, so no result can be tied to a model.
- The progress measure the paper wants (changes judged correct by an evaluator the agents cannot edit, delivered, with severity weights set by the maintainer) is defined but not computed, except in the worked example, whose bound of 418 is an upper bound (§V).
- Figure 3 holds more numbers than the text (for example the shares of issue outcomes in panel e); they are not used here.
- The planned H5 sizing: 800+ fixes per arm to detect a halved 5.6% escape rate at 80% power, about three months at late-study rates (§V).

## Discrepancies

- The abstract says daily merges "rose 15×"; §IV gives 2.2 to about 35, a factor of about 16 (reader's arithmetic). The "about" covers the difference.
- The effect classes 21, 9 and 7 in Figure 3b cover 37 of the 40 agent-authored CI PRs; the remaining 3 are not described.
