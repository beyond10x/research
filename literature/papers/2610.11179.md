## Question

How do AI-authored pull requests (AIPRs) change reviewers' work, what makes an AIPR reviewable enough
to get reviewer effort, and what fairness and accountability rules do reviewers expect (§3.1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · reviewer-willingness-gap-for-ai-authored-prs · E3
- practice: respondents rated, for the same eight change types and with CI assumed passed, how willing they were to accept a human-authored PR (HPR) and then an AIPR without additional explanation, on a five-point scale; the HPR item always came first (§3.2.3, §4.3.1, §6.2).
- claim: "willingness was significantly lower for AIPRs than HPRs across all eight change types", also for low-risk work; passing CI did not make AI-generated revisions as acceptable as human ones (§4.3.1).
- evidence: share answering likely or very likely, HPR then AIPR (Table 5, n = 239, all pHolm < .001): cleanup 86.2 to 73.6, documentation 85.4 to 66.9, test additions 79.5 to 61.5, refactor with no behavior change 52.3 to 19.7 (gap -32.6 pp, rrb = -0.837), performance 23.0 to 12.1, concurrency 12.1 to 11.3, auth or security 8.4 to 5.4, complex feature 11.7 to 7.5. Respondents rating the AIPR lower against higher: 81 against 39 for test additions, 146 against 21 for refactors (§4.3.1). Gap not associated with code-review experience (rho = 0.049, p = .455).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3, a within-respondent comparison with no control. The HPR item was not randomised before the AIPR item and the AIPR wording stressed human explanation, so the authors call the gaps "upper bounds on the effect of authorship alone" (§4.3.1, §6.2). The outcome is stated willingness in a scenario, not review behaviour.

### F2 · observation · review-capacity-bottleneck · E2
- practice: respondents reported how AIPRs changed their review communication, review volume and the load from low-effort newcomer AIPRs (Figure 2, §4.2.1).
- claim: AIPRs "reshape review practice by changing both the amount and kind of review work that maintainers must manage" (§4.2.1).
- evidence: 90.8% reported at least some change in communication expectations and 74.1% a moderate or significant change (95% CI 68.2 to 79.2); 77.8% reported more PRs requiring review since AIPRs became common, 57.7% a moderate or significant increase (51.4 to 63.8); 60.7% called low-effort AI-generated newcomer PRs a minor issue or major problem, 25.9% (62 respondents) a major problem (Figure 2). 12.6% (30) reported the same volume and 2.5% (6) a decrease (Figure 2B).
- effect: hurts
- reader: self-reported perception with no baseline measure of review time or volume. The sample was drawn partly from repositories with known agent PR activity (§3.2.4), which favours people who see AIPRs.

### F3 · practice · validation-beyond-ci-before-review · E2
- practice: ask for or run validation beyond a passing CI before investing review effort in an AIPR: tests, edge cases, local runs, logs, benchmarks, security checks (§4.2.2, §4.2.3, §4.3.2).
- claim: AIPRs become reviewable when they show "readiness beyond passing CI"; CI is "necessary but insufficient" (§4.3 RQ2 answer, §5.3).
- evidence: 60.0% (117 of 195 substantive responses) described validation-first communication; 62.5% (125 of 200) said they replaced surface-level trust with active validation (Tables 3 and 4). Testing evidence beyond "it works for me" was rated highly or extremely influential for newcomer AIPRs by 81.2% (Figure 4C, §4.3.3). Missing tests despite behaviour change were a deferral trigger for 51.0% and failing CI with no attempted fix for 39.7% (Figure 4B). 66.5% required passing CI or tests first (Figure 4A).
- effect: helps
- reader: the numbers describe what reviewers say they require, not an outcome of requiring it, so E2. No measure of defects caught, review time or merge outcome with and without the practice. A quoted respondent says generated tests "hallucinates mock data, tests tautologies, or misses critical edge cases" (Table 4), which is a claim about agent output with no count behind it.

### F4 · practice · named-human-owner-for-agent-pr · E2
- practice: require an intent statement, AI-use provenance and an identifiable human who will maintain the change after merge before treating an AIPR as ready for review (§4.2.3, §4.3.2).
- claim: reviewers' effort is conditional on "identifiable post-merge ownership"; accountability is the missing signal in unreviewable AIPRs (Abstract, §4.2.3).
- evidence: 59.5% (119 of 200) adopted intent, provenance and ownership gates (Table 4); 41.8% required evidence that a known human would maintain the code and 32.2% an explicit ownership statement; 56.9% a clear description or intent (Figure 4A); 28.7% (56 of 195) shifted communication toward human ownership (Table 3); a vague AI-generated description was the most frequent deferral trigger at 64.9% (Figure 4B). 60.9% (78 of 128) said AI use is acceptable when the human steward visibly understands and validates the change (Table 7).
- effect: helps
- reader: stated gates, no outcome measured. The ownership options were listed in the questionnaire, so selection rates show endorsement of presented alternatives (§3.2.2, §6.1). The paper's recommended "stewardship trace" (§5.4) is a proposal with no test.

### F5 · practice · demonstrated-understanding-gate-for-newcomer-agent-prs · E2
- practice: for a suspected AI-assisted drive-by PR from a newcomer, keep review open on conditions (the contributor shows understanding, discusses the issue first, meets a stricter bar or passes automated screening) instead of closing it (§4.3.3).
- claim: "Respondents did not favor automatic closure"; they "preferred conditional review" (§4.3.3).
- evidence: 193 (80.8%) chose at least one special-handling strategy and 40 (16.7%) none. Demonstrate understanding before review 55.6%, issue discussion before a PR 38.1%, stricter quality bar 33.1%, automated screening before human review 29.7%; each differs from no special handling (Holm p < .001, paired OR 1.81 to 3.35). Template closure 20.1%, queue deprioritisation 23.8% and closing apparently unmodified AI output 12.6% did not differ from no special handling after correction (§4.3.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: a stated preference among listed options, with no measure of what the strategies do to review load or newcomer retention. The authors note that project policies most often choose aggressive closure and that social desirability may explain the gap to their respondents (§5.5, §6.1).

### F6 · observation · process-signals-over-profile-signals-for-credibility · E3
- practice: respondents rated the influence of 10 signals on whether a newcomer AIPR is credible, on a five-point scale (Figure 4C, §4.3.3).
- claim: strongest signals are "concrete behaviors within the current review process"; profile-level and future-commitment cues are much weaker (§4.3.3).
- evidence: rated highly or extremely influential: professional response to initial feedback 82.4%, testing evidence 81.2%, appropriate scope 77.8%, response to CI failures or bot comments 71.5%, active GitHub profile 23.4%, stated intent to contribute more 21.3%. Friedman chi2(9) = 564.14, p < .001, Kendall's W = .262; Holm-corrected post-hoc tests rate process signals above profile signals (§4.3.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: E3 because the signals are compared with each other within respondents, but nothing is compared on an outcome. Ratings of listed signals; the paper itself calls its readings of signalling and reciprocity "post hoc" (§5.2).

### F7 · practice · change-type-evidence-bundles · E2
- practice: replace a generic request for tests with evidence matched to the risk of the generated change, such as explain plans, migration dry-runs, rollback notes, screenshots, benchmarks, failing-before-passing tests (§4.3.2, §4.4.1).
- claim: respondents wanted AIPR governance "embedded in project workflow infrastructure rather than left to reviewer caution alone" (§4.4.1).
- evidence: 33.3% (57 of 171) of proposed team rules were change-type evidence bundles (Table 6); open-ended answers for entry gates (40 respondents, 16.7%) and deferral triggers (36, 15.1%) asked for reproduction notes, failing-before-passing tests, benchmarks, stress traces, API-version checks, threat models and rollback plans (§4.3.2).
- effect: helps
- reader: respondents' proposals, not a tried practice. The count is the share of rule proposals in one open question, where each respondent named one rule.

### F8 · practice · small-staged-agent-prs · E2
- practice: ask that generated changes arrive as smaller, staged, narrow or mechanical PRs, and defer large AIPRs that are not decomposed (§4.2.3, §4.3.2, §4.4.1).
- claim: large generated diffs shift review cost to maintainers; containment keeps them reviewable (Table 4, Table 6).
- evidence: 45.0% (90 of 200) adopted reviewability and bandwidth controls such as smaller scope, issue-first submission, templates and queueing (Table 4); a very large PR without decomposition was a deferral trigger for 51.0% (Figure 4B); 14.0% (24 of 171) proposed generated-diff containment rules (Table 6); appropriate scope was rated highly influential by 77.8% for newcomer AIPRs (§4.3.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: stated practice and proposals. The 45.0% theme mixes several controls (issue-first, templates, queueing, rejection), so it is not specific to staged PRs.

### F9 · practice · human-plus-agent-review · E2
- practice: run an AI model as a pre-reviewer, secondary reviewer or consistency checker before the human review, with code-review instructions added to the agent (§4.2.3, Table 4).
- claim: respondents who did this said "human review can focus on design, architecture, and big picture" (Table 4 example).
- evidence: 9.0% (18 of 200) of substantive practice-adaptation responses were AI-assisted review triage (Table 4). No outcome is given.
- effect: helps
- reader: a small theme with two quoted cases; no data on what the AI review catches or costs. The paper's own research agenda lists AI-to-AI review as untested (§5.6).

### F10 · practice · accepted-review-comments-as-instruction-rules · E2
- practice: when a review comment changes the code in a meaningful way, update the AI's instruction files so the correction is kept for later agent runs; or use a prompt template aligned with company guidelines (Table 6, agent-workflow hygiene theme).
- claim: review corrections should feed back into future agent behaviour (§4.4.1, Table 6).
- evidence: 9.4% (16 of 171) of proposed team rules were agent-workflow hygiene and learning loops (Table 6); the example quoted is "For any comment, which ended up changing the code in a meaningful way, we update the AI's instruction file(s), so this is now documented for the future." No effect is measured.
- effect: helps
- reader: E2 because the support is a share of proposed rules plus one quoted statement, with no outcome. It is the only finding here about instruction files, and the smallest theme in Table 6.

### F11 · observation · reviewer-audit-mode-for-prompt-only-prs · E2
- practice: respondents described how reviewing substantial human effort against prompt-produced code changed their willingness to invest review time, their emotional response and their sense of reciprocity (§4.4.2, Table 7).
- claim: reviewers invest more when submitters visibly understand and own the change and "shift toward audit, triage, or rejection when AIPRs appear to offload explanation, debugging, or validation onto maintainers" (§4.4.2).
- evidence: of 128 codeable responses: accountable human stewardship 60.9% (78), reciprocal reviewer investment 57.0% (73), audit mode for prompt-only AIPRs 45.3% (58), offloaded strain and frustration 43.0% (55), tool-neutral review standards 17.2% (22) (Table 7). In the communication question, 27.7% (54 of 195) described a shift from mentoring to transactional review (Table 3).
- effect: hurts
- reader: coded self-descriptions with no measure of review comments or review time. The outcome that gets worse is mentoring and reviewer experience; the tool-neutral minority (17.2%) is reported by the authors, so the pattern is not universal.

### F12 · practice · ai-use-admissibility-policy · E2
- practice: write a team rule that says when AI-authored work is acceptable, restricted or rejected, and separates light AI assistance from agent-generated PRs (§4.4.1, Table 6).
- claim: AIPR governance should be encoded in "PR templates, review queues, owner-routing rules, agent instructions, and admissibility policies" (§4.4.1).
- evidence: 36.8% (63 of 171) of the rules respondents proposed were AI-use boundary and admissibility rules, the largest rule theme (Table 6). Cited from Hora and Robbes, not this study's data: 118 of the 1,000 most-starred GitHub repositories had an AI-related policy, 78% of those permitted AI-assisted contributions and 51% required disclosure (§2.6).
- effect: helps
- reader: proposals only, and the policy figures are another study's. No project in this study applied the rule and was measured.

### F13 · practice · queue-limits-and-owner-routing-for-agent-prs · E2
- practice: limit how many AI-authored PRs one contributor may have open, require issue-first discussion, and route AI-authored changes that touch auth, permissions, migrations or user-facing text to a domain owner or stakeholder (§4.4.1, Table 6).
- claim: such controls protect reviewer bandwidth from cheap, repeated or unsolicited AIPRs and put risky changes with the responsible human (Table 6).
- evidence: queue controls 17.5% (30 of 171) and risk-based routing with stakeholder sign-off 15.2% (26) of proposed rules (Table 6). In open answers on the newcomer item respondents described "routing security-sensitive changes to domain owners" and tagging a human maintainer (§4.3.3).
- effect: helps
- reader: respondents' proposals at a share of 15% to 18% each; no project outcome is shown. The authors flag possible tension with open contribution (§2.6, §5.3).

## Limits stated by the authors

- Cross-sectional and observational; designed to characterise reviewer judgments, not to establish a causal effect of AI authorship on review behaviour (§6.2).
- In the paired willingness items the HPR item preceded the AIPR item, order was not randomised, and the AIPR wording stressed human explanation (§6.2).
- Perceived authorship, not verified provenance; AIPR exposure is self-reported in bands (§6.1).
- Predefined options make listed considerations salient; selection rates are endorsement of presented alternatives, not a ranking of what reviewers would name unprompted (§6.1, §3.2.2).
- Self-reports can differ from practice and carry recall and social desirability bias; reviewability debt is an interpretive concept, not a measured quantity of reviewer time or defects (§6.1).
- Possible demand effects, because the study description made the human against AI comparison explicit (§6.2).
- Purposive, non-probabilistic sample from visible mature open-source projects and LinkedIn; the response rate cannot be computed (§6.3, §3.2.5).
- Exploratory analyses by experience and exposure had limited power; non-significance is not read as no association (§6.4).
- No attention checks or machine-response detection in an anonymous survey (§6.4).
- Findings are tied to the time of the study, as agents and policies change fast (§6.3).

## Reader's notes

- No agent output was examined and no repository outcome was measured. Every effect in this file is the direction respondents stated, so none is above E3, and `controlled` is false.
- Findings F3 to F5 and F7 to F13 describe practices that respondents say they use or propose. The paper contains no measure that any of them improves review time, defects found, merge rate or reviewer load.
- The open-ended themes are coded multi-label, so percentages in Tables 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8 do not add to 100 and each has its own denominator (195, 200, 171, 128, 129).
- The sample was built partly from repositories with known agent-authored PRs (516 emails) and partly from other mature projects (3,357 emails), plus LinkedIn; the share of respondents from each frame is not given (§3.2.4, §6.3).
- Respondents were asked for "one team guideline they would change" (§4.4.1); responses could carry several themes, so Table 6 shares compare rule types, not how many respondents back each one.
- The survey asks reviewers about PRs by agents; it does not separate agents or models. The paper names Copilot, Claude Code, Codex and Devin only as examples (§1, §3.2.4).
- 52 of 239 respondents (21.8%) had reviewed fewer than 10 AIPRs (Figure 1a). The 60.7% for low-effort newcomer AIPRs is over all 239, and 39 (16.3%) answered "not observed" and 40 (16.7%) "N.A." (Figure 2C), so it is not a share of those who saw the problem (83 + 62 = 145 of 239, reader's arithmetic).
