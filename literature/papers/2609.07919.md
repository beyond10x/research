## Question

How do OSS projects adapt their contribution policies to AI-mediated contributions, with what
rationales, and what governance framework organises those concerns (§I, RQ1 and RQ2)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · ai-use-admissibility-policy · E2
- practice: observed in the 209 unique projects: the stance of the policy on AI-mediated contribution, coded permitted, regulated, prohibited or unclear by Claude Code (Opus 4.7) and checked by hand (§III, Table I, §IV).
- claim: the two sources differ: HR "skewing heavily toward AI policies that allow the use of AI (69%)", MelissaWM "more restrictive" with "36% that prohibit any AI use" (§IV).
- evidence: 209 projects from 118 + 106 − 15 (§IV). 42 prohibit and 34 regulate (§V.1, §V.2, §VII). Only 118 of the 1,000 most-starred repositories have a policy at all (§I). The per-stance counts of Figure 1 are in the figure only.
- effect: none
- reader: prevalence only; no outcome of any policy is measured, so `none` records that no direction is claimed (P9 case). The authors say the sample "do[es] not support statistical generalization" and that results "should not be read as claims about how common particular policy positions are" (§VIII). The 36% and 69% are properties of how each list was assembled.

### F2 · observation · ai-ban-rationales · E2
- practice: observed in the 42 projects that prohibit AI-mediated contributions: the reasons their policy texts give (§V.1, Figure 2).
- claim: maintainer burden is "the dominant and near-universal rationale"; AI "eliminates the natural effort-based backpressure that previously limited" low-quality submissions (§V.1).
- evidence: of 42 projects: maintainer burden 34 (81%); code quality and correctness 33 (79%); copyright, IP and licensing 24 (57%); DCO and authorship certification 15 (36%); community trust and culture 15 (36%); learning and contributor development 14 (34%); ethics 8 (19%); security 3 (7%) (§V.1).
- effect: none
- reader: counts of stated reasons from policy text, coded by an LLM and verified by the authors. A project can give several reasons, so shares add to more than 100%. §VII gives other counts for the same groups (see Discrepancies). No outcome is measured; a ban's effect on maintainer load is not tested.

### F3 · observation · contributor-must-understand-and-own-ai-work · E2
- practice: regulate AI use by making it conditional on the contributor understanding the change and being able to explain and defend it; the 34 regulating projects do this (§V.2).
- claim: "AI is tolerated if understanding accompanies it, rejected if it does not"; "regulated is not a soft version of prohibited" and is "defined by conditionality (e.g., 'if you use AI...')" (§V.2, §VII).
- evidence: code quality and contributor understanding required in 34 of 34 regulating projects (100%); maintainer burden and review cost cited in 30 (88%); copyright and IP in 9 (26%) (§V.2). Quotes: badlogic/pi-mono "Human must fully understand and be able to explain changes"; FastAPI "Human must understand all submitted code" (§V.2).
- effect: none
- reader: no outcome is measured: the paper reports that the rule is stated, not that it lowers review cost or defects. The 100% is by construction partly: the projects were sorted into "regulated" by what their policy says, and the paper says this requirement "distinguishes 'regulated' from 'prohibited' most clearly".

### F4 · practice · human-authored-review-dialogue · E2
- practice: require that issue text, pull request descriptions and review replies are written by the human contributor, also where AI-written code is allowed (§V.2).
- claim: AI drafting the communication means "the maintainer is no longer talking to a person but to an AI that is impersonating her" (§V.2).
- evidence: human-human interaction and community dialogue is a concern in 14 of 34 regulating projects (41%). Jellyfin: "LLM output is expressly prohibited for any direct communication, including issues or comments, feature requests, pull request bodies or comments" (§V.2).
- effect: none
- reader: prevalence and one quote; nothing measured on review quality. Related to F7, where the same rule is applied to bug reports.

### F5 · practice · newcomer-protected-ai-rules · E2
- practice: write the AI policy so that newcomers and mentoring contexts are treated differently: reserve good-first issues for human newcomers, apply stricter rules to mentorship-programme participants, or exempt trusted maintainers (§V.A.1).
- claim: AI-mediated first PRs "consume [the review] investment while weakening the signal that would make it worthwhile" (§V.A.1, on Zig).
- evidence: only 17 of 209 projects (8%) distinguish new from regular contributors. Mechanisms: 5 maintainer exemptions (Ghostty, Polars); 6 reserve good-first issues for newcomers (LLVM, Firefox); 3 stricter for mentorship contributors (CPython, CCExtractor); 4 implicit trust (Linux Kernel, curl) (§V.A.1). LLVM: "AI tools must not be used to fix GitHub issues labelled good first issue."
- effect: none
- reader: counts of how many projects wrote such a rule, with no effect on newcomers or review load measured. The mechanism counts sum to 24 against 17 projects; the text says only that two categories overlap (reader's arithmetic). Compare 2609.07542 and the slug `demonstrated-understanding-gate-for-newcomer-agent-prs`.

### F6 · practice · accepted-issue-gate-for-ai-contributions · E2
- practice: accept contributions only for issues that were accepted or discussed first, so unsolicited AI-generated work can be closed without review (§V.A.1, §VI).
- claim: policies "should protect maintainer attention through scope limits, pre-discussion requirements, throttles, or closure rules for low-return submissions" (§VI).
- evidence: six projects, including ESLint and go-delve, use an accepted-issue gate, "with substantial overlap" with the 5 maintainer exemptions (§V.A.1).
- effect: none
- reader: a count of adopters among 209; no effect on submission volume or reviewer time is measured. The gate is one of the few mechanisms in the paper that a team running agents could copy as a deterministic rule.

### F7 · observation · ai-policy-scope-coverage · E2
- practice: observed: projects that permit disclosed AI-written code but prohibit AI-written issue and bug-report text (§V.A.2).
- claim: "projects that are permissive about AI-mediated code are often strictest about AI-mediated bug report text", because code "can have testability gates that separate correct from incorrect; a bug report has no equivalent mechanism" (§V.A.2).
- evidence: seven named projects: NumPy, SciPy, SymPy, Matplotlib, Jellyfin, searxng, Zulip (§V.A.2). No count of how many permissive projects do not do this.
- effect: none
- reader: seven cases named, no denominator. The testability argument is the authors' reasoning and the one place where the paper ties a policy to a verification mechanism; it is not tested.

### F8 · observation · ai-policy-by-contribution-type · E2
- practice: observed: whether the policy treats bug fixes and new features differently (§V.A.2).
- claim: "Virtually no policy distinguishes between a bug fix ... and a new feature" (§V.A.2).
- evidence: DuckDB is "the sole project in the entire 209 set" that draws the line by name: bug fixes have "a verifiable ground truth while features require design judgement", and the final decision is left to the maintainer ("may be accepted") (§V.A.2).
- effect: none
- reader: absence of a rule, in 209 projects, with no outcome measured. §VI nevertheless lists "distinguish contribution types" as one of three recommended adaptations, so the recommendation goes beyond what the policies show.

### F9 · practice · ai-disclosure-requirement · E0
- practice: require contributors to disclose that AI was used, as the only signal of AI involvement (§VI).
- claim: "Disclosing that AI was used, and how, is useful for transparency, but insufficient as a signal of contributor ownership"; signals must have "higher evidentiary value" because AI makes them cheap to produce (§VI, Table III "Effort signals weaken").
- evidence: argument only; no data on disclosure in the parts read. §III records disclosure as a coded field (yes, no, recommended, unclear) but §V reports no disclosure counts.
- effect: mixed
- reader: asserted by the authors as part of the framework. `mixed` records "useful but insufficient". No test of whether disclosure changes review or acceptance.

### F10 · practice · human-operator-accountability-for-agent-submissions · E0
- practice: each project states whether autonomous agent submissions are allowed, and requires a human operator to accept accountability or rejects fully autonomous submissions (Table III, Table IV "Autonomous agentic boundary").
- claim: "Accountability is unassignable" when "contributions can be initiated and submitted without a human directing the specific submission" (Table III).
- evidence: framework statement only. The policies in §V are coded for a "bot" target actor (Table I) but no count of autonomous-agent rules is given.
- effect: helps
- reader: asserted as a framework principle with no supporting data in the parts read. `helps` is the direction the authors claim.

### F11 · practice · llm-coded-policy-with-manual-verification · E0
- practice: code each policy page with Claude Code (Opus 4.7) from a template built by hand on 12 projects, then verify every case by hand and against the original dataset authors' labels (§III).
- claim: "the LLM's output closely aligned with our manual assessments", with discrepancies "particularly regarding project categorization", so the output was treated "as a preliminary baseline" (§III).
- evidence: 12 projects compared; no agreement rate, no count of discrepancies, no per-field result in the text. The full list of 209 was then verified manually (§III).
- effect: mixed
- reader: no numbers, so E0 for the quality of the LLM coding. `mixed`: close on most fields, wrong often enough on stance to need a full manual pass. The 12 projects were also the ones the template was built from (§III), so the comparison is not on held-out cases.

### F12 · practice · governance-framework-for-ai-contribution-policy · E0
- practice: the AI Contribution Governance Framework: 12 concerns in 7 dimensions (Table III) and 12 acceptability principles in three layers, project, contributor and maintainer (Table IV) (counts are the reader's arithmetic from the tables), as an aid for writing a project's AI policy (§VI, §VII.B).
- claim: it "helps OSS communities develop AI contribution policies and provides researchers with a vocabulary" (abstract); it models review as "a capacity-constrained signaling problem" (§VI).
- evidence: none beyond derivation from the 209 policies and the authors' discussion; no adopter, no project that used it, no expert check (§III, §VIII). The authors call it "empirically grounded" and "formative rather than prescriptive" (§III, §VIII).
- effect: helps
- reader: the framework is the authors' own synthesis and is not evaluated, so E0. Several dimensions "go beyond the explicit wording of the policies" (§VIII). Principles that bear on agent work: accountability of a human who can explain the change, no burden shifting to maintainers, evidence for acceptance (tests, rationale), and review decisions that stay with a human even when AI summarises or flags (Table IV).

## Limits stated by the authors

- The sources "do not support statistical generalization to all OSS projects"; results are not prevalence claims (§VIII).
- Only projects that already adapted their policies are studied; nothing is known about projects that did not, or whether they struggle with AI (§VII.A, §VIII).
- Some policies were only in informal channels (blog posts, public statements, as for curl and Ladybird), and many policy texts were brief or vague (§VIII).
- The step from policy evidence to framework is a construct-validity threat: the framework may be too close to the data or go beyond it (§VIII).
- The policies are not evaluated for correctness or effect: "rather than evaluating the correctness of individual policies" (§III).

## Reader's notes

- No coding agent is scored and no outcome of any policy is measured. The study describes what projects wrote; it does not show that any rule reduces review load, defects or slop. All `effect` values are the direction the authors claim or `none` for a count.
- The coding of 209 policies is by an LLM with a manual pass; no agreement statistic is given. The framework (F12) is the authors' own and is not evaluated; the policies coded are other projects' documents, so `independent: true`.
- Policy text is a stated rule, not behaviour: whether projects enforce the rules is not studied.
- The two lists have different selection logic (most-starred with a commit in 2026, against a curated list built around the debate), so F1's stance shares depend on that choice.
- Relevance to agent work: the rules found are about what a human contributor must show (understanding, ownership, authored communication, a prior accepted issue). They are a record of what maintainers want from an agent's operator; the paper has no data on which of them can be checked by a tool.
- Data overlap: the HR dataset is the one of 2605.16706 (Hora and Robbes), and 2609.07542 (Hora, Robbes, Zacchiroli, same date) studies 281 policies; counts in the three papers are not additive.

## Discrepancies

- §V.1 gives, for the 42 prohibiting projects, quality and correctness 33 (79%), copyright and IP 24 (57%) and ethics 8 (19%); §VII says they prohibit "mainly for three distinct reasons: 18 prioritize code quality ..., 16 cite legal uncertainty ..., and 8 reject AI usage as a matter of ethical principles". The 18 and 16 are not explained as a primary-reason count in the parts read.
- §V.1 prints 14 projects as 34% of 42; 14 of 42 is 33.3% (reader's arithmetic).
- §I says the 1,000 repositories were the "most active"; §IV says "most-starred".
- §V.A.1 counts 17 projects that distinguish new from regular contributors; the five mechanism counts sum to 24 (reader's arithmetic), and the text names overlap only between maintainer exemptions and accepted-issue gates.
