## Question

When a user states a requirement only after the agent has begun editing, is the agent's earlier code
deleted or replaced more than at matched non-requirement points, does that depend on timing or on
operation type, and does revealing a requirement late or warning of it early change the rework (§1)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · late-requirement-arrival-prior-code-deletion · E3
- practice: observed, not assigned: in real sessions, the lines of prior agent-written code deleted or replaced in the edit that follows a post-onset requirement (not an observed-failure correction), and in the next 5 edits, against up to 3 matched edits from the same session with no requirement (§4.2.3, §5.3).
- claim: "agents delete roughly twice as many lines of prior agent-written code" after a new requirement arrives than at edits with no new requirement; the authors call it an association, not a cause (§1, §6.1).
- evidence: 452 matched real events average 57.5 invalidated lines against 29.4 for controls, ratio 1.96 (95% repository-bootstrap CI 1.31 to 2.82), difference +28.2 [+10.1, +44.4] (§5.3). Complete Sol set 66.5 against 32.3 (ratio 2.06) and complete Sonnet set 60.3 against 29.3 (2.06). Control restricted to edits after a user turn with no new requirement: 66.5 against 32.1, ratio 2.07 (Table 4). Post-hoc rematch on live lines and edit index: ratio 1.66 [0.97, 2.71]. 356 non-overlapping events: 1.82 [1.19, 2.62] and 1.67 [1.16, 2.30]. 104 events from sessions with one canonical event: 1.22 [0.60, 2.39]. Net-deletion measure, row level: 2.28 [1.19, 4.36] against 1.89 raw (§5.3). In a 221-event diagnostic, invalidated lines had survived a median of 6 edits and 74% at least 3 edits; an LLM judge labelled 55% of 147 sampled events requirement-driven, 31% incidental, 14% unclear (§5.1.2).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E3, a comparison between matched edit positions with no assignment; the authors name task phase and difficulty as unmeasured confounds (§6.5). The outcome is a replay count of deleted lines, not a judged defect or a cost; on six adversarial test histories the replay has precision 0.40 and recall 0.67 (§5.1.2), and the authors call the count an upper bound. The matched ratio falls to 1.66 with an interval that includes 1 under one post-hoc rematch. Sessions are a screened subset (about 58% reconstruct) with longer median length than excluded ones (398 against 251 turns, §5.1).

### F2 · observation · late-vs-early-requirement-rework · E3
- practice: observed, not assigned: invalidated lines after requirements that arrive in the second half of a session against the first half (§4.2.3, §5.3).
- claim: "absolute burden shows no detectable declining trend" over a session; the timing result "is sensitive to how incomplete follow-up is handled" (Abstract, Answer to RQ2b).
- evidence: pre-specified observable-window analysis: early 49.2 and late 56.0 lines, negative-binomial late-to-early ratio 1.19 [0.87, 1.64], difference +6.9 [−7.2, +21.6]. Only 612 of 921 events (66.4%) have all 5 follow-up edits; incomplete events sit later (normalized position 0.73 against 0.45). Post-hoc complete-window ratio 1.48 [1.10, 1.99], inverse-probability-weighted 1.52 [1.15, 2.02] (§5.3). The proportional measure falls late: odds ratio 0.45 [0.34, 0.58] (Sol); recent-work ratio 0.182 to 0.109 (§5.3). Pipeline tests p = 0.208 and 0.622, q = 0.277 and 0.622, none below 0.05 (Table 5).
- effect: mixed
- reader: the pre-specified test is inconclusive and the two post-hoc analyses point to more late rework, so no single direction is claimed; `mixed` records that split. Intervals admit moderate effects, and the authors give a minimum detectable ratio of about 2.0 (§6.5). The window-length check on 150 sessions is not monotonic (§5.3).

### F3 · observation · destructive-requirement-operation-rework · E3
- practice: observed, not assigned: invalidated lines after events that remove or replace a requirement (destructive) against events that add, constrain or relax one (§4.2.4, §5.4).
- claim: no resolved difference between destructive and non-destructive events; the larger raw gaps come from the number of requirement units per event (§5.4, §6.2).
- evidence: 134 destructive events average 55.7 lines against 52.4 for 787 non-destructive, difference +3.3 [−19.3, +26.7]; DIR difference +0.018 [−0.033, +0.080] (§5.4). Raw complete-set gaps 81.6 against 51.1 (Sol) and 66.5 against 47.2 (Sonnet), with 11.2 against 3.2 units per event (Sol). Single-unit events: +25.4 [−18.1, +56.7] (Sol) and −1.9 [−27.3, +26.2] (Sonnet). Table 5: p = 0.056 and 0.160, q = 0.224 and 0.277.
- effect: none
- reader: a null with wide intervals that the authors say "permit material associations"; it is not evidence of equality. Destructive labels are mechanical (an event is destructive if any unit is), and fine operation labels have human AC1 0.58 (§5.1.1).

### F4 · practice · delayed-requirement-reveal · E4
- practice: give Aider the same late requirement either together with the base task in round 1 (upfront arm, round 2 is a neutral "review and finalize" prompt) or only in round 2 (delayed arm); same final tests, same number of calls and token budget (§4.3.1, Experiment E1). 25 constructed tasks, 2 back-ends, 2 replicates.
- claim: delayed reveal relocates implementation into the post-reveal round "rather than adding further rework" (Abstract, Answer to RQ4).
- evidence: round-2 churn 0.1 lines upfront and 9.3 delayed, difference +9.2 [+6.4, +12.8] (manipulation check). Cumulative two-round churn is lower under delay in the instrumented Sol runs, −4.4 [−7.9, −1.6]. Final-test correctness 95.0% upfront and 92.9% delayed; of 98 complete pairs, 3 pass only upfront and 1 only delayed (exact McNemar p = 0.625). 198 of 200 runs usable (§5.6).
- effect: none
- reader: arm contrast on equal tasks, so E4; the paper does not say how runs were randomised, and it reports an "assigned-arm" analysis. The authors state that E1 "cannot cleanly isolate additional overwriting" because round 2 differs between arms (§4.3.1), so the finding rests on churn totals and correctness, and the cumulative-churn result covers one back-end. One provider, constructed tasks, 25 tasks.

### F5 · practice · advance-warning-without-requirement-content · E4
- practice: tell the agent in round 1 that an addition will follow, without saying what it is (anticipated arm), against no warning (unanticipated arm); both implement the identical addition in round 2 (§4.3.1, Experiment E2).
- claim: advance warning alone produces "no detected effect on subsequent overwriting"; the authors do not claim equivalence (Abstract, §5.6).
- evidence: pooled difference in overwritten round-1 lines (unanticipated minus anticipated) +0.16 [−0.56, +1.06]; backend-specific estimates have opposite signs (§5.6, Figure 12b). Final-test correctness 92.7% anticipated and 93.8% unanticipated, task-blocked difference −0.02 [−0.062, +0.000]; of 95 complete pairs, 2 pass only anticipated and 3 only unanticipated (McNemar p = 1.000). 193 of 200 runs usable, 7 without a round-1 commit.
- effect: none
- reader: E4 by design on 25 tasks and two back-ends of one provider; the warning carried no requirement content, so the result says nothing about warnings that do (the authors say the same, Answer to RQ4). The authors say E2 was designed after E1's asymmetry was found and is "not an independent replication" (§4.3.1). Missing round-1 commits after assignment (4 anticipated and 3 unanticipated) can bias an available-case contrast.

### F6 · practice · two-llm-agreement-gate-with-human-adjudication · E2
- practice: label every requirement unit with two independent LLM pipelines (gpt-5.6-sol, claude-sonnet-5); accept an event automatically when both agree on entailment and on destructive versus non-destructive operation; send the rest to human adjudication (§4.2.2, Figure 3). Check against a separate gold sample of 437 units and a blind audit of 100 auto-accepted events.
- claim: the gate plus adjudication yields a canonical event set whose coarse labels are reliable; fine labels are not and stay descriptive (§5.1.1, §6.5).
- evidence: of 1,275 union candidates, 658 pass the gate and 617 go to humans, who reject 290 and keep 327, of which 263 join the set (§5.1). Held-out, pooled LLM requirement-detection precision 0.78 (Sol 0.81, Sonnet 0.73); destructive-operation macro-F1 0.86. Human recoding agrees with 93 of 100 auto-accepted events. Human AC1 0.95 for entailment, 0.79 for destructive operation, 0.96 for observed-failure, 0.73 for the emergence decision (§5.1.1). An "LLM-only versus human-backstopped sensitivity" is announced in §4.2.2; no number for it appears in the parts read.
- effect: helps
- reader: E2, numbers against a human reference with no run without the gate; the benefit of the second model or of adjudication is not isolated. The authors built and validated their own pipeline (independent: false for this finding). Human agreement for segmentation was measured on a sample enriched for long turns (§5.1.1).

### F7 · observation · agreement-by-label-and-unit-granularity · E3
- practice: observed, not assigned: inter-annotator agreement for requirement labels at different label granularity and at different unit sizes (§5.1.1, §6.3).
- claim: agreement rises as the unit coarsens from span to edit-event, and coarse labels are far more reliable than fine ones; the authors set the confirmatory unit by measured agreement (§2.4, §6.3).
- evidence: Jaccard 0.25 at span level against 0.78 at edit-event level (§6.3). Between the two LLM pipelines on 754 events, AC1 0.89 for entailment and destructive operation, 0.54 for fine operation, 0.65 for fine trigger. Human held-out AC1 0.79 for destructive operation against 0.58 for fine operation, and 0.96 for the observed-failure gate against 0.38 for fine trigger (§5.1.1). Krippendorff alpha for human unit-count agreement 0.65 against Gwet AC1 0.80 (§5.1.1).
- effect: mixed
- reader: E3, between conditions of the same labels, not an intervention. The authors warn that "higher agreement at coarser units does not by itself demonstrate more accurate labels" (§3.4). One corpus and one codebook. `mixed`: coarse labels reliable, fine labels not.

### F8 · practice · elicit-requirements-before-first-edit · E0
- practice: before the agent writes any code, prompt the user for constraints, acceptance criteria and known edge cases (a "specification checkpoint"); proposed, not tested (§6.4, §6.6).
- claim: it could reduce the post-onset deletion excess toward the matched-edit level, so asking users to state requirements earlier "is not, by itself, a sufficient intervention" and changes are needed on the agent side (§6.4).
- evidence: none for the intervention. The authors give the observed excess (about 34 lines for Sol, 31 for Sonnet, §6.4) as an input to a power calculation and say the conversion of post-onset into pre-onset arrivals "is currently unknown".
- effect: helps
- reader: a design implication drawn from F1, which is an association, and from F5, which warns without content. The statement that editing before requirements stabilise "is not a product of user habits" but of agents' inability to act on unseen constraints (§6.4) is not tested by either study.

## Limits stated by the authors

- The comparison in RQ2a is associational; task phase and difficulty are unmeasured and the deletion count does not show that a deletion serves the requirement (§6.1, §6.5).
- Single opt-in open-source corpus, restricted to the clean-start reconstructible subset (about 58% of eligible sessions); not claimed representative or valid for dirty working trees (§6.5).
- Study 2 uses 25 constructed tasks and two back-ends from one provider (§6.5).
- Replay lineage has false positives (moves, reindentation, whole-file rewrites); the count is an upper bound (§2.3, §6.5).
- Events near the end of a session are censored; censoring-aware modelling is future work (§6.5).
- Null results on timing and operation type have intervals that admit moderate or material effects (§6.5).
- The study was not externally registered (§4.2.4).

## Reader's notes

- The outcome is deleted lines, which counts rework volume but not cost, time or correctness of the final code. Study 1 has no measure of whether the final result was better or worse.
- The paper's own matched ratio ranges from 1.22 to 2.31 across specifications; "twice" is the central value, not a bound.
- Event multiplicity (units per event) differs between destructive and other events by a factor of 3 or more, and the event is the analysis unit, so any contrast between event types carries that composition effect.
- Study 2 does not measure how much code a late requirement overwrites: E1 cannot isolate it (§4.3.1) and E2 fixes round-2 work and varies only warning. No experiment here assigns late arrival and measures deletion, so F1 stays observational.
- The coding agent in Study 1 is not named in the parts read, so the finding cannot be tied to a harness or a model.
- The semantic audit (55% requirement-driven) uses a single unnamed LLM judge on 150 events from the first 200 sessions (§4.2.3).

## Discrepancies

- The net-deletion ratio is given as 2.28 against 1.89 for raw at row level (§5.3, §6.5), as 2.31 [1.20, 3.91] matched-set-weighted (Answer to RQ2a), and as "row-level ratio 2.31 vs. 1.96" (§6.3, fourth lesson).
- §6.1 says "55% of invalidations are judged requirement-driven"; §5.1.2 and §4.2.3 say the LLM labels each sampled event, 55% of 147 judged events.
- The abstract says burden "shows no detectable decline over a session"; the post-hoc complete-window and weighted analyses in §5.3 report ratios of 1.48 and 1.52 with intervals above 1, which the Answer to RQ2b states as greater late-session burden.
- §5.1 and Figure 7 call the clean-start sample "approximately 2,060" sessions and "approximately 58%"; the paper says the miss-rate vector was not retained, so no exact count is available (§5.1).
