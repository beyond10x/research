## Question

Can a coding agent be left alone to work through a chain of dependent requests, and if it cannot, is
the cause the code it writes or the mistakes nobody verifies (§1, §3)? What does the least outside
check recover (§5)?

## Findings

### F1 · observation · chained-steps-on-own-code · E3
- practice: 20 requests derived from consecutive commits of a repository, each given in a fresh conversation on the repository as the previous request left it, graded by hidden tests after every request; the agent is told neither the verdict nor that it is on a chain (§3.2). Compared: chain completion against completion of the individual requests of the same chains.
- claim: "models that complete 66-84% of the individual requests they are given complete only about half of the 20-request chains made of the exact same requests" (§4.1).
- evidence: Table 2, 280 chains, 8 attempts, pass@1 from 39.2 (Opus 4.8) to 66.7 (Opus 5.5), mean 51% (§4.1); request completion 66.2 to 84.0; strict pass@1 27.5 to 55.8; pass@8 average 71% (§4.1). 16% of chains are completed by none of the ten models on the first attempt and 16% by all ten (§4.1). The abstract also gives 95% of requests completed "taken one at a time"; the source of that number is not in the parts read.
- effect: hurts
- reader: a comparison of two measures on the same requests, with no run of the requests in isolation among the parts read, so E3. Chain completion is a product of per-request outcomes, so some fall is arithmetic, and the paper says the per-request failure rate is flat at 3 to 6% along the chain (§4.1). The requests are model-written specifications of developers' tests (§3.1).

### F2 · observation · failure-carry-forward · E3
- practice: a request is issued on a repository where an earlier request was submitted as complete and failed its hidden tests (silent continuation, §3.3); compared with the same model's next request after a certified pass, and with the same request attempted from the developers' own state.
- claim: "failure of the next request's own tests rises sharply after a single unverified mistake, with an average increase of 18 percent points (nearly tripling)"; "the odds of failing roughly doubling with every additional unverified mistake beneath a request" (§4.2).
- evidence: Table 21, 5 models, own-work failure of request k+1: after a certified pass 3.8, 4.3, 9.6, 16.9, 11.9%; after one untreated miss 21.8, 28.5, 25.0, 30.4, 29.3% (Fable 5, GPT-6 Astra, Kimi K3, GPT-5.6, GLM-5.2). Same requests from the developers' state: 13.1, 13.5, 5.6% against 21.8, 28.5, 25.0%, paired difference 8.7 [−2.2, 20.4], 15.0 [5.7, 24.8], 19.4 [12.4, 27.2] points (F.3). Table 22: failure with 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or more open defects beneath, Fable 5 8.0, 12.7, 36.1, 33.7, 66.1, 92.5%; odds ratio per defect 1.72 to 2.32. Figure 4: success of the next request with 0, 1, 2, 3+ failed requests beneath, Opus 5.5 96.5, 93.6, 91.9, 78.1%; Opus 5 95.8, 92.2, 86.0, 61.1%. Only 14 to 33% of failing tests are ever repaired (§4.1).
- effect: hurts
- reader: the gold-state control removes part of the request-difficulty confound, and the authors say about 9 of the 18 and 24 points for Fable 5 and GPT-6 Astra are request difficulty or selection (F.3). The developers' state differs from the broken state in more than verification status, and no randomisation assigns the miss, so E3. Table 22 is described by the authors as "descriptive, not a controlled dose" (F.4).

### F3 · practice · agent-written-base-code · E4
- practice: request k+1 is attempted on k requests that all passed their contracts, written either by the agent itself (retried until certified) or by the developers, under identical specification, budget and grader (AGENT CODE and HUMAN CODE settings, §3.3, B.1).
- claim: "when the code beneath a request is verified, models leave code nearly as buildable as a human's" (§4.2).
- evidence: across eleven models the agent-minus-human gap runs from −3.6 to +0.8 points, average −1; the interval excludes zero for three models (Opus 5, Opus 4.8, GLM-5.2) at 3 to 4 points (§4.2). Figure 4: next-request success on human against agent code, Opus 5.5 96.5 against 95.9, Opus 5 94.8 against 91.2. Making the earlier requests' tests visible in the repository lifts both arms 4 to 5 points and leaves the gap unchanged, −0.4 to +1.5 points (B.2).
- effect: none
- reader: an assigned contrast on equal boundaries, eight attempts per boundary, so E4. The "verified" agent prefix is the one where the agent retried until the hidden contract passed, which uses the oracle; the finding says nothing about unverified agent code, which is F2.

### F4 · observation · local-test-pass-without-lower-miss-rate · E3
- practice: none applied; the agent's last local test run and its closing message are read from the trajectories of requests that were later graded (F.6.4, I.2).
- claim: agents "trust their own tests, which barely lower the chance of a miss"; "the agents test what they wrote, not what was asked" (§4.2, F.6.4).
- evidence: 55,487 submitted requests of ten models: 97% invoke a test runner (90 to 100% per model); submissions preceded by a passing run fail the hidden contract 8.3% of the time against 8.6% for all submissions; 59% of the misses follow a clean local run, 96% follow some test run (F.6.4). A sample of 300 misses and 300 accepted submissions, five models: running the repository suite, odds ratio 1.44 [0.90, 2.29]; a passing last run 1.33 [0.91, 1.98] (Table 35). In at least 43 of 300 misses (14.3%) the agent's own output named a test the grader later failed (I.2).
- effect: none
- reader: groups compared without assignment, so E3. The 8.3 against 8.6 figure compares a subset with the whole it belongs to. The I.2 sample is stratified on outcome and comes from earlier single-conversation runs, and the paper says its rule-based shares are not comparable with the F.6.4 census (I.2).

### F5 · practice · fresh-context-reviewer-agent · E2
- practice: a second model reviews a finished implementation without knowing its author, may write and run tests under a budget of 60 calls and 45 minutes, and returns accept, reject or abstain. Each of ten models reviews 2,180 items: 109 wrong implementations per author, each paired with the same author's correct implementation of the same request (§4.3, C.1).
- claim: "none of the ten models separates wrong from correct implementations well enough to serve as the check" (§4.3).
- evidence: lenient reviewers (four Claude models, GLM-5.3, GLM-5.3-Flash, Kimi K3) accept 77 to 95% of wrong implementations; strict reviewers (GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1, GPT-5.6) reject 41 to 58% of correct ones; 4 to 19% of matched pairs are discriminated; Youden J at most 0.15 (§4.3, Table 12). Abstract: right 53% of the time when asked whether an implementation is correct.
- effect: none
- reader: numbers against a chance reference with no run of the chain with and without review, so E2. The wrong items are the models' own failures on a benchmark whose tests the paper's pipeline judged, and some failures are suspected to be test-side (F.6); prompt variants for the reviewer are not among the parts read.

### F6 · practice · external-failure-signal-with-repair-round · E4
- practice: after a request fails its hidden tests, send one fixed message ("External verification of your previous submission failed … Fix the repository … Do not modify or create test files") and allow one repair round before the next request. Under the benchmark's handoff protocol the repair runs in a fresh conversation given the failed request's specification and the message (K.1, K.6).
- claim: "a single external 'that's wrong' after a failed request and one repair round lift chain completion from 51% to 59%, and a repaired mistake no longer hurts the requests that follow" (abstract).
- evidence: Table 41, 10 models, 280 chains, one attempt per arm paired with the Autonomous first attempt: pass@1 changes by +7.1, +8.9, +2.9, +12.1, +22.1, +16.1, +3.9, +1.1, +7.1, +6.4 points (Opus 5.5, Opus 5, GPT-6 Astra, Fable 5, GLM-5.3, GLM-5.3-Flash, GPT-6.1, GPT-5.6, Kimi K3, Opus 4.8); 7 of 10 intervals exclude zero. Share of newly failing tests repaired by the next request rises from 8 to 30% to 28 to 60% (K.6, Figure 4). Repaired states then fail the next request at 3.4 to 12.4%, unrepaired at 22.7 to 31.1% (Table 21). In the continuous-conversation audit, a perfect supervisor at every failed request lifts completion by 7.9 to 16.1 points (Table 39). Restarting in a fresh conversation without the message changes the next-request failure by +4.1, +0.4 and −1.2 points (Table 21).
- effect: helps
- reader: E4: the same chains with and without the message, paired. The message comes from the paper's hidden grader, so it is a perfect verifier ("an oracle bit", K.1), which no deployed user has; the repaired and unrepaired subsets are selected on outcome (K.1). More than half of misses survive the repair round and 26 to 55% of chains still end short (§5.2). The 51 and 59 in the abstract mix an eight-attempt mean with one-attempt arms (see Discrepancies).

### F7 · practice · external-failure-signal-with-repair-round · E4
- practice: as F6; the measurement is regression rate, strict pass@1 and calls per chain.
- claim: the repair round, "blind to the work it mends", raises the regression rate and adds calls (§5.1, K.6).
- evidence: Table 41: regression rate rises for all ten models, by 3.2 to 8.9 points, with 4 of 10 intervals excluding zero; strict pass@1 moves by −2.5 to +2.5 points; the repair rounds add 47 to 294 calls per chain, 12 to 98 per round (K.6).
- effect: hurts
- reader: same design as F6, one attempt per arm. The authors attribute the rise to the fresh conversation having to rediscover the work (K.6); a continuous-conversation comparison of regression rate is not among the parts read.

### F8 · practice · named-failing-tests-in-failure-message · E4
- practice: append the identifiers of the failing test nodes to the failure message (Name arm), compared with the bare message (Reject), on the same starting misses (K.1, K.3, K.6).
- claim: "Naming the failing tests once achieves what insisting achieves in about three rounds: insisting repeats the agent's belief about what it built; naming the test replaces it with a fact" (§5.1).
- evidence: Table 38, continuous conversation, 939 starting points per arm (GPT-5.6) and 834, 827 and 821 (Kimi K3; bare, named and repeated message): repair rate after one round 46.4 against 30.0% (GPT-5.6) and 46.8 against 29.5% (Kimi K3), paired difference +16.4 [11.0, 22.0] and +17.1 [11.2, 22.8] points. Table 42, handoff: Opus 5.5 44.5 against 16.8%, +27.7 [18.5, 37.3]; GPT-6.1 Sol 37.1 against 12.6%, +24.5 [18.6, 30.9]; the named round breaks a protected test in 12.0% and 12.6% of rounds against 7.2% and 4.9% for the bare one.
- effect: helps
- reader: E4, paired on identical starting points. Only 2 models in the continuous arm and 2 in the handoff arm with finished cells (Table 42 has dashes for the others). The failing test names come from the hidden grader, so the practice needs tests that already exist.

### F9 · practice · repeated-bare-failure-message · E4
- practice: re-send the bare failure message after each failed repair, up to eight rounds in the continuous conversation (Insist arm) and four under handoff (K.3, K.6).
- claim: "more attempts are an inefficient substitute for telling the agent what is wrong"; returns diminish quickly (§5.1).
- evidence: Table 38, repair rate after round 1 to 8: GPT-5.6 30.0, 38.1, 44.3, 47.6, 49.8, 52.2, 53.0, 53.9%; Kimi K3 31.1, 42.3, 48.2, 52.6, 54.1, 56.0, 57.6, 58.7%. No round after the fourth adds more than 3 points; more than 40% of misses remain (§5.1). Median calls spent on a miss that is never repaired: 78 and 73. Under handoff four rounds repair 28.8% (Opus 5.5) and 14.4% (GPT-6.1 Sol), against 44.5 and 37.1% for one named round (Table 42).
- effect: helps
- reader: repair rate rises with rounds, so `helps` on that outcome with a low yield per call; no cost comparison against Name beyond calls. Intention-to-treat with censored runs counted as unrepaired (K.3). Two models in the continuous arm.

### F10 · practice · false-alarms-from-verifier · E4
- practice: the verifier rejects a correct submission with probability p of 0.1 to 0.5 (nested levels fixed by one random draw per chain and request) and sends the failure message with one repair round, as well as the true alarms (K.1, K.5). GPT-5.6-sol and GPT-6 Astra, 100 chains per level, the chains from 100 repositories.
- claim: "A wrong 'wrong' costs little on its own" and "completion never drops below the unsupervised level" (§5.4, Figure 7).
- evidence: Table 40, continuous conversation: completion without a supervisor 44% (GPT-5.6) and 49% (GPT-6 Astra), with a perfect supervisor 60 and 64%; at p = 0.1 to 0.5, GPT-5.6 58, 54, 46, 49, 47% and GPT-6 Astra 64, 58, 61, 53, 53%. Phantom rounds: 97.2% and 97.6% end in a changed state that still passes; 1.4% and 1.7% break the work; median 8 and 6 model calls; the phantom rounds are 10 to 34% of all model calls for GPT-5.6. Under handoff (Table 43), a phantom round breaks a passing test in 3.5 to 11.4% of cases at p = 0.1; Opus 5.5 at p = 0.5 gives back the whole gain (−9 [−18, −1] against the perfect supervisor, −2 [−11, 7] against Autonomous).
- effect: mixed
- reader: assignment of the false alarms is random, so E4. Only 2 models, 100 chains, intervals of about 20 points wide. A first run wrote the alarm schedule into a directory the agent could read; GPT-5.6 read at least one file in 13 to 19 of 100 attempts per level, and the paper reports the re-run with the files sealed (K.1, K.5). Many cells of Table 43 are empty because chains were still running.

### F11 · practice · alternative-harness-same-model · E4
- practice: run the same chains under Codex CLI 0.146.1, the OpenHands SDK 1.46.0 or pi 0.84.2, each invoked afresh per request, against the mini-swe-agent 2.4.6 run, with the hidden grader and the model settings unchanged (F.1, K.7).
- claim: "No harness completes more chains than mini-swe-agent"; "the harness does not, on its own, allow models to verify their own work" (F.1, §4.1).
- evidence: Table 44, 58 chains from 52 repositories, 4 models (Opus 5.5, GPT-5.6, Kimi K3, GLM-5.3-Flash), one attempt per arm: the paired pass@1 difference runs from 0.0 (GPT-5.6, OpenHands SDK) to −29.3 points (GLM-5.3-Flash, Codex CLI); 7 of 12 intervals cover zero and 5 lie below it; 67 to 90% of chains get the same verdict; the first failure falls at the same request in 139 of 264 chains that fail under both. GLM-5.3-Flash regression rate 36 to 62% against 26% (K.7).
- effect: hurts
- reader: E4 paired ablation of the harness, but 58 chains and one attempt per arm. Most of the 12 intervals cover zero, so "hurts" rests on 11 negative point estimates and 5 clear ones. The tool-time budget cannot be enforced inside the other harnesses and a per-call limit stands in; pi has no per-command timeout and 4 chains stalled for over two hours (K.7).
### F12 · observation · regression-of-own-earlier-work · E2
- practice: each failed request of the first attempts of ten models (4,787 sites) was labelled by a judge model (GPT-5.6-sol), and a second judge model re-executed the suspected benchmark-side sites (F.6).
- claim: "agents break behavior the request did not touch, the most common cause for all models but one"; they break "behavior it implemented itself, in a conversation it no longer has, against tests it never saw" (§4.2, F.6.1).
- evidence: first judge, all failures: regression 27.8%, missed requirement 14.1%, wrong logic 10.6%, integration error 6.9%, other agent-side 4.4%, suspected benchmark-side 33.3% (Table 24). Regression is the modal class of first failures for every model, 37 to 55%. In 1,111 of 1,251 regression sites with a failing protected test, the test belongs to an earlier request of the same chain (F.6.1). After the second judge, regressions are 31% of new failures, 48% of first failures (Figure 8 caption). The second judge confirmed 795 of 1,733 suspect sites as test-side and reassigned 743 to agent causes (F.6). Two judge models chose the same tag on 55% of 275 sites, κ 0.47 [0.40, 0.54] (F.6).
- effect: hurts
- reader: a distribution with no comparison, so E2; the schema has no value for a share with no direction, so `hurts` is the closest (see the final message). The labels are LLM opinion and "indicative, not exact" in the authors' words. About a third of failures were first suspected to be test-side and 795 sites (16.6% of 4,787, reader's arithmetic) stayed test-side after re-execution, so part of what counts as an agent failure is a hidden test stricter than the specification.

## Limits stated by the authors

- The benchmark measures fixed-test correctness, not user intent, readability or production readiness; it is Python-only and many repositories are small; twenty requests do not imply a twenty-link dependency chain (§6).
- Headline pass@1 judges only the final repository; strict pass@1 does not (§6).
- Specifications are model-written and model-audited, not yet human-reviewed (§6, D.5).
- The repositories come from public agent-task pools and nearly all changes predate the model cutoffs; three Claude models reproduce 14 to 19% of sampled change blocks near-verbatim, and the paper finds no measurable gain in chain completion on memorised repositories, with intervals of ±12 to 18 points that "bound but do not exclude an effect" (§6, E).
- The compute settings "do not equalize compute across models" (F.1).
- The failure labels are indicative, with two model judges agreeing on 55% of tags and no human relabelling (F.6).
- The mechanism studies of Appendix K run in one continuous conversation and are not interchangeable with the Table 2 protocol (K).
- Table 22 is descriptive, not a controlled dose (F.4). Verifier cost per check (about $0.001) is not a measured bill or a human-review cost (F.4).
- The hypothesis that agents in any setting with persistent work, a hidden contract and dependent requests will build on broken assumptions is untested beyond this benchmark (§5.3).

## Reader's notes

- The verifier in §5 is the benchmark's own hidden test suite, so the "one bit" is a perfect verdict with no cost to produce. A team that has such tests has the verifier; the paper does not measure what a weaker verifier (a human glance, a smoke test) recovers, only a random false-alarm rate on top of the perfect one (F10).
- The repositories are small and little used: median 424 lines of code at the first chain's base, median 46 stars, median 97 lines of tests, 34 repositories with one chain and 81 with two (Table 3). How the results carry to a large codebase is not shown.
- The tasks are reconstructions of the developers' own commits, specified from the developers' tests; the agent never sees the tests (§3.1). A request is a fresh conversation with no memory of the earlier ones (§3.2), which is the cause the authors give for regressions of the agent's own earlier work (F.6.1). A session that keeps its conversation or keeps notes in the repository is not tested, except that the continuous-conversation arms in Appendix K use one conversation per chain.
- Two different protocols give the headline numbers: eight attempts per chain with a fresh conversation per request (Tables 2, 25, 41), and one attempt per chain in one continuous conversation (Tables 21, 22, 37 to 40). Table 37's baseline completion (27.9 to 65.7%) is not Table 2's pass@1.
- Verifier cost and human cost of verification are not compared with the gain; the paper states the per-check compute only (F.4).
- The paper runs ten models and varies the harness, the verifier message, the false-alarm rate and the review role; it does not vary instruction files, skills or the specification text. A better-written request was not tested, though the authors rewrite specifications that models failed blind (§3.1).
- The authors build the benchmark and design the arms; the models are third-party and none is their own product. Hence `independent: true`, with the benchmark design as the confound the reader should keep in mind.
- Kimi K3's perfect-supervisor completion in Table 37 is a lower bound: 18 of 145 audited continuations reached the 12-hour wall and count as failures (K.1).
- Some cells of Tables 42 and 43 are empty because chains were still running when the tables were built (K.6).

## Discrepancies

- The abstract says agents complete "95% of requests taken one at a time"; §1 and §4.1 give 66 to 84% for the individual requests the chains consist of. Figure 4 gives 91 to 97% for a request on a certified state. The 95% is not derived in the parts read.
- §1 says one repair round fixes "a tenth to a third" of the misses; §5.1 says "a third to a half". Table 21 and Table 37 give 29.4 to 45.4% for five models, and Figure 4 gives 28.0 to 60.0% for ten models; none gives a tenth.
- The abstract's "from 51% to 59%" puts an eight-attempt mean (Table 2: 51.4, reader's arithmetic) next to a single-attempt arm. The first-attempt mean of the Autonomous column of Table 41 is 50.6 and the Verified mean 59.4 (reader's arithmetic).
- §4.2 says regression is the most common cause "for all models but one"; F.6.1 says it is the modal class of first failures for "every model"; the Figure 8 caption says "nine of ten models" for all new failures. The populations differ (first failures against all failures), and the paper does not say so in one place.
- F.6.1 gives regressions as 28% of all failures (Table 24, first judge); the Figure 8 caption gives 31% after the second judge. Both are stated; the text does not say which the main text uses.
- §5.4 says rejecting one correct submission in ten "costs at most 2 points of completion"; under the handoff protocol (Table 43) GPT-5.6 at p = 0.1 sits 8 points below its Autonomous run (−8 [−16, 0]). The §5.4 figure is the continuous-conversation result (Table 40).
