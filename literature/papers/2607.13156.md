## Question

How does the use of AI combined with a Design System affect the time required and the execution
characteristics of front-end development tasks (§1)?

## Findings

### F1 · practice · design-system-aware-ai-assistant · E4
- practice: developers implement two screens from a mockup with an internal AI assistant contextualised with the enterprise Design System; compared with developers who use the Design System components, tokens and patterns without the assistant, on the same task (§2.1, §2.2).
- claim: "AI-assisted development significantly reduced completion time compared to both the manual baseline and DS-only development (p < 0.05)" (§3.1).
- evidence: mean time to delivery in minutes, AI-Assisted against Design System, Table 1 and Table 3: Angular 164.00 against 216.75 (24.4% less), iOS 316.00 against 374.00 (15.5%), Android 163.25 against 213.25 (23.4%). Each arm has 21 participants over three stacks, per-stack n not stated (§2.1). The test, and the comparison it covers, is not named (§3.1).
- effect: helps
- reader: a control arm on one task, so E4 on the claim. Assignment to groups is described as balanced by stack and seniority; random assignment is not stated. Developers worked remotely and asynchronously, and the task was timed to the reviewing specialists' acceptance, which was done live by people who know the groups (§2.2; whether they were blind to group is not stated). Three of the four authors list Zup affiliations, and the assistant is called "internal" (§2.1), so `independent: false`.

### F2 · practice · design-system-aware-ai-assistant · E4
- practice: as F1; the control is the Manual group, who work "without assistance from structured tools" (§2.1).
- claim: AI assistance reduced time to delivery by 46.7% to 69.4% against manual work (Abstract, §3.1).
- evidence: mean minutes, AI-Assisted against Manual, Table 3: Angular 164.00 against 536.15 (69.4% less), iOS 316.00 against 593.00 (46.7%), Android 163.25 against 387.75 (57.9%). The Manual group has 7 participants and was run in the first cycle only (§2.1).
- effect: helps
- reader: the manual arm is small, 7 people over three stacks, and absent from the second cycle, so cycle and arm are mixed in this comparison if the AI group of both cycles is pooled; the paper does not say which AI participants are compared with the manual ones. Per-stack means rest on very few people.

### F3 · practice · design-system-aware-ai-assistant · E3
- practice: as F1; the measurement is task completeness, the share of required design elements and structural fidelity present, judged by the reviewing specialists (§2.3).
- claim: developers in the AI group "achieved a higher average completeness rate regarding the required design elements and structural fidelity" (§3.1).
- evidence: Table 2, average completeness: Manual 68%, Design System 85%, AI-Assisted 96%. No spread, test or per-group n is given for this table.
- effect: helps
- reader: E3 because the table gives group means and nothing else. The specialists were not multi-reviewer (§4.3), the scoring rubric is not given, and a section returned for correction kept the timer running, so a final completeness of 96% may follow from corrections already made during the timed task.

### F4 · practice · design-system-aware-ai-assistant · E3
- practice: as F1; the measurement is the standard deviation of completion time within a group (§3.1).
- claim: AI assistance "drastically decreased performance variability among developers" (§3.1).
- evidence: SD in minutes, Manual, Design System, AI-Assisted, Table 1: Angular 145.0, 85.0, 42.0; iOS 160.0, 112.0, 51.0; Android 110.0, 75.0, 35.0.
- effect: helps
- reader: the SD falls with the mean in every stack, as expected when times shrink; the paper gives no coefficient of variation or variance test (the ratio of SD to mean is not computed by the authors). With 7 people per arm per stack at most, an SD is a noisy estimate. The AI arm may also have been more homogeneous because all used one pre-configured tool.

### F5 · practice · design-system-components-without-ai · E3
- practice: developers apply the organisation's Design System components, layout tokens and patterns, against developers who build the screens manually (§2.1).
- claim: "the Design System alone improved efficiency relative to manual workflows" but its impact "was systematically smaller than that of AI" (§4.1).
- evidence: mean minutes, Design System against Manual, Table 1: Angular 216.75 against 536.15, iOS 374.00 against 593.00, Android 213.25 against 387.75. Reductions of 59.6%, 36.9% and 45.0% (reader's arithmetic). Completeness 85% against 68% (Table 2). No test is stated for this pair.
- effect: helps
- reader: the Manual arm is 7 people in the first cycle only (§2.1), and no test is given for this comparison; the p < 0.05 in §3.1 names the AI group against the other two. E3 for that reason.

### F6 · practice · design-system-aware-ai-assistant · E3
- practice: as F1; supervising specialists recorded the duration and frequency of breaks during the task, read as an indirect indicator of friction and cognitive load (§2.3, §3.3).
- claim: the AI group "exhibited consistently shorter and less frequent breaks", which "suggest reduced workflow friction and smoother task execution" (§3.3).
- evidence: longest break, Manual, 195 minutes in the "R3 dataset"; Design System group "often between 60 and 110 minutes"; AI-Assisted 0 to 90 minutes (§3.3). No counts, means or test.
- effect: helps
- reader: ranges only. Break time is part of the measured time to delivery (§2.3), so this is not independent of F1. That a break reflects a dead end or cognitive load is the authors' inference; a pause can be a meal or a meeting. The data were collected by the specialists by observation.

### F7 · observation · ai-assistance-effect-by-stack · E3
- practice: none applied; the time reduction is split by technology stack, Angular, iOS and Android (§3.2).
- claim: "all stacks benefit from AI assistance", but magnitude differs: complexity reduction in Angular, variability smoothing in iOS, smaller gains over an already low baseline in Android (§3.2, §4.4).
- evidence: AI against Manual, Table 3: Angular 69.4%, iOS 46.7%, Android 57.9%; AI against Design System: 24.4%, 15.5%, 23.4%. iOS has the widest SD, 160.0 minutes for Manual (Table 1).
- effect: helps
- reader: the explanations for each stack (UIKit or SwiftUI familiarity, predictable Android layouts) are the authors' conjecture; none is tested. Stack is confounded with the people in it, 7 per arm at most.

### F8 · practice · enterprise-conventions-in-assistant-context · E0
- practice: give the AI assistant the organisation's own design guidelines as context, against a generic assistant with none (§4.2).
- claim: "Contextualized AI produced better outcomes than generic assistance: AI tools aware of the enterprise Design System avoided non-compliant code suggestions" (§4.2).
- evidence: none in the paper. No arm uses a generic assistant, and no count of non-compliant suggestions is given (§2.1, §3).
- effect: helps
- reader: a lesson written in §4.2 that the experiment does not test; the arms are manual, Design System only, and the contextualised assistant. The limits section says results "may differ when using generic AI tools" (§4.3), which is consistent with this being untested.

## Limits stated by the authors

- All participants come from one large Brazilian enterprise, which may limit generalisation to other cultures, workflows and design governance (§4.3).
- The task is two predefined screens, which does not capture requirement refinement, API integration, review cycles or maintenance (§4.3).
- Completeness and fidelity were judged by specialists, not by automated or multi-reviewer assessment, so borderline cases are subjective (§4.3).
- The assistant is tailored to the organisation's Design System; benefits may differ for generic tools (§4.3).

## Reader's notes

- The assistant's model, its prompt, how the Design System was supplied to it, and whether it is an agent (edits files, runs the build) or a completion tool are not stated. Nothing here bears on an unattended agent.
- Assignment to groups is "balanced by their technical stack and seniority" (§2.1); random assignment, the statistical test, effect sizes and per-stack n are not given. The 7-person Manual arm was run in the first cycle only.
- Time to delivery ends when a specialist accepts the section, and returned sections keep the timer running (§2.2), so the metric mixes speed and first-pass quality. Whether the specialists knew a participant's group is not stated.
- The task timed against an 8-hour day (§2.2) yet two Manual means exceed 480 minutes (see Discrepancies).
- Three authors list affiliations with Zup, the company whose employees were the subjects and whose tool was tested; the paper does not state a conflict of interest or who built the tool.
- Industry track paper, 4 pages (SBES Industry 2026); methods and statistics are brief.

## Discrepancies

- §2.2 caps the task at "a maximum duration of an 8-hour workday" (480 minutes), yet Table 1 gives Manual means of 536.15 minutes (Angular) and 593.00 minutes (iOS). The paper does not say how a mean can exceed the cap.
- Table 3 gives AI against Design System in Angular as 24.4%; 164.00 against 216.75 is 24.3% (reader's arithmetic). The other five percentages in Table 3 match.
- §3.3 cites an "R3 dataset" and "both datasets"; the study has two cycles and three groups, and no R1, R2 or R3 is defined.
- §4.4 calls the results "robust across three technological stacks" while §3.2 says iOS has "the widest dispersion" and the paper gives no per-stack n or test.
- §4.2 states that the assistant "avoided non-compliant code suggestions", a result no section reports.
